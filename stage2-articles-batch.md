A Class I recall is the FDA's emergency tier. It means the agency has determined there is a reasonable probability that consuming the product will cause serious health harm or death. The CDC declared this outbreak over on October 2 because no new cases linked to the same contamination source had been reported within the standard monitoring window. Check the FDA's recall notice directly at FDA.gov for lot numbers and affected date ranges before deciding whether a product you already have is included.

**What "lack of sterility assurance" means**

Here is the distinction that most news reports have missed: the FDA's concern is not that bacteria were found inside consumer bottles. No end-product contamination was confirmed in distributed lots. When those controls cannot be verified as functioning correctly, the FDA requires a recall even without confirmed contamination. The logic is straightforward: if the production process cannot guarantee sterility, the product cannot be labeled as sterile, and eye drops must be sterile because they go directly onto corneal tissue.

This is why the classification is Class II rather than Class I. This Walgreens recall involves a different and less severe scenario: a plant that failed to demonstrate proper manufacturing controls, not a confirmed outbreak.

To fund this payment from tariffs alone, the US would need to increase tariff rates by roughly sixteen times their current level without any reduction in import volume. Standard trade economics make that impossible: higher tariffs reduce imports, which reduces the very revenue base the plan depends on. ## PRIORITY 10 — Story 12: E85 Bioethanol Fuel Savings

**HEADLINE:** E85 Bioethanol Fuel Is Nearly 50% Cheaper at the Pump — But the Real Savings Are Different

---

Drivers across the US and Europe are switching to E85 bioethanol fuel as pump prices stay high, attracted by prices as low as €0.85 per liter in France and significantly lower per-gallon costs in parts of the US compared to standard gasoline. Before booking a conversion kit installation, the math is more complicated than the price board suggests.

E85 is a fuel blend containing up to 85% ethanol derived from agricultural crops — primarily corn in the US and sugar beet or grain in Europe. It is currently sold at a substantial discount to standard unleaded gasoline. In France, where the fuel has seen a surge in popularity, E85 is priced roughly 50% lower per liter at the pump than E10 (standard 10% ethanol unleaded). In the US, E85 prices vary significantly by region and are most accessible in the Midwest corn belt.

Here is the calculation most promoters skip: ethanol contains roughly 76,000 BTU of energy per gallon compared to 114,000 BTU in standard E10 gasoline — about 33% less energy density. That means your engine burns more E85 per mile than it would gasoline, reducing fuel economy by approximately 20 to 25% depending on the vehicle and driving conditions.

Calculate your annual driving distance before investing in a conversion. The pump price gap between E85 and standard gasoline is real, but the fuel economy penalty closes it. Net savings per mile typically work out to 25 to 35% — not 50% — at current market prices. For a driver covering 15,000 kilometers (about 9,300 miles) annually, that can still add up to meaningful savings of around €700 to €900 ($750 to $950) per year. The break-even threshold on a certified flex-fuel conversion kit — which typically costs €700 to €1,200 installed — falls somewhere between 10,000 and 15,000 kilometers of annual driving.

**What drivers need to know before converting**

Not every car can run on E85. Flex-fuel vehicles — those factory-equipped to handle high-ethanol blends — can switch without modification. Standard gasoline vehicles require a certified sensor conversion kit that recalibrates the engine management system to adjust the fuel-to-air mixture for ethanol's different properties.

Installing an uncertified aftermarket kit risks voiding the manufacturer's engine warranty. Cold-start problems in winter temperatures are a known risk with improperly calibrated systems: ethanol's volatility characteristics differ from gasoline, and poorly mapped fuel injection can cause hard starts in temperatures below freezing.

The US Alternative Fuels Data Center maintains a locator for E85-compatible fueling stations. In the US, availability is strongly concentrated in Midwest states and is sparse on the East and West Coasts. In Europe, French availability has expanded rapidly under a bioethanol promotion program; availability varies widely across other EU member states.

On emissions: lifecycle analyses by France's Collective du Bioéthanol and by the US Department of Energy find that beet- and grain-derived E85 reduces net greenhouse gas emissions by up to 50% compared to petroleum gasoline when accounting for carbon absorbed during crop growth. That benefit is reduced, though not eliminated, when accounting for agricultural land use and fertilizer production.

If you drive over 12,000 miles (15,000 km) annually, own a flex-fuel or convertible vehicle, and have a compatible fueling station within a reasonable distance of your normal routes, the economics of E85 are genuinely favorable at current prices. Under those conditions, the conversion kit pays for itself within roughly 10 to 14 months.
