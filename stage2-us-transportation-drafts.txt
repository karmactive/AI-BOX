HEADLINE: Virginia I-81 Project Gets Two Federal Loans Worth Up to $89.7M for Truck Climbing Lanes

---

Drivers on I-81 through Virginia's Appalachian grades know the problem firsthand: trucks labor uphill through steep mountain terrain, forcing everything behind them to slow. The federal government has approved two federal loans totaling up to $89.7 million in low-interest financing to speed up Virginia's $4 billion I-81 Corridor Improvement Program.

The U.S. Department of Transportation confirmed the two loans through the Build America Bureau's TIFIA program, with Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announcing the package. The money supports Virginia's $4 billion I-81 Corridor Improvement Program across 325 rural miles of the highway. Key work includes dedicated truck climbing lanes in Washington County and Augusta County, exit ramp extensions and merge lanes in Wythe County and around Wytheville, and replacement of two bridges — at Peppers Ferry Road and the northbound span over I-77 near Wytheville. Build America Bureau Executive Director Morteza Farajian said securing the loans at half the standard interest rate lets Virginia deliver these improvements faster and more affordably. The Virginia I-81 improvement project funding gives the state access to capital at roughly half the standard TIFIA interest rate.

If you drive I-81 through Virginia, the daily bottlenecks caused by slow-moving freight on steep grades are intended to be addressed by dedicated truck climbing lanes. Because this funding is part of a corridor overhaul running to 2035, rolling construction is ongoing across the 325-mile corridor. For East Coast freight operators, the climbing lanes are expected to reduce delivery delays on one of the most congested inland freight corridors on the Eastern Seaboard.

The Wytheville area sits at the junction of I-81 and I-77, where freight volume from the South meets the steep northbound grades. The ramp extensions and merge lanes in Wythe County and around Wytheville are intended to address safety and merging bottlenecks in that stretch. The two bridge replacements — Peppers Ferry Road and the northbound I-77 crossing — replace aging structures, and both are direct targets of the funded scope. Virginia has been [advancing infrastructure upgrades along major freight corridors](https://karmactive.com/virginia-infrastructure-funding) as freight demand along the East Coast has grown. Earlier phases of the program included [pavement rehabilitation and interchange improvements](https://karmactive.com/i-81-corridor-phase-updates) that are already underway.

The $89.7 million is a financing mechanism, not a new grant. The loans support Virginia's existing $4 billion I-81 Corridor Improvement Program. At roughly half the standard TIFIA interest rate, the lower borrowing cost means more of the capital raised goes toward construction.

**When will the Virginia I-81 improvement project be completed?**
Construction on many projects within the program is already underway. The full $4 billion corridor improvement program is scheduled for completion in late 2035, meaning drivers should expect phased construction across different sections of the 325-mile corridor over the next decade.

The I-81 Corridor Improvement Program is projected to generate $5.2 billion in economic activity, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. Check back as construction phases advance.

HEADLINE: Cascadia High-Speed Rail Plan Targets 2028 for Potential Route Options; No Preferred Route Yet

---

The vision is a train connecting Portland, Seattle, and Vancouver, B.C. at speeds of up to 250 miles per hour — potentially reducing current travel times between the region's major cities to under an hour. Washington state officials are actively advancing that plan. But there is a gap between the proposal and the calendar.

Washington State Department of Transportation is moving forward with plans for a high-speed rail corridor through the Cascadia region, linking the Portland area to Vancouver, B.C. through Seattle. The proposed service could carry trains at up to 250 mph — far faster than the current Amtrak Cascades service, which is track-limited to 79 mph on the shared freight corridor. State officials have outlined a phased planning process as part of a regional partnership involving Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia. A Service Development Plan is expected to be completed by the end of 2028, after which environmental review, design, funding, and construction would follow.

If you regularly travel the I-5 corridor or fly between Portland and Vancouver, don't change your travel habits just yet. While the proposed high-speed line would radically shrink regional travel times, potential route options won't even be identified until the end of 2028 — and that plan will not select a preferred route or station locations — making passenger service a long-term vision rather than an imminent reality.

## The 2028 Planning Milestone

Washington officials are targeting completion of the Service Development Plan by the end of 2028. That plan will identify potential route options, not select a preferred route or station locations. WSDOT program administrator Ron Pate has said that rail construction itself could be between 15 and 20 years away.

The corridor crosses an international border, requiring coordination between U.S. and Canadian transportation authorities as part of the Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia regional partnership. [Earlier Cascadia rail planning](https://karmactive.com/cascadia-rail-planning) has shown how multi-jurisdictional coordination shapes project timelines. The Amtrak Cascades service that currently links the corridor has been the subject of ongoing upgrades — [new Airo trainsets entered service in late 2026](https://karmactive.com/amtrak-cascades-updates), though current track conditions still limit speeds to 79 mph.

A 250-mph electrified rail line could offer a lower-emissions alternative to flying the corridor, depending on construction impacts, electricity supply, and ridership levels. Amtrak Cascades currently covers the Portland-to-Seattle leg in roughly three and a half hours at 79 mph. The environmental case is part of what drives state backing for the project, though any such gains depend on the line actually being built.

Officials won't have potential route options until the end of 2028, and that plan will not select a preferred route. WSDOT program administrator Ron Pate has said that rail construction itself could be between 15 and 20 years away; no operating date has been set.

**How fast will the Pacific Northwest high-speed rail go?**
WSDOT envisions high-speed rail service that could reach up to 250 mph. For comparison, the current Amtrak Cascades service on the same corridor is track-limited to 79 mph. WSDOT's vision includes trips of less than one hour between the region's major cities, though no final route, schedule, or number of stops has been set.

HEADLINE: Alstom Launches U.S. Innovation Station in Pittsburgh to Advance Rail Mobility Innovation

---

Alstom has launched its first U.S. Innovation Station in Pittsburgh, focused on improving accessibility, sustainability, resilience, and passenger experience across the North American rail market. The station is the company's fifth Innovation Station worldwide, joining existing sites in Paris, Stockholm, Singapore, and Montreal.

The Alstom U.S. Innovation Station is based in Pittsburgh alongside the company's global development center for signaling and train control. Karina Ricks leads the hub as its inaugural director; she previously served as Associate Administrator for Innovation at the Federal Transit Administration and as founding director of Pittsburgh's Department of Mobility and Infrastructure. The hub will bring together transit agencies, cities, universities, startups, and industry partners, applying what Alstom calls a "local for global" approach — developing solutions locally that can scale internationally.

For U.S. transit agencies, the Pittsburgh hub offers a local point of contact for collaborating on mobility challenges around accessibility, sustainability, and system resilience — the areas Alstom has set as the station's focus. Buy America compliance for any resulting products would depend on the specific procurement and manufacturing requirements involved, not on the Innovation Station's location.

Alstom's choice to place this hub in Pittsburgh, alongside its global development center for signaling and train control, follows the model applied at its other Innovation Stations — embedding a new collaboration hub within an existing center of technical capability. Karina Ricks brings direct institutional knowledge of Pittsburgh's transit landscape to the role.

Alstom did not detail specific projects, staffing levels, or partners at the launch.
