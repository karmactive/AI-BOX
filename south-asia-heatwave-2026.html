The Science Behind the Scorching

The 2026 heatwave gripped India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh since mid-April, pushing temperatures above 46°C across Indian cities and to 44°C in Karachi — the highest Pakistan's largest city has recorded since 2018. At least 37 people died of heat-related causes in India. At least 10 died in Karachi in a single day. Climate change tripled the probability of this event, and the same conditions would have been 1°C cooler without human-induced warming.

April heat is intensifying faster than May — meaning South Asians now face both intense dry heat early in the season and humid heat as the monsoon approaches. The window for the body to recover is narrowing from both ends of the calendar.

The Night Crisis: When Darkness Brings No Relief

One of the most dangerous aspects of 2026 is what happens after dark. India's nights are no longer cooling. Research found that 70% of districts experienced five or more additional very warm nights per summer over the last decade. Only 28% saw a similar increase in very hot days. Nights are heating faster than days — and that is where the body's recovery fails.

380 Million Workers With No Place to Hide

About 75% of India's workforce — roughly 380 million people — work in heat-exposed conditions. Construction, agriculture, delivery, street vending. These are people for whom "work from home" is not an option. Net earnings for Delhi's informal workers drop by as much as 40% during heatwaves. In 2024 alone, heat caused an estimated $194 billion in potential income losses.

India's record power demand of 256 GW strained the grid and triggered localised blackouts across a country already navigating serious climate infrastructure gaps. A white paper from the Harvard Salata Institute warns India's GDP could face a 5.4% drag by 2030 from heat stress alone.

When the Harvest Fails Before the Monsoon Arrives

The heatwave arrived before farmers were ready. Agricultural drought now covers more than one million square kilometres across South Asia. Punjab and Haryana wheat farmers were forced into emergency irrigation. Mango and banana plantations in southern India saw flower and fruit drops.

Power Out, Water Gone: The Cascade No City Can Afford

India's power demand peaked at a record 256 GW. In parts of Pakistan's Balochistan, power cuts lasted up to 16 hours a day. When power fails, water pumping stations go offline — leaving Karachi, Lahore, and Hyderabad residents without tap water during extreme heat. Heat becomes hardest to bear precisely when the systems meant to help fail together.

Who Bears the Brunt

Extreme heat does not hit everyone equally. Scroll through the five groups most exposed — and find out what practical steps can help.

Outdoor Workers

Agricultural labourers are 35 times more likely to die from heat stress than workers in other sectors. Most of India's 380 million heat-exposed workers have no health insurance, no labour protections, and no option to stop. Earnings drop 40% during heatwaves for informal workers in cities like Delhi.

Practical Steps

  • Drink 1 litre per hour during heavy outdoor work
  • Take 15–20 minute shade breaks every hour
  • Shift heavy work to before 9am or after 5pm
  • Wear light, loose, long-sleeved clothing and a hat

Elderly

Heat deaths among those over 65 have risen 70% over two decades. The elderly cannot regulate body temperature as efficiently and many live alone. Research warns that 200 million or more people in India will face deadly heat by 2030.

Practical Steps

  • Check on elderly relatives and neighbours daily
  • Ensure they drink water even when not thirsty
  • Keep them in the coolest room with windows shaded by day
  • A wet cloth on the neck and forehead gives significant relief

Farmers

Heat at grain-filling stage shrinks wheat and mustard yields. Punjab and Haryana farmers faced emergency irrigation demands. Every 1°C rise cuts wheat yields approximately 6%. With below-average monsoons forecast, no seasonal relief is guaranteed.

Practical Steps

  • Use mulching to retain soil moisture
  • Irrigate in early morning or evening
  • Work in fields only before 10am and after 4pm
  • Carry ORS and water at all times

Urban Poor

Urban poor communities live in crowded settlements with little ventilation. Indoor temperatures stay dangerous hours after sunset. No air conditioning, no alternatives. When power cuts kill pumping stations, they lose water too. Wealthier residents' air conditioning units expel heat outdoors — worsening conditions on the streets.

Practical Steps

  • Use a fan with a bowl of ice — it drops air temperature noticeably
  • Soak feet in cool water; keep windows open at night
  • Use public cooling centres when available
  • Avoid cooking during 11am–4pm if possible

Children

Children's bodies heat up faster and cool less efficiently. They often don't notice their own dehydration. Schools without ventilation become heat chambers during peak hours.

Practical Steps

  • Children should drink water every 20–30 minutes, especially during play
  • Keep school bags light during heatwave days
  • Limit outdoor activity between 11am and 4pm
  • Watch for: unusual drowsiness, dry mouth, no tears when crying

What Is Working

Some cities are showing what is possible. Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan combines early warning systems, heatstroke management units, increased green cover, and work schedule shifts for outdoor labourers. Mumbai's 3.5-acre urban forest in Marol has reduced local temperatures by up to 4°C for nearly 173,000 residents nearby. Cool roofs using reflective materials have been adopted across multiple states.

The Council on Energy, Environment and Water has called for night-time temperatures to formally enter heat risk planning, heat insurance to protect livelihoods, and a national repository of Heat Action Plans.

Documented Conditions, 2026

The World Weather Attribution team assessed the 2026 South Asia heatwave against both current and pre-industrial climate conditions. The India Meteorological Department issued warnings covering heatwave conditions across multiple states from April through June. The World Meteorological Organization published a monsoon outlook forecasting below-average rainfall for the region.

The Council on Energy, Environment and Water mapped how 57% of India's districts — encompassing three-quarters of its population — now fall into high to very high heat risk categories. A white paper supported by Harvard University's Salata Institute examined how heat reshapes livelihoods and labour across a workforce of 380 million in heat-exposed jobs.

These were the conditions documented in 2026 — in cities where heat action plans are being expanded, in fields where crops were under irrigation emergency, in districts where nights refused to cool, and in communities where power cuts, water shortages, and extreme heat arrived together.
