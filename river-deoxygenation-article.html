4 in 5 of the World's Rivers Are Running Out of Oxygen

Forty years of satellite data. 21,439 rivers. One slow, invisible emergency unfolding beneath the surface.

Published May 19, 2026 · Source: Science Advances · NIGLAS, Chinese Academy of Sciences

"One milligram per liter of oxygen separates a thriving river from a dying one — and across 21,439 rivers studied, that margin is shrinking decade by decade."

Photo: U.S. Geological Survey / Public Domain

Four out of five rivers monitored across the globe are losing oxygen. A study published May 15, 2026, in Science Advances tracked dissolved oxygen concentrations in 21,439 rivers over nearly four decades. Researchers from NIGLAS at the Chinese Academy of Sciences used Landsat satellite observations and artificial intelligence to map the slow but measurable decline.

Tropical rivers — including those across India and the Amazon basin — face the steepest losses. Dissolved oxygen is what keeps river ecosystems functioning. When it falls, fish die, water quality degrades, and freshwater food webs break apart. Freshwater vertebrate populations have already dropped 85% since 1970 — the steepest collapse of any ecosystem type on Earth.

Why Rivers Are Losing Oxygen

Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen. As global temperatures rise, rivers warm, and warming rivers shed oxygen. The NIGLAS study found that reduced oxygen solubility caused by rising temperatures accounts for over 60% of the observed deoxygenation. The rate of decline sits at –0.045 mg/L per decade — quiet enough to miss year-to-year, devastating enough to reshape ecosystems over a generation.

What fewer reports discuss is the compounding role of nutrient pollution. Agricultural runoff — rich in nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilisers — fuels algal blooms. When that algae dies and decomposes, bacteria consume the remaining oxygen. The result: rivers under both agricultural and thermal stress lose oxygen faster than either factor alone would predict.

The danger thresholds are well established. The EPA's minimum dissolved oxygen standard for aquatic life is 5 mg/L. Fish begin to show signs of stress below that level. Below 3 mg/L, sustained exposure kills fish. Below 1 mg/L, a river becomes hypoxic — functionally dead for most vertebrate life. Cold-water species like trout and salmon are the first displaced, even before rivers cross those thresholds.

Hypoxic rivers produce significantly more methane — a greenhouse gas more potent than CO₂ over short timeframes — creating a self-reinforcing cycle.

There is another dimension rarely discussed: hypoxic rivers generate more methane — a greenhouse gas more potent than CO₂ over short timeframes. Warming drives deoxygenation. Deoxygenation increases methane output. Methane accelerates warming. This feedback loop is not yet fully quantified, but its direction is not in dispute.

The consequences for food systems are also real. Freshwater fish in the US already carry contamination risks equivalent to a month of polluted water consumption — and declining oxygen only compounds those risks as stressed ecosystems concentrate toxins and disrupt nutrient cycles.

"Deoxygenation is a very slow process. If we have a long period, the negative impact will attack the river ecosystems. The low level of oxygen can cause a series of ecological crises such as biodiversity decline, water quality degradation and maybe some fish will die."

A Crisis With Deep Roots

The Gulf of Mexico's dead zone — the largest area of oxygen-depleted coastal water in the western Atlantic — has existed in measurable form since 1972. The Chesapeake Bay has suffered seasonal hypoxia since at least the 1950s. The 2026 NIGLAS study reveals that the conditions feeding those dead zones are now spreading upstream, into rivers themselves.

This is not an isolated phenomenon. Multiple compound threats — habitat loss, pollution, and now deoxygenation — are converging on freshwater species simultaneously. Each stressor makes the others worse. A river losing oxygen is also more likely to carry agricultural runoff, face warmer summers, and support fewer species capable of absorbing ecological shocks.

Where the Risk Is Highest

The study identifies a clear geographic pattern. Tropical rivers between 20°S and 20°N are most affected — this band encompasses the Amazon basin, Central Africa, Southeast Asia, and India. Rivers in the Eastern United States and across the Indian subcontinent face the highest risk of crossing into hypoxic territory. Colder, higher-altitude rivers in Scandinavia and parts of Canada show the lowest current risk, though warming is already beginning to affect them.

What the Numbers Look Like by 2100

If current trends hold, the world's rivers will lose an additional 4% of their oxygen by 2100 on average — up to 5% in the most vulnerable systems. These are not catastrophic numbers in isolation. But at the margins of life support, small changes carry large consequences.

The EPA's threshold for aquatic life — 5 mg/L — is not a comfortable buffer. Many rivers in agricultural regions are already running at 6–7 mg/L. A 4% reduction from already-stressed baselines can push those systems below viable thresholds for entire species communities. Trout and salmon — cold-water specialists with narrow oxygen tolerances — will be the first displaced. Hardier, pollution-tolerant species will follow over longer timescales.

The freshwater vertebrate decline already stands at 85% since 1970. Deoxygenation adds another mechanism to the forces already documented as drivers of that decline — on top of habitat destruction, invasive species, and pollution. It is not a new crisis but a new dimension of an existing one.

What Can Actually Be Done

Reducing fertiliser runoff from gardens and lawns, supporting river restoration projects, choosing native plants, and reducing personal carbon footprints all contribute. At a policy level, the science is explicit: combined water quality and climate strategies — not separate ones — are what the evidence calls for. Treating river health and climate change as independent problems misses the compound nature of the threat.

🌱

Reduce Garden Runoff

Switch to slow-release or organic fertilisers. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus from lawns and gardens flows directly into waterways, feeding the algal blooms that strip oxygen.

🌿

Plant River Buffers

Native vegetation along riverbanks filters runoff, stabilises banks, shades the water (keeping it cooler and oxygen-richer), and provides habitat. It is one of the most effective low-cost interventions.

🗳️

Support Integrated Policy

Advocate for water quality regulations that account for climate projections — not just current conditions. The 2026 study makes the case for unified climate-water policy frameworks at every governance level.

🔬

Monitor Local Waterways

Citizen science programs that track dissolved oxygen, temperature, and species presence build the local data that feeds into global analyses. Community monitoring is not symbolic — it is data collection.

🌡️

Reduce Your Carbon Footprint

Temperature drives more than 60% of the observed deoxygenation. Every fraction of a degree of avoided warming has a measurable effect on how much oxygen rivers can hold.

🐟

Choose Sustainable Seafood

Supporting sustainable fisheries and reducing demand for farmed fish that require intensive feed cycles reduces the agricultural runoff pressure on freshwater systems upstream.

In Summary

The study, conducted by researchers at the Nanjing Institute of Geography and Limnology and published in Science Advances on May 15, 2026, covered dissolved oxygen trends across 21,439 rivers globally from 1985 to 2023. The analysis identified sustained deoxygenation in 78.8% of those rivers, with warming-driven changes in oxygen solubility identified as the leading cause, accounting for over 60% of the observed change.

Tropical rivers between 20°S and 20°N were identified as the most affected zone. A projected additional 4% oxygen loss by 2100 was outlined under current trajectory conditions, with up to 5% in the most vulnerable systems. The study also noted that rivers in the Eastern United States and India were among those most at risk of reaching hypoxic thresholds — with Indian rivers facing a projected 2–12% reduction in oxygen saturation by 2071–2100.

The data covers 38 years and 21,439 waterways. The trend line across those rivers points in one direction. What happens from here depends on the choices made in the decades the analysis does not yet cover.
