HEADLINE: Factorial Energy Partners with Mitsui Kinzoku to Scale Solid-State Batteries
PRIMARY KEYWORD: Factorial Energy Mitsui Kinzoku
FORMAT: News analysis | TARGET: 500–700 words | SUBHEADINGS: One
=========================================================================

Every few months a battery company announces a partnership and calls it a milestone. Most of those announcements describe a lab collaboration. This one is different: it addresses the part that battery startups almost always get stuck on — manufacturing at scale.

Factorial Energy, the Massachusetts-based battery developer listed on Nasdaq (FAC), and Japan's Mitsui Kinzoku signed a joint development agreement on September 17, 2026, to industrialise Factorial's Solstice all-solid-state battery platform. The partnership connects Factorial's cell design and manufacturing validation expertise with Mitsui Kinzoku's A-SOLiD™ argyrodite sulfide solid electrolyte, which is already in production at a dedicated facility in Saitama, Japan, and moving toward mass output. Factorial operates as a fabless battery developer — it designs cells and validates manufacturing processes rather than building its own production plants.

Solstice targets an energy density of 450 Wh/kg. Current lithium-ion cells in passenger EVs typically reach around 250–300 Wh/kg. Mercedes-Benz completed real-world road testing in an EQS test vehicle that covered more than 1,200 kilometres — roughly 745 miles — on a single charge using Factorial cells. Stellantis integrated Factorial cells into a Dodge Charger Daytona demonstration vehicle.

For drivers weighing whether to buy an electric vehicle today or hold out for next-generation technology, solid-state commercialisation will happen in phases. Initial commercial integration in 2027 will focus strictly on high-performance and luxury vehicles. Motorists who need reliable daily commuting can buy existing vehicles now without fear of immediate obsolescence. Those seeking 700-mile real-world highway range and sub-20-minute winter charging should expect a significant price premium when these vehicles arrive.

**Why the Mitsui Kinzoku deal matters**

The deal divides the challenge of solid-state battery manufacturing into two explicit responsibilities. Mitsui Kinzoku handles the chemistry: its A-SOLiD™ electrolyte combines the high ionic conductivity needed for power output with the physical processability needed for industrial manufacturing lines. Factorial handles the engineering: cell design, process development, supply-chain qualification, line integration and manufacturing validation.

One detail competitors have largely missed: Mitsui Kinzoku controls an estimated 90 percent of the global market for ultra-thin copper foil used in semiconductor manufacturing. That level of market concentration is unusual for any single supplier. It means Mitsui Kinzoku has the precision manufacturing infrastructure, industrial chemistry expertise and supply-chain relationships that solid-state battery production requires — and that a startup could not replicate quickly by building its own facilities.

Solstice also maintains thermal stability up to 90°C (194°F). Standard lithium-ion batteries begin to degrade above 60°C. That gap matters for hot-climate markets [LINK: EV battery performance in hot climates: what Australian drivers need to know] and for vehicle safety in collision scenarios where heat is generated rapidly.

Solstice uses dry-cathode processing — no liquid solvents during cell assembly. Conventional lithium-ion manufacturing relies on NMP (N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone), a toxic solvent requiring energy-intensive recovery ovens. Eliminating it cuts factory energy demand and removes a chemical-handling risk from the production line.

Factorial's structure as a fabless developer is the irreplicable part of this arrangement. While competitors pursued standalone gigafactory projects with multi-billion-dollar capital requirements, Factorial converted its manufacturing challenge into a supply-chain contract: electrolyte from Mitsui Kinzoku's existing Saitama plant, cell assembly validation through established partners. That approach moves the commercialisation timeline forward without waiting for greenfield construction.

**FAQ**

**When will solid-state batteries be available in electric cars?**
Initial commercial integration of solid-state cells is targeted for 2027, based on manufacturer deployment schedules. First rollouts will focus on high-performance and luxury vehicles. Mass-market production requires manufacturing partnerships to bring per-cell costs down, with wider availability expected toward the end of this decade.

**What is the difference between solid-state and lithium-ion batteries?**
Solid-state batteries replace the liquid electrolyte in conventional lithium-ion cells with a solid material — in Factorial's case, a sulfide-based electrolyte. This removes the flammable liquid component, allows higher energy density, and improves thermal stability. The manufacturing challenge is producing solid electrolytes consistently at the volumes needed for vehicle production.

**What does Mitsui Kinzoku bring to Factorial?**
Mitsui Kinzoku supplies its A-SOLiD™ argyrodite sulfide solid electrolyte, produced at a dedicated facility in Saitama, Japan. The company holds an estimated 90 percent market share in ultra-thin copper foil for semiconductors — a supply-chain position that reflects its precision manufacturing capacity and materials expertise.

Factorial has also confirmed its first commercial passenger-vehicle order and a first aerospace order earlier in 2026. Field test telemetry from Stellantis's Dodge Charger Daytona demonstration fleet is expected in early 2027. HEADLINE: EPFO Wage Ceiling Raised to ₹25,000: Who Gets New Mandatory PF Coverage and What Changes
PRIMARY KEYWORD: EPFO wage ceiling 25000
FORMAT: News explainer | TARGET: 650–800 words | SUBHEADINGS: One
=========================================================================

Your payslip is about to change — and whether that is good or bad news depends on your salary and how you weigh short-term take-home against long-term savings.

The Union Cabinet approved raising the mandatory EPFO wage ceiling from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 per month, effective September 17, 2026. The change was announced by the Prime Minister's Office. It is the first revision to the wage ceiling since September 2014 — a 12-year gap. The government estimates that approximately 51 lakh additional employees will come under mandatory EPFO coverage as a result. Annual government expenditure on the scheme is expected to rise to roughly ₹11,339 crore from approximately ₹10,250 crore currently.

The three components of EPFO coverage are the Employees' Provident Fund (EPF), the Employees' Pension Scheme (EPS), and the Employees' Deposit Linked Insurance (EDLI). The ceiling change affects mandatory coverage thresholds across all three.

If you earn between ₹15,000 and ₹25,000 per month, your EPF deduction will increase from this month. That means a slightly smaller take-home salary — but a larger retirement corpus. Your employer's contribution also rises. For a worker at the new ₹25,000 ceiling, the employee EPF contribution at 12 percent is ₹3,000 per month — compared with ₹1,800 at the old ₹15,000 ceiling. The increase in monthly deduction is ₹1,200 for someone earning at the new ceiling. The employer contributes a matching amount. The net effect on monthly take-home is a reduction, but the accumulated corpus grows faster.

**How the increase affects your salary**

The wage ceiling sets the maximum salary amount on which mandatory EPF contributions are calculated, not the total salary. Employees earning more than ₹25,000 per month remain outside mandatory coverage on the excess — their contributions above the ceiling are voluntary. The change primarily affects workers in the ₹15,001–₹25,000 per month range who were previously not mandatorily covered.

For those workers, the change means:
— Employee contribution: 12 percent of basic wages up to ₹25,000
— Employer contribution: 12 percent, split between EPF (3.67 percent) and EPS (8.33 percent)
— EDLI coverage: provides life insurance linked to the PF account

The EPS component is particularly significant for workers at the lower end of the new coverage band. EPS is the pension element — employees do not contribute directly, but the employer's 8.33 percent goes into a pension pool. For workers newly covered at, say, ₹18,000 per month, this is the first time they will begin building EPS pension entitlement. [LINK: how EPF and EPS work: a salary worker's guide]

The key misread to avoid: this change does not mean every covered employee receives an extra ₹10,000 in salary. It means the wage on which mandatory contributions are calculated rises. Workers earning above ₹15,000 but below ₹25,000 who were previously outside mandatory coverage now enter the EPFO system.

The 12-year gap since the last ceiling revision is the most important context here. India's inflation since 2014 has substantially eroded the real value of the ₹15,000 threshold. At current price levels, ₹15,000 in September 2014 terms would require a significantly higher nominal amount to maintain the same purchasing-power coverage. The ₹25,000 ceiling is therefore better understood as a catch-up adjustment than as a policy expansion.

**FAQ**

**What is the new EPFO wage limit?**
The mandatory EPFO wage ceiling is now ₹25,000 per month, effective September 17, 2026. Employees earning up to ₹25,000 per month are mandatorily covered under EPFO. This is the first revision since September 2014.

**Who benefits from the ₹25,000 EPFO ceiling?**
Workers earning between ₹15,001 and ₹25,000 per month who were not previously under mandatory EPFO coverage. The government estimates approximately 51 lakh additional employees will come under coverage. These workers now receive EPF savings, EPS pension entitlement and EDLI life insurance cover.

**Does the change increase PF contributions?**
Yes. For workers newly covered or those whose full salary now falls within the ceiling, the 12 percent employee contribution applies to a higher base. A worker at the ₹25,000 ceiling contributes ₹3,000 per month versus ₹1,800 at the old ceiling. Take-home pay decreases slightly; the retirement corpus increases.

Employer compliance and EPFO registration for newly covered employees will be handled through the standard EPFO employer portal. [LINK: EPFO employer compliance guide for 2026] The government notification from September 17 is the operative document — implementation circulars from EPFO providing operational detail will follow.

"If you park your electric car in the Supreme Court here, by the time we dismiss the matter, you go back and your car is charged."

Justice P.S. Narasimha said those words during a hearing at the Supreme Court of India this week. They were not part of any order. They were not directed at a party. The case before him was not about electric vehicles at all.

The remark was made orally by Justice P.S. Narasimha during the hearing of Shri Swami Samarth Agencies Pharmaceutical and Consumer Distributors v. M.I.D.C. and Ors. (Diary No. 53282-2026). That case involves a pharmaceutical distribution dispute. Electric vehicle charging infrastructure had no formal bearing on the proceedings. The judge's observation — that installing EV charging stations at the Supreme Court premises would encourage lawyers and litigants to switch to EVs — arose as an incidental comment, not as a direction to any party or institution.

If you own an EV in India, a Supreme Court judge's remarks about installing charging stations at the court are a signal, not a policy. Justice Narasimha said it would encourage EV adoption. But the case involved a pharmaceutical dispute, not EV policy. Do not expect charging points at the Supreme Court next month. Do expect EV infrastructure to stay on the judicial radar.

Justice Narasimha also observed that "switching from fossil fuels to electric makes a world of difference in terms of pollution and sustainability." That statement reflects a view consistent with India's national EV adoption targets, but it carries no legal weight in itself. The distinction between a judge expressing a personal or institutional opinion during oral proceedings and the court issuing a binding direction is fundamental.

A judicial observation is a comment made by a judge during a hearing that is not part of the court's formal ruling or order. It can indicate the direction of judicial thinking and sometimes precede formal directions in subsequent hearings — but it is not enforceable on its own. Courts regularly make observations on matters tangentially connected to a case before them. Those observations sometimes prompt government departments to take action proactively; more often they remain part of the oral record without procedural consequence.

The significance here is narrower but real: the Supreme Court of India is one of the country's most visible institutions. When a sitting judge publicly expresses support for EV infrastructure at the court itself, it adds institutional weight to a policy conversation that the central government has pursued through the FAME scheme and state-level charging mandates. It is not a mandate. But it is visible. [LINK: India EV charging infrastructure: where the network stands in 2026]

He said: "If you park your electric car in the Supreme Court here, by the time we dismiss the matter, you go back and your car is charged." The remark came during a hearing on a pharmaceutical distribution dispute and was a judicial observation — not a court order.

Was this a binding Supreme Court order on EV charging? No. The statement was an oral observation made during a hearing on an unrelated case. It has no legal force. An order requiring the installation of EV charging stations at the Supreme Court has not been issued. [LINK: how to read Supreme Court oral observations: what they mean for policy]

HEADLINE: NASA Curiosity Hits 5,000 Martian Days as Rover Climbs 1 Kilometre Up Mount Sharp
PRIMARY KEYWORD: Curiosity rover 5000 days Mars
FORMAT: Science news | TARGET: 450–600 words | SUBHEADINGS: None
=========================================================================

The image NASA published to mark the occasion looks like a postcard from another planet — because it is. What it isn't is a photograph.

NASA's Curiosity rover reached its 5,000th Martian sol — a Martian day — on August 29, 2026, equivalent to more than 5,137 Earth days since it landed on Mars in August 2012. A Martian day is approximately 24 hours and 40 minutes. Curiosity celebrated the milestone while positioned on the Chocolatal sand ridge inside Gale Crater, midway through its long climb up the slopes of Mount Sharp (Aeolis Mons). Since landing, the rover has gained 1 kilometre of elevation on that climb.

If you saw NASA's Curiosity postcard from Mars, know that it is not a single photograph. NASA merged two black-and-white navigation-camera panoramas — one taken on the morning of August 30 (sol 5,000) and one on the evening of September 2 (sol 5,003) — and colourised them. The blue portions represent the morning image; the yellow portions represent the evening image. The split is an artistic decision made by humans on Earth. The real surface of Mars is red and dusty. The postcard is a celebration of 5,000 days of exploration, not a literal view through the camera.

NASA's Curiosity mission has been operating continuously on Mars for over 14 Earth years — longer than the Mars Science Laboratory mission designers planned at the outset. The rover was designed for a two-year primary mission. Its nuclear power source, a Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator, continues to produce usable electricity from the decay of plutonium-238. That power source is why Curiosity can still operate when other solar-powered rovers would have been dark for months.

Gale Crater, where Curiosity landed, was selected because it contains geological evidence of ancient water activity. Mount Sharp, at the centre of the crater, is a layered mountain whose sediment layers represent billions of years of Martian geological history. Curiosity has been drilling into and analysing those layers — detecting organic molecules, measuring methane fluctuations and building a picture of a Mars that once had surface water and a thicker atmosphere. [LINK: what NASA's Curiosity has found on Mars: a timeline of discoveries]

The 5,000th sol postcard is both a scientific and a communications milestone. NASA's navigation cameras are black-and-white instruments designed for driving safety and terrain mapping. They are not colour cameras. The artistic colouring choice — blue for morning, yellow for evening — reflects temperature and lighting differences between the two capture times, but the colour representation is interpretive. NASA identifies the postcard explicitly as "an artistic interpretation of the scene," not a processed raw image.

What is NASA's Curiosity rover 5,000th day postcard? NASA's Curiosity rover marked its 5,000th Martian day by releasing a composite panorama taken from the Chocolatal sand ridge in Gale Crater. The image merged two black-and-white navigation-camera panoramas from August 30 (sol 5,000) and September 2 (sol 5,003) and colourised them — blue for morning, yellow for evening. NASA describes it as an artistic interpretation, not a raw photograph.

How long has Curiosity been on Mars? Curiosity has been on Mars for more than 5,137 Earth days as of August 29, 2026, when it reached its 5,000th Martian sol. A Martian day (sol) is approximately 24 hours and 40 minutes. The rover landed in August 2012.

NASA's Curiosity mission continues. Future science updates from Gale Crater and Mount Sharp will be published to NASA Science's mission blog. [LINK: latest space exploration news: NASA missions in 2026]
