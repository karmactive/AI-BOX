# ARTICLE 1: GIG-WORKER MINIMUM STANDARDS

Australia's delivery workers have new workplace protections as of today, August 17. The Fair Work Commission's first-ever minimum standards order now applies to people who deliver food, drinks, and groceries through apps like Uber Eats and DoorDash.

The order sets a minimum pay rate of $31.30 per engaged hour. If a platform's workers earn less than this amount over an earnings period (up to 21 days), the platform must top up the difference. This isn't automatic—platforms calculate earnings across the full period and cover any shortfall before the next cycle starts.

The rules apply to workers who use vehicles like cars or bikes to deliver items. People using vehicles larger than one tonne are excluded. These workers remain independent contractors, not employees. The order doesn't convert them into full-time staff, but it does create enforceable baseline protections.

Beyond pay, the new standards cover personal accident insurance. Workers who get injured while working through these platforms now have coverage. The order also includes formal dispute resolution processes, so if something goes wrong, there's a clear way to address it. Platforms must provide workers with detailed information about how earnings are calculated, what expenses are covered, and how deactivation works.

Workers also gain new rights to organise. They can now appoint workplace delegates and establish feedback forums where the platform must listen to worker concerns. Significant changes to platform operations require consultation with workers first.

The Fair Work Ombudsman will enforce these standards. Platforms that fail to comply face fines. Workers who believe they've been treated unfairly can lodge complaints.

This order matters because it's the first of its kind in Australia. No previous minimum standards order covered gig workers. The Transport Workers' Union applied for these protections over a year ago, arguing that app-based delivery workers needed baseline rights without becoming employees. The Commission spent months hearing evidence and consulting before issuing the draft order in July. Workers and platforms both had chances to respond to the proposal.

The agreement shows a middle ground: workers get protection without employment status. They keep flexibility but gain minimum pay, insurance, and formal complaint processes. For people doing delivery work, it means knowing what they'll earn and having recourse if something goes wrong.

Some platforms may need to adjust operations or payment structures to comply. For workers already earning above $31.30 per hour, nothing changes immediately. For those earning less, platforms must now guarantee this minimum over each earnings period.

# ARTICLE 2: NAB HOME LOAN APPLICATIONS

Australia's biggest bank recorded a 15% drop in home loan applications this quarter. That number sounds alarming—but what exactly fell, and what does it tell us about the housing market?

The key point: applications fell by 15% compared to the previous quarter. This doesn't mean house prices crashed 15%, or that people stopped buying homes entirely. It means the number of loan applications NAB received dropped compared to three months earlier. Understanding this distinction matters because media headlines often blur these different things together.

Think of it this way: fewer people asking for loans doesn't equal fewer people buying homes. Some buyers might be using savings instead. Others might be switching to different banks. Existing homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages aren't applying for new loans—they're just paying what they agreed to years ago.

Owner-occupier applications fell 14%, while investor applications dropped 17%. That's the real story. Investors—people buying property to rent it out—pulled back significantly more than people buying their first or next family home. This split matters because it tells us who's cautious right now.

Why the pullback? Interest rates remain elevated, making borrowing more expensive. A loan that cost $2,000 monthly a year ago might cost $2,200 now. That extra $200 monthly matters across years. On top of that, the government announced tax changes that will eventually affect investors. Starting from May 2026, investors buying established properties can't deduct losses against non-property income like wages. From July 2027, negative gearing for residential properties applies only to new builds.

There's confusion about these dates, so separating fact from timeline matters. If an investor bought before May 12, 2026, they're not affected. Properties bought after that date face restrictions on deductions—but not immediately eliminating negative gearing entirely. Investors can still claim losses against property income and capital gains; they just can't use them against regular wages. It's a narrowing of what's allowed, not a complete ban.

NAB's cash earnings reached about $1.83 billion this quarter. Despite the fall in applications, the bank's overall loan book grew modestly. Total gross loans and acceptances reached $451.4 billion, up 1% from the previous quarter and 4% year-on-year. The bank's direct lending channel—where customers borrow straight from NAB rather than through brokers—grew to 50.9% of total lending, up from 47.7% earlier in the year. This shift in distribution channels is significant because it shows how the lending business is evolving.

Credit quality metrics show early warning signs but nothing severe. The bank's "watch loans"—accounts flagged for monitoring—rose from 1.11% of the portfolio in March to 1.17% in June. Loans overdue by more than 90 days stayed at 1.01%, while loans 30 to 90 days overdue edged up to 1.63%. These rises are small but moving in the wrong direction matters to watch.

The Reserve Bank's own data puts NAB's numbers in context. Housing credit growth remains strong across the banking system overall, not weak. Owner-occupier credit is growing at rates close to its long-term average. Investor credit has actually surged significantly. So one quarter of lower NAB applications doesn't mean the banking system is weakening broadly.

Housing supply is another part of the picture. NAB's own economic forecasts note that dwelling starts rose 34% since late 2023, with 235,000 homes under construction. Yet net additions to housing stock still lag population growth. Fewer investor applications might reflect weaker investor demand without fixing the underlying supply shortage that keeps prices elevated.

NAB's housing outlook became more cautious. The bank is watching how interest rates, tax changes, and consumer uncertainty interact. But applications falling doesn't immediately predict house prices falling or the market crashing.

What this really shows: investors are reassessing their strategy, interest rates are affecting borrowing decisions, and the tax changes are influencing choices right now—even before the rules fully take effect in 2027.

Eight days earlier, his wife of 69 years, Nola Thompson, passed away at 90. It was strategic, creating lasting structures that continue without their involvement.

The key detail: the foundation preserves the principal and distributes investment income as grants annually. That means the fund doesn't shrink. It keeps generating money for community welfare, supporting causes that Roy and Nola identified as priorities. A $5 million fund, even earning modest returns, produces substantial annual support indefinitely.

Their connection to mental health was deep. The Thompson Brain & Mind Healthcare organisation identifies them as major supporters. Roy earned a Companion of the Order of Australia in 2018, recognising his community service. But the honour reflects what his life demonstrated: sustained commitment to building institutions, not just writing cheques.

What made their approach distinctive was longevity. They didn't fund causes for a year or two. They established structures designed to outlast them. Universities, health foundations, mental health services, eating disorder treatment, emergency services—their giving spanned education, emergency care, mental health, eating-disorder treatment, community hardship support, and rescue operations. That breadth shows they identified genuine needs across their community, then committed resources to address them.

Roy's passing at 95 and Nola's at 90, just eight days apart after 69 years together, closes a chapter for the Sunshine Coast. But the institutions they built—the scholarships, the research facilities, the accommodation centres, the treatment programs—continue operating. Their legacy isn't measured in a single donation or a building dedication. It's measured in thousands of lives touched by services they made possible.

# ARTICLE 4: SPACEX IPO

SpaceX went public at $135 per share, raising approximately $75 billion in gross proceeds. But the real story isn't just the headline valuation. It's about how much of that wealth is locked away and what happens when insiders can finally sell.

The company offered 555,555,555 Class A shares on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX. That's a precise number chosen for branding and symmetry, but what matters is the scale: SpaceX's founders, early investors, and employees collectively held far more shares that they couldn't sell immediately.

This is where lock-up periods become important. Elon Musk, SpaceX's founder and CEO, faced a 366-day lock-up. He couldn't sell shares for a full year after the IPO. Other executives, directors, and early institutional investors faced staggered restrictions. These aren't punishment—they're standard practice meant to prevent massive insider selling that could crash the stock price immediately after the public offering.

On August 6, the first lock-up restrictions expired, affecting an estimated $120 billion in eligible shares. That's massive. If insiders decided to sell everything at once, they could theoretically flood the market. Even if they didn't, the mere possibility created uncertainty in trading.

The SEC prospectus warns explicitly that future large share sales by Musk or other shareholders—or even speculation that sales could happen—could push the stock price down. That's not prediction. That's the company telling investors in its official filings that insider selling risk exists.

So what's actually driving the valuation? SpaceX has three main business engines. Starlink, the satellite internet service, is expanding globally and generating recurring revenue from millions of subscribers. The launch business handles commercial and government missions, competing with rivals while maintaining high pricing power. The space infrastructure side involves data routing, communications, and satellite services. Together, these create a company worth far more than traditional aerospace firms.

Morgan Stanley, the underwriter, set a base price target of $300 per share and a bull-case scenario of $600. The analysis hinges on Starlink's subscriber growth, increasing orbital launch frequency, and space-based data infrastructure becoming commercially viable. These are long-term bets on technologies that work but require scale.

Here's the distinction that matters: the stock price doesn't equal the business fundamentals. SpaceX's IPO showed strong demand for the shares. But markets also move on sentiment, lock-up expirations, broader tech trends, and whether analysts think the $300 or $600 case is more likely. A stock decline doesn't automatically mean SpaceX's rockets stopped working or Starlink lost subscribers.

The xAI acquisition matters more than headlines suggest. SpaceX acquired xAI in February 2026, integrating an AI company into its operations. The SEC filing requires SpaceX's financial statements to include xAI's historical results retroactively, adjusted for a five-for-one stock split in May 2026. That split made shares cheaper but didn't change ownership stakes. When you own 100 shares pre-split, you own 500 shares post-split—each worth one-fifth as much.

Why does the xAI connection matter? SpaceX is positioning itself as infrastructure for both space and artificial intelligence. Starlink provides bandwidth. SpaceX infrastructure provides satellite compute. xAI needs both. This isn't just a rocket company anymore. It's becoming an AI infrastructure play.

The IPO proceeds are committed to specific uses: satellite constellation expansion, launch vehicle development, launch infrastructure, and general corporate purposes. That capital supports the long-term business model. But it also means SpaceX has money to execute, regardless of whether the stock trades at $100, $300, or $600.

For investors, the August 6 lock-up expiration created a test. Insiders had the chance to sell. The market had the chance to absorb that selling pressure. The question now is whether large insider sales actually happened or whether confidence in the business kept people holding.

What this IPO actually shows: SpaceX raised enormous capital at a high valuation, but investor expectations are stretched across several scenarios—conservative at $300, bullish at $600, and pessimistic below that. Lock-up expiration adds uncertainty. The business fundamentals remain strong, but the stock price reflects investor sentiment, not just operational reality.
