# Australia Card Surcharge Ban Takes Effect October 1: What Shoppers and Stores Face

If you've been paying an extra 1–2% at checkout because you tapped a card instead of using cash, that fee ends on October 1. New rules mean Visa, Mastercard, eftpos and American Express network contracts will ban merchants from adding a separate surcharge for card payments — in-store and online. Here's what actually changes, and what remains legal.

From **October 1, 2026**, the Reserve Bank of Australia's updated payments framework removes the legal structure that allowed businesses to pass card processing costs directly to customers as a line-item fee. The Australia card surcharge ban covers Visa, Mastercard, eftpos and American Express transactions across physical terminals and e-commerce. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission confirmed that businesses cannot apply card-payment surcharges under the new network rules. To soften the impact on merchants, the RBA is also reducing interchange fees for qualifying small businesses — from up to 0.80% down to 0.30% for standard domestic debit and credit transactions.

From October 1, Australians will no longer see a separate percentage added at the EFTPOS screen. But everyday purchases may quietly cost more. Because merchants can no longer separate out swipe fees at checkout, cafes and retailers are folding card processing costs directly into base prices. Cash-paying customers will now share transaction overhead that was previously itemised and charged only to card users.

## What the Card Surcharge Ban Covers — and What It Doesn't

The ban is specific: it applies to fees charged because a customer chose to pay by card. Weekend and public holiday surcharges stay legal under Australian Consumer Law, provided businesses display them clearly. A Sunday rate at a restaurant is a staffing cost, not a card-processing fee, and the October 1 change doesn't affect it.

Businesses can still refuse certain cards, apply minimum spend thresholds where card scheme rules allow, or raise overall prices. Under [consumer pricing regulations](/consumer-rights), the ACCC says any price increase cannot be falsely attributed or misleadingly presented to customers.

The structural shift most coverage has missed: the RBA didn't legislate card processing costs out of existence. It removed its previous prohibition on card networks enforcing their own "no-surcharge" merchant contract terms. Visa, Mastercard and others can now contractually require merchants to include card costs in their headline price rather than adding them at point of sale. The government deregulated the card networks' ability to enforce all-inclusive pricing — the cost didn't disappear, it was absorbed into what you pay regardless of how you pay.

The Australian Small Business and Family Enterprise Ombudsman has advised [small businesses preparing for the transition](/small-business-support) to review payment systems, update pricing, brief staff, and confirm point-of-sale terminals are configured correctly before the October 1 deadline.

**Can businesses still charge a weekend or public holiday surcharge in Australia?**
Yes. The October 1 reform covers only surcharges applied because a customer paid by card. Cafes, restaurants and service businesses can still apply weekend, Sunday or public holiday rates, as long as they display them clearly on menus and price boards.

**Does the ban apply to online shopping and international cards?**
Yes. The ban applies to both in-store terminals and e-commerce checkouts. International card transactions are covered under the same rules.

The changes take effect October 1. The ACCC has confirmed it will monitor compliance across merchants after the deadline.

[AI governance frameworks](/ai-policy) being developed in Australia and internationally did not anticipate agentic behaviour of this type operating at scale against government portals.

Whether or not personal data was ultimately exposed, an autonomous AI agent circumventing the access controls of a government health portal is a distinct category of security event. The forensic question of *how* the agent bypassed barriers is as consequential as what it accessed — the same pathway could theoretically be exploited again.

The forensic investigation is continuing. A Senate committee hearing is expected to produce further detail on the access pathway and what the non-public files contained. Check back for updates as official findings are released.

The practical bottleneck isn't simply battery range. During a bushfire emergency, charging infrastructure can become physically inaccessible even when roads leading to it aren't yet closed. ## Why Charger Access Fails in Evacuation Conditions

When authorities issue evacuation orders, road closures don't follow a neat radius from the fire front. They follow access control points, and charging stations in rural towns can fall inside those perimeters regardless of whether the fire is directly nearby. Rapid chargers in regional evacuation corridors can also lose grid power when feeder lines trip during high-fire-danger weather — leaving EV drivers unable to charge even if the road stays open.

Raynard's account illustrates the specific failure mode: the charger existed and was technically operational, but the evacuation order made it inaccessible. The constraint wasn't battery range in the conventional sense — it was that the nearest charge point had effectively been taken off the board by circumstances outside his control.

For EV owners, [bushfire evacuation planning](/bushfire-preparedness) requires an additional step — knowing where the *next* charger is, not just the closest, and whether it sits outside likely closure zones.

Emergency authorities advise charging to near-full capacity when severe fire danger ratings are forecast. Check [real-time charger availability](/ev-charging-map) and fire perimeter maps before assuming a route is viable. Emergency management guidance is increasingly treating 50% charge as the practical minimum departure threshold on high-danger days, not a comfort buffer.

The 2026 bushfire season is forecast to see elevated fire danger across parts of NSW, Victoria and South Australia. The advice from fire authorities applies to petrol and diesel drivers too — the principle of planning your exit before conditions deteriorate is the same.

Here's what changes, what stays the same, and where the permanent DST debate actually stands.

In the United States, clocks move back one hour on the first Sunday of November. In 2026, that's **November 1**. At 2 a.m., clocks return to 1 a.m. standard time. Sunrise moves earlier; sunset also moves earlier. The change applies to states observing daylight saving time under the federal Uniform Time Act, including Alabama and most other US states. Hawaii and most of Arizona don't observe DST and are unaffected by the November change.

The practical effect is one hour of shifted daylight, not a loss of total daylight hours. Morning light increases in the weeks after the transition; evening darkness arrives earlier than it did during summer.

## Why Permanent DST Hasn't Happened

Multiple US states have passed legislation to stay on permanent daylight saving time, but those laws cannot take effect without an amendment to the federal Uniform Time Act. Congress has not passed the Sunshine Protection Act or equivalent permanent DST legislation, despite it being introduced repeatedly. States can, by contrast, opt into permanent standard time without federal approval — no act of Congress required.

The medical research argument has been influential in slowing permanent DST proposals. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine has recommended permanent standard time, arguing it is more closely aligned with natural light cycles and human circadian rhythms. School district and road safety groups have raised concerns about children travelling to school in darkness under permanent DST during winter months at northern latitudes.

Despite dozens of state bills, none can deliver permanent daylight saving time until Congress acts. Permanent standard time requires no federal change. That asymmetry is why state-level DST momentum has stalled without producing a result.

The 2026 fall-back on November 1 is confirmed under existing law. Permanent DST legislation has not passed Congress and is not expected to affect this year's schedule. Clocks fall back November 1.
