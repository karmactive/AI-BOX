## British Airways Owes £29,195 After HR Botched Dismissal of Flight Attendant Over "Mile High Club" Comments

A Reading Employment Tribunal has ordered British Airways to pay £29,195.69 to a former cabin crew member — not because the judge ruled him innocent, but because BA's investigators questioned him before taking a complete statement from the complainant. The ruling is a direct lesson in what procedural shortcuts actually cost UK employers.

Charly Mateo, 37, was employed by British Airways from September 2018 until his dismissal in July 2024. The incident at the center of the case took place in March 2024 while Mateo was traveling in a "positioning" capacity — flying as a passenger, not working active crew — when he allegedly told a female colleague he wanted her to join the "mile high club" and touched her knee behind a rest curtain. BA dismissed him four months later. He brought an unfair dismissal claim before Employment Judge Anna Macey at Reading Employment Tribunal. The award came to £29,195.69. His request for reinstatement was denied.

For UK workers and managers, this judgment demonstrates that internal company investigations must strictly adhere to ACAS procedural sequencing. Even when an employer holds a genuine belief that gross misconduct occurred, skipping basic procedural steps — such as questioning an accused employee before locking down comprehensive complainant statements — renders a sacking legally unfair and exposes companies to substantial five-figure tribunal penalties.

Judge Macey described Mateo's behavior as "strange" and an "invasion of privacy." She accepted that BA held genuine grounds to believe the misconduct occurred and that the breakdown of trust was sufficiently serious to make reinstatement impracticable. Both findings went in British Airways' favor.

The airline still lost because of how it ran its investigation. BA's HR team interrogated Mateo before securing a detailed, finalized witness statement from the complainant. Under the ACAS Code of Practice — the standard employment tribunals apply when judging whether a dismissal was reasonably conducted — employers must build a proper evidential foundation from all parties before confronting the person under investigation. BA skipped that step, and the court held the investigation did not meet the required standard.

That sequencing failure cost £29,195.69, even as the judge simultaneously refused to order Mateo's return to the airline.

### Why You Can Win a Tribunal Even If You Behaved Wrongly

The Mateo case answers a question many people get wrong: can you receive compensation even if the tribunal accepts that you acted inappropriately?

Yes. UK employment tribunals evaluate two distinct questions separately. The first is whether the employer had reasonable grounds to believe misconduct occurred. The second is whether the employer followed a fair and thorough procedure before dismissing. A finding on the first does not decide the second.

Mateo's compensation was reduced to reflect his contribution to the situation — a standard tribunal adjustment when employee conduct played a part. He walked away with money, not a job.

For HR departments handling misconduct cases with time pressure or reputational sensitivity — common in aviation, where crew welfare and brand image are closely linked — the temptation to compress investigative steps is real. Employment tribunals do not accept urgency as a justification. The cost of following ACAS sequencing correctly is administrative time. The cost of skipping it, as British Airways now knows, can exceed £29,000 per case.

BA has not confirmed whether it intends to appeal the compensation calculation.

## High LDL Cholesterol Is Dangerous Even in Fit, Healthy People — What Cardiologists Say About the Myths Going Viral

Cardiologists are pushing back against a wave of viral online content claiming that LDL cholesterol is harmless, that statins cause widespread memory loss, and that people who feel healthy can safely ignore elevated lipid numbers. The claims are spreading on social media and leading some patients to discontinue prescribed medication. The medical evidence does not support any of them.

The core argument being circulated — that LDL cholesterol is not actually dangerous if a person is physically fit, slim, and eating well — contradicts decades of cardiovascular research. The American Heart Association and the European Society of Cardiology both conclude that elevated LDL-C is an independent risk factor for heart disease and stroke regardless of other lifestyle markers. The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' Collaboration meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials shows that every 1 mmol/L sustained reduction in LDL-C produces a 21–22% relative reduction in major cardiovascular events.

If you have considered stopping your statin prescription after watching viral videos claiming cholesterol is harmless, medical evidence contradicts those claims. Decades of clinical trials prove that LDL cholesterol directly penetrates arterial walls to form plaque, regardless of how healthy your lifestyle feels. Discontinuing prescribed lipid-lowering therapy drastically increases your lifetime risk of heart attacks and strokes, while documented statin side effects are medically manageable and far rarer than online forums suggest.

### Can You Be Fit, Thin and Active — and Still Have Dangerous Cholesterol?

Yes. A person can have normal blood pressure, blood sugar, weight, and exercise capacity while carrying elevated circulating LDL. The reason relates to how plaque accumulates. LDL particles penetrate the walls of blood vessels and oxidize over time, initiating an inflammatory process that progressively narrows arterial capacity. This happens independent of cardiovascular fitness. A person who runs marathons but carries genetically elevated LDL from early adulthood will have more arterial plaque by their fifties than a sedentary person with low LDL.

Mendelian randomization studies — which use genetic variants as natural experiments to isolate LDL's causal contribution — confirm the mechanism. People born with naturally lower LDL due to specific genetic variants have proportionally lower lifetime cardiovascular risk even without any other lifestyle advantage. This is not a question of association; it is a causal relationship established over more than 30 years of research.

### What About Statin Side Effects?

Muscle aches are the most commonly reported concern among statin users, and social media amplifies individual negative experiences far beyond their statistical prevalence. The SAMSON trial — a randomized double-blind N-of-1 study specifically designed to isolate the drug's physical effect from participants' expectations — found that more than 90% of muscle symptoms reported by statin users were driven by the nocebo effect: the anticipation of side effects caused by reading negative accounts before starting the medication.

Genuine statin-associated muscle symptoms do occur in clinical practice, and they are medically manageable. Changing the statin drug, adjusting the dose, or reviewing interactions with other medications resolves the problem for the majority of patients. Stopping medication without medical guidance is not the appropriate response.

Treatment decisions are not made on LDL numbers alone. The American Heart Association says overall cardiovascular risk — which includes age, blood pressure, smoking status, diabetes, and family history — guides clinical recommendations. A 55-year-old with a family history of early heart disease and LDL of 4.5 mmol/L faces a very different risk profile from a 28-year-old with no family history and the same number.

Can you be healthy with high LDL cholesterol if you exercise and eat cleanly? No. Regular exercise and a balanced diet reduce overall metabolic risk, but they cannot fully counteract the biological damage of elevated circulating LDL over time. Excess LDL penetrates blood vessel linings and oxidizes regardless of physical fitness, initiating plaque accumulation that restricts blood flow to the heart and brain over decades.

The cardiologists currently engaged in correcting this online misinformation wave are not arguing for universal statin use. They are arguing against patients making irreversible decisions about lifesaving preventive therapy based on influencer content rather than evidence-based cardiology guidelines.

The traditional "dead level" rule was written for flat leather saddles in an era where saddle geometry was uniform. Modern cut-out saddles with curved profiles require a different measurement approach. Placing a carpenter's level across the full length of these saddles produces a misleading reading because the curve means the nose and tail sit higher than the middle section where your sit bones actually contact the saddle. Tilt should be measured across the middle third of the saddle, not rail-to-rail.

### Why Triathletes and Time Trialists Set Their Saddles Lower at the Front

Triathletes and time trialists use extreme forward-lean positions that rotate the pelvis sharply toward the handlebars. In that position, a level saddle concentrates intense pressure on the perineum and genital nerves, causing numbness that builds over long events. A downward tilt of two to five degrees counteracts this by allowing the pelvis to rotate anteriorly without compressing the spine into a bend.

UCI regulations now permit up to nine degrees of saddle tilt, with a one-degree measurement tolerance, giving a theoretical ten-degree adjustment window. For most road cyclists who are not using a full aero position, two to four degrees of nose-down tilt is the range associated with relief from soft-tissue discomfort without generating forward slippage.

The critical point that most saddle tilt advice omits: if you tilt the nose down without simultaneously shortening the reach to your handlebars, you will slide forward on the saddle and brace your upper body continuously to stay in position. This trades groin numbness for neck pain and wrist strain. Any tilt beyond flat should be accompanied by a reach adjustment, ideally during a professional bike fit rather than a trial-and-error garage adjustment.
