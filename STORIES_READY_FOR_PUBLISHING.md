## COMPLETE ARTICLE WITH EMBEDDED LINKS

Dementia kills nearly 2 million people globally each year and is the leading cause of death in the UK. With cases rising as populations age worldwide, researchers are urgently exploring prevention strategies. New research now suggests that something simple—limiting when you eat each day—may help protect your brain from memory loss and cognitive decline.

Scientists have discovered that eating within a shorter time window, a practice called time-restricted eating, can reduce cognitive decline and slow <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/the-brains-unsung-heroes-how-microglia-cells-are-shaping-alzheimers-research/" target="_blank">brain aging</a>. The research comes from multiple clinical trials and studies conducted over the past three years, showing that limiting your food intake to specific hours helps restore natural rhythms in your body that break down with age.

Researchers at <a href="https://today.ucsd.edu/story/intermittent-fasting-improves-alzheimers-pathology" target="_blank">UC San Diego School of Medicine found that when eating is limited to certain hours, the brain becomes better at preventing damage linked to Alzheimer's disease</a>. In mouse studies, animals on a time-restricted eating schedule showed improved memory and less buildup of amyloid, a sticky protein that accumulates in brains affected by cognitive decline. The findings are significant because they point to circadian rhythm—your body's internal clock—as a critical factor in <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/new-study-proves-plant-based-diets-reduce-mortality-and-major-chronic-diseases/" target="_blank">brain health</a>.

"Circadian disruption may be one of the main drivers of Alzheimer's disease, rather than being merely a result of neurodegeneration," explained researcher Paula Desplats from UC San Diego. This suggests that fixing your body's broken clock may actually prevent brain damage from happening in the first place.

A recent study presented at the NUTRITION 2026 conference examined 47 women aged 50 to 79 with overweight or obesity. Half the women restricted their eating to a 9-hour window, typically between 10 a.m. and 6 p.m., while others maintained a standard 12-hour eating window. The women on the restricted schedule showed measurable cognitive improvements compared to controls. The research suggests an additional benefit: stopping food intake at least 4 hours before bed appears particularly important for brain health.

When you restrict eating to shorter hours, your body switches from using glucose as fuel to using ketone bodies. This metabolic switch matters because Alzheimer's brains have difficulty using glucose efficiently, but they can still use ketones effectively. This metabolic shift provides an alternative energy source the aging brain can actually utilize. Blood glucose levels drop while ketone bodies increase, creating a healthier fuel supply for brain cells.

Mark Mattson, a neuroscientist at Johns Hopkins Medicine with 25 years of research in intermittent fasting, states: "Intermittent fasting can reduce high blood pressure, improve cognition, and prevent and even reverse chronic diseases including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancers." His research has shown that in older adults with mild cognitive impairment, time-restricted eating improved cognitive function over a 3-year period compared to age-matched controls who didn't practice fasting.

The mechanism involves more than just alternative fuel. Time-restricted eating activates the <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/is-your-childs-attention-span-suffering-new-study-links-traffic-pollution-to-cognitive-decline/" target="_blank">gut-brain axis—a communication network between your gut microbiome and brain</a>. During fasting periods, your gut bacteria produce metabolites that support brain health. Additionally, fasting triggers your brain's internal cleanup process, called autophagy, which removes harmful proteins like amyloid and damaged cellular structures. This cellular housekeeping is crucial for maintaining sharp thinking abilities.

However, adaptation takes time. Research shows your body requires 4-6 weeks to adjust to time-restricted eating. During the first few weeks, hunger during fasting windows is common, but this typically subsides as your metabolism and hormones shift. Experts emphasize that timing isn't the only factor—during your eating window, consuming adequate protein and fiber-rich whole foods matters significantly. This prevents muscle loss during fasting and maintains gut health that supports the brain.

Genetic factors also influence results. The APOE gene, which affects Alzheimer's risk, appears to determine how individual brains respond to time-restricted eating. Some people may experience greater cognitive benefits than others depending on their genetic profile, explaining why personalized approaches matter.

<a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06429124" target="_blank">Current clinical trials through the NIH are testing whether time-restricted eating can help people already diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer's disease</a>. Researchers are studying different eating windows to understand optimal timing and whether benefits continue over months and years. Studies published in Cell Metabolism (August 2023) and presented at NUTRITION 2026 show consistent improvements in memory and cognitive markers.

People should monitor for symptoms like dizziness or excessive fatigue when starting time-restricted eating. Those with diabetes, eating disorders, chronic health conditions, or taking medications should consult healthcare providers before significantly changing eating patterns. Additionally, symptoms of clinical depression or anxiety may emerge during adaptation, requiring medical attention.

The next phase of research will examine sex differences in response to fasting, genetic factors beyond APOE, and optimal eating windows for diverse populations. Researchers acknowledge current trials involve relatively small groups that are mostly women, highlighting the need for larger studies in diverse populations.

Cognitive decline isn't inevitable with aging. For people concerned about memory loss or dementia risk, time-restricted eating offers an accessible, evidence-based intervention worth discussing with healthcare providers.

Edmonton was hit by a severe thunderstorm on Saturday night that brought intense rainfall, widespread flooding, and power outages affecting multiple neighborhoods. <a href="https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/edmonton/thunderstorm-road-closures-power-outage-9.7284916" target="_blank">Environment Canada data shows nearly 30 millimetres of rain fell between 9 p.m. and 10 p.m., an unusually high amount concentrated in a single hour</a>.

The rapid rainfall overwhelmed drainage systems across the city. Multiple major roadways experienced significant flooding Sunday morning. Northbound Groat Road and sections of Whitemud Drive were closed to traffic as water accumulated across lanes. Connors Road, 127th Avenue, and 107th Avenue also flooded, forcing authorities to restrict access and warn drivers to avoid the areas.

Power disruptions affected a large portion of the city. <a href="https://globalnews.ca/news/11994058/edmonton-storm-whitemud-drive-flooding/" target="_blank">EPCOR reported 14 neighborhoods without electricity following the storm</a>, with the utility working through Sunday to restore service. Most outages were expected to be resolved by late Sunday morning or early afternoon as crews addressed downed lines and damage to electrical infrastructure. The power disruptions also affected traffic signals in multiple locations, creating hazardous driving conditions as intersections lost signal control.

The Yellowhead Trail, one of Edmonton's major transportation corridors, reopened to traffic by Sunday morning after flooding forced closure Saturday night. However, water continued to affect secondary roads and residential areas throughout the city Sunday.

Severe thunderstorms like this one are increasingly common during summer months as weather patterns shift. The concentrated rainfall demonstrates how urban infrastructure can be overwhelmed when precipitation rates exceed design capacity. Cities worldwide are reassessing drainage systems and flood mitigation strategies in response to more intense weather events.

Residents and city officials are assessing damage to buildings, vehicles, and personal property. The city cleanup continued into Sunday afternoon as crews addressed debris and water removal on affected streets. Environment Canada is monitoring weather conditions for potential additional thunderstorms over the coming days.

Edmonton's emergency response protocols activated quickly, with city services coordinating with EPCOR to restore power and clear roadways. The event underscores the importance of infrastructure preparedness for extreme weather events.

## COMPLETE ARTICLE WITH EMBEDDED LINKS

Two active hurricanes are currently affecting the Pacific Ocean region, with Hurricane Genevieve strengthening into a major storm while Hurricane Fausto continues to weaken. Both systems are being closely tracked by the National Hurricane Center, though they pose different hazard levels to nearby land areas.

<a href="https://toronto.citynews.ca/2026/07/26/genevieve-becomes-a-major-pacific-hurricane-off-mexico-but-no-direct-threat-to-land-is-forecast/" target="_blank">Hurricane Genevieve intensified to a major Category 3 hurricane with maximum sustained winds of 120 mph</a>, making it one of the stronger storms currently active in the Pacific. The hurricane's center is located approximately 515 miles southwest of Manzanillo, Mexico, and is traveling west-northwest at 16 mph. Forecasters indicate the storm will continue on a course well offshore from Mexico's coastline, keeping it away from direct land impact.

Despite the hurricane remaining at sea, Genevieve is still creating significant ocean hazards. The National Hurricane Center warns of life-threatening surf and rip current conditions along Mexico's southwestern coast, generated by swells from the distant storm. Mariners are advised to avoid the areas most affected by the large waves, and swimmers are cautioned against entering the water where rip currents are active.

Hurricane Fausto presents a different scenario. <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/24/weather/hurricane-fausto-tropical-storm-hawaii-updates.html" target="_blank">The storm has weakened to a Category 1 hurricane with maximum sustained winds of 85 mph as it moves toward the central Pacific</a>. Fausto's center was positioned approximately 910 miles east of Hilo, Hawaii, moving west at 14 mph. Forecasters predict further weakening over the coming days, with the system likely downgraded to tropical storm status within 48-72 hours.

While Fausto no longer poses a direct threat to Hawaiian islands or other populated land areas, it will still generate significant surf for parts of Hawaii. The National Hurricane Center recommends continued monitoring of the system's track, as tropical systems in the Pacific can be unpredictable.

The simultaneous presence of two hurricanes in the Pacific is not unusual during peak hurricane season from June through November. The warm ocean temperatures fueling these storms typically persist through late September, meaning additional hurricane development remains likely.

Residents in coastal regions from Mexico through Hawaii should monitor official National Hurricane Center updates for the latest forecasts. Emergency management agencies are prepared to issue watches and warnings if either storm's trajectory changes to threaten populated areas.
