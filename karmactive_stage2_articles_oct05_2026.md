## Why the Super System Is More Exposed Than It Looks

Australia's compulsory superannuation pool has grown to $4.5 trillion precisely because decades of mandatory contributions have flowed in faster than retirees have drawn them out. That is changing. Baby Boomers are retiring in growing numbers. The net demographic flow — contributions minus drawdowns — is shifting from consistently positive to increasingly neutral, and heading toward negative over the next decade.

Bank of America's analysis argues that Australian super funds have structured their portfolios for a long accumulation phase. They hold significant allocations in unlisted assets: domestic infrastructure, commercial property, and private equity. These assets generate long-term returns but cannot be converted to cash quickly if a fund needs to meet a spike in retirement drawdowns.

The difference between industry and retail super funds matters here. Industry funds — which manage the retirement savings of most Australian workers — have historically maintained heavier allocations to unlisted domestic infrastructure than retail funds. Retail funds tend to hold higher proportions in liquid listed equities and cash. If drawdown pressures mount, industry funds face a harder choice: sell unlisted assets at discounted valuations or sell liquid equities to maintain cash. The RBA's current rate setting makes both options costlier.

The scenario Cabana describes does not threaten fund solvency. Australian Prudential Regulation Authority oversight, mandatory capital standards, and the structure of the super guarantee mean fund collapses are not the risk being flagged. The risk is narrower: subdued returns on balanced options over the next two years if both US rates and Australian rates remain elevated while asset valuations face valuation pressure.

Members still decades from retirement have time to absorb lower near-term returns. The calculation is different for members aged 55 to 65 who are approaching or entering the drawdown phase. Those members carry sequence-of-returns risk: a period of below-average returns at the start of retirement permanently reduces the balance available for the remainder of retirement.

Reviewing the unlisted asset allocation in your fund's default balanced option is the practical step. Most fund dashboards now disclose what percentage of a balanced option sits in unlisted infrastructure, commercial property, or private equity versus listed shares and cash. If the unlisted proportion is above 30% in your default option and you are within five years of a planned retirement date, a conversation with a financial adviser about your drawdown sequencing is worth scheduling.

No. Australia's superannuation funds are heavily capitalized and strictly regulated by APRA. The "tipping point" identified by Bank of America refers to shifting demographic cash flows: as more members retire and draw down pensions, funds must maintain higher cash liquidity rather than locking capital into long-term illiquid property and infrastructure. Individual account balances are protected by the fund structure, but total return performance on balanced options will reflect how well funds manage the transition.

The next APRA quarterly superannuation performance and liquidity disclosure is the primary data point to watch. APRA's reporting will show whether fund liquidity buffers are building at the pace Cabana's analysis suggests they need to.

Lilly announced results from the TRIUMPH-1 Phase 3 trial in October 2026. At the 12mg dose over 80 weeks, participants achieved a mean weight reduction of 28.3%. Earlier Phase 2 data had reported up to 24.2% weight loss at 48 weeks. Secondary endpoints showed blood pressure normalization, a 72% rate of prediabetes resolution as measured by HbA1c normalization, and liver fat clearance of up to 80% in participants with metabolic liver disease. Retatrutide works by activating three hormone receptor pathways simultaneously: GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide), GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1), and glucagon. Current approved drugs — semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) — activate one or two of those pathways. Retatrutide activates all three. That third pathway, the glucagon receptor, is the mechanism that distinguishes retatrutide from its predecessors. Eli Lilly has not submitted a regulatory filing with the FDA. ## What the Glucagon Receptor Actually Does

Most coverage of retatrutide focuses on the 28% weight loss figure. The less-reported clinical finding is what the glucagon receptor pathway contributes beyond appetite suppression.

GLP-1 and GIP receptor activation — the mechanisms in semaglutide and tirzepatide — primarily reduce appetite by slowing gastric emptying and signaling satiety to the brain. The glucagon receptor does something different: it increases energy expenditure and drives hepatic fat clearance. The liver metabolizes more fatty acids under glucagon stimulation. That is why the liver fat clearance data in retatrutide's trials is more dramatic than in drugs that do not activate glucagon receptors.

Over 85% of participants in the TRIUMPH trials with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) — the condition previously called NAFLD or fatty liver — achieved normal liver fat levels below 5%. This places retatrutide not just as a weight management drug but as a potential treatment for metabolic liver disease, a condition with no currently approved pharmaceutical therapy in most markets.

For the [growing population using GLP-1 medications for weight management](https://www.karmactive.com/glp1-ozempic-wegovy-weight-loss-guide/), this distinction matters. Patients who have experienced a plateau on semaglutide or tirzepatide, or who carry a fatty liver diagnosis alongside their obesity, represent a population for whom the triple agonist mechanism offers a clinically distinct option — when it becomes available.

Phase 3 data also documented safety considerations that physicians managing patients on retatrutide will need to monitor. Resting heart rate increases were recorded during the trial, peaking around week 24 before stabilizing. The dose escalation protocol required slowing to allow cardiovascular adjustment. That means patients on retatrutide will need cardiac monitoring that current semaglutide or tirzepatide protocols do not routinely require.

Nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal side effects — common across the GLP-1 class — were also reported. The triple agonist mechanism does not eliminate these; the addition of glucagon receptor activation introduces its own metabolic effects, including potential impacts on blood sugar in patients without diabetes.

This is not a drug that will be handed out without physician oversight. The trial data supports a prescription model with active monitoring, not a direct-to-consumer telehealth model of the kind that has grown around semaglutide microdosing.

While viral articles celebrate the weight loss numbers, the most clinically distinctive finding in the TRIUMPH trial data concerns liver fat. MASLD affects an estimated 25%–30% of adults in the United States and has no pharmaceutical treatment currently approved by the FDA. Retatrutide's glucagon receptor pathway targeting liver fat positions it not only as an obesity drug but as an organ-level therapy — a framing that semaglutide and tirzepatide's trial data cannot support with the same evidence base.

# Solar System's Outer Planets May Become Unstable Far Sooner Than Earlier Models Suggested

A new study has recalculated how long the solar system's outer planets will remain in their current orbits. The answer — roughly one billion years after the Sun becomes a white dwarf — is about one billion times shorter than earlier models suggested. It is also a timeline that begins five billion years from now and ends roughly six billion years after that. For practical purposes, this is about mathematics, not planetary emergency.

The study, published in The Astronomical Journal and available through arXiv, used numerical N-body simulations to model the long-term fate of the outer solar system after the Sun exhausts its nuclear fuel and transitions into a white dwarf. The Sun is currently a mid-life main-sequence star with roughly five billion years of hydrogen-burning fuel remaining. When that fuel runs out, it will expand into a red giant — consuming Mercury, Venus, and almost certainly Earth in the process — before shedding its outer layers and contracting into a white dwarf. The new study asks what happens to the surviving outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) after that transition. The answer is that the changed gravitational dynamics of the post-white-dwarf system, combined with cumulative gravitational nudges from passing stars in the galactic disk, will destabilize the outer planet orbits. The AAS Nova summary of the research places the instability timeline at approximately one billion years after white-dwarf formation — a number roughly one billion times shorter than pre-white-dwarf estimates of $10^{14}$ years or longer.

For anyone unsettled by sensational headlines, this study has zero practical consequence for Earth or human civilization. The revised astronomical timeline of 100 billion years applies only to the cold remnants of outer planets long after the Sun exhausts its nuclear fuel and engulfs the inner planets in five billion years. It represents an advance in galactic orbital mathematics, not an imminent celestial hazard.

## What Actually Becomes Unstable, and When

Reading the study's results as "the solar system will fall apart" requires ignoring the timeline structure. Earth will face the Sun's red giant expansion in approximately five billion years — a timeline that already exceeds the current age of the Earth. The outer planet instability described in the study occurs one billion years after the Sun becomes a white dwarf. Adding that to the five-billion-year red-giant transition timeline, the total is roughly six billion years from today before the outer solar system enters its destabilization phase.

At that point, Earth no longer exists. It was consumed or thermally sterilized billions of years earlier. The instability the study describes applies to objects — possibly the remnant cores of outer planets — orbiting a cooling stellar cinder.

Prior models estimated the outer solar system could remain marginally stable for $10^{14}$ years or more — periods vastly longer than the current age of the universe. The new simulations compress that dramatically, but from an impossibly large number to merely a very large one. The comparison that makes headlines ("a billion times shorter") is accurate and deeply misleading simultaneously: the new timeline is still tens of billions of years.

## What Is Actually Driving the Instability

Viral articles frame the study as revealing hidden chaos within our solar system. The primary mechanism identified in the paper is external, not internal.

The Sun today holds the outer planets in stable orbits through gravitational force. As the Sun loses mass during its red giant phase and again as it sheds material into a white dwarf, its gravitational grip weakens. The planets' orbital energies relative to the Sun change as the central mass decreases.

Into that weakened system, the study's simulations introduce the gravitational perturbations from passing field stars in the galactic disk — other stars moving through the galaxy that come within range to tug on outer planet orbits over geological timescales. In the current solar system, these stellar flybys have negligible effect because the Sun's gravity is dominant. In a post-white-dwarf system with half the current solar mass, those perturbations accumulate. Over approximately one billion years of stellar flyby exposure, they strip the outer planets away into interstellar space.

It is not primarily that the solar system is internally chaotic. It is that external stellar traffic, which today barely affects orbital dynamics, becomes decisive once the Sun has lost enough mass to make the outer planet orbits loosely bound.

## Will Earth Be Destroyed by This New Solar System Instability?

No. The study's central result concerns the long-term fate of the outer planets after the Sun loses mass and becomes a white dwarf. Earth is expected to face the Sun's red giant expansion far earlier — in approximately five billion years — so the billion-year post-white-dwarf timescale does not apply to Earth at all. Earth's timeline ends billions of years before the outer planet instability the paper models would begin.

The research represents a meaningful revision of long-term planetary dynamics models and has implications for how astronomers study exoplanet systems around white dwarf stars. Roughly one-third of known white dwarf stars show evidence of heavy elements in their atmospheres — material from disrupted planetary bodies. The new model may help explain how that disruption occurs.
