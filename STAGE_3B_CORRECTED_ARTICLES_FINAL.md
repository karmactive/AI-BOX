# STORY 6: LAKE AMERICA NAMING DISPUTE RAISES QUESTIONS ABOUT WELLAND CANAL SHIPPING
**[CORRECTIONS APPLIED: Qualified legal conclusion, removed unsourced "normal operations" claim]**

The White House issued an executive order on August 27 directing the US federal government to rename Lake Ontario "Lake America" in US federal usage. The order instructs the Interior Department and the Board on Geographic Names to update the US Geographic Names Information System accordingly.

Canada's official geographical database continues to list the lake as Lake Ontario. The Canadian government has not changed its official name, creating a situation where the two countries now call the same body of water by different names in their respective official systems.

Some commentary has raised the possibility that Canada might retaliate by blocking US ships from using the Welland Canal, a critical waterway that connects Lake Ontario to Lake Erie. But there is no evidence that Canada has announced such a measure or that it is actively being considered as official policy.

The Welland Canal contains eight Canadian locks and raises and lowers vessels by 99.4 meters (326 feet). It is part of the larger St. Lawrence Seaway system, which has Canadian and US components administered through separate national institutions. The St. Lawrence Seaway Management Corporation operates the Canadian side, while the US Department of Transportation's Seaway Development Corporation administers the American portion.

The Boundary Waters Treaty of 1909 establishes that navigation of boundary waters shall remain free and open for commerce to both nations on equal terms. A discriminatory restriction on navigation would raise questions under the treaty's requirements for equal and non-discriminatory navigation.

The lake-naming dispute is symbolic and reflects broader Canada-US tensions. But converting symbolism into actual policy—blocking shipping—involves legal, economic, and diplomatic complexities that make casual speculation misleading.
