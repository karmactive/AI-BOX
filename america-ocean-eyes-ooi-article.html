New scientific reanalysis published today, June 12, 2026, confirms that the Atlantic "cold blob" south of Greenland — a long-watched anomaly in ocean surface temperatures — is being driven by a weakening Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation. The finding arrives at the worst possible time. On May 21, 2026, the National Science Foundation announced it was "descoping" the Ocean Observatories Initiative — the very network of sensors that has been watching the Atlantic's behavior for over a decade. Recovery of instruments from four of five ocean arrays is now underway or scheduled, with the Endurance Array already being pulled from the water this month. More than 900 instruments, a $368 million infrastructure investment, and ten years of continuous ocean data are being dismantled. What the ocean is saying right now is clearer than it has ever been. The tools built to listen are being removed.

What the Silence Will Cost

Each card below covers one of the major ocean systems the OOI tracked. Click to expand.

🌊
AMOC Monitoring
Atlantic circulation tracking
▼

The Irminger Sea Array sat directly within the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation — the deep current system that moderates climate across Europe and the Americas. It measured water density, temperature, and salinity at multiple depths, providing real-time evidence of how the current is changing. New research published June 12, 2026 confirms the Atlantic cold blob south of Greenland is caused by a weakening AMOC. That array is now being decommissioned.

Loss means reduced early warning capacity for an event that could add up to 1 metre of sea level rise to North Atlantic coasts and double flood costs for Miami and NYC.
🌡️
Marine Heatwave Tracking
Ocean temperature anomaly detection
▼

OOI arrays provided continuous, depth-resolved temperature data across four ocean regions. Marine heatwaves — events in which ocean surface temperatures run abnormally high for five or more days — are increasing in frequency and intensity. Early detection depends on dense sensor networks like OOI. Without the Endurance and Pioneer arrays, the Pacific Northwest and U.S. East Coast lose their most detailed real-time ocean temperature monitoring.

Loss reduces early detection capacity for ocean heatwaves that can devastate fisheries, bleach coral reefs, and intensify coastal storms.
⚠️
Coastal Flood Data
Sea level and storm surge inputs
▼

The Pioneer Array off the U.S. East Coast fed continuous data into storm surge and coastal flood prediction models. Current velocity, water column structure, and temperature data all influence how accurately meteorologists can forecast the behavior of coastal storms. The array sat in the zone where Gulf Stream dynamics interact with the continental shelf — a particularly important area for flood modeling along the Atlantic seaboard.

Loss degrades the data inputs for coastal flood prediction models that protect hundreds of millions of people living on the U.S. East Coast.
🐟
Fishery Health
Ocean chemistry and species habitat data
▼

Station Papa in the Gulf of Alaska and the Endurance Array off the Pacific Northwest coast monitored the ocean conditions that determine where fish populations can survive: dissolved oxygen, pH (a measure of ocean acidification), nutrient levels, and temperature. Commercial fisheries in both regions — worth billions of dollars annually — depend on scientific forecasts that draw on exactly this type of continuous observational data. The largest ocean census in history has documented the scale of marine biodiversity at stake.

Loss removes key inputs for fishery management decisions that affect food security, coastal economies, and endangered species protection.
♻️
Carbon Cycle Research
Ocean CO₂ uptake and acidification
▼

The ocean absorbs roughly 25–30% of all human-produced CO₂ emissions annually. OOI arrays measured dissolved inorganic carbon, pH, and partial pressure of CO₂ at multiple ocean locations to track how this carbon sink is changing over time. The Irminger Sea is one of the most active sites of deep water formation globally — where surface carbon is carried to depth and sequestered for centuries. Losing continuous measurement there leaves a critical gap in global carbon cycle accounting.

Loss weakens the ability to accurately model how much carbon the ocean is absorbing — a core input for global climate projections and emissions policy.

The Ocean Observatories Initiative was built between 2009 and 2016 at a cost of $368 million. Since becoming fully operational, it has maintained continuous measurements of water temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, pH, and current velocity across four major ocean regions: the Endurance Array off Oregon and Washington, the Pioneer Array along the U.S. Atlantic coast, the Irminger Sea Array between Greenland and Iceland, and Station Papa in the Gulf of Alaska. Together, these arrays monitored marine heatwaves, ocean acidification, carbon sequestration, fishery health, seafloor volcanism, and the deep circulation systems that regulate global climate.

Ed Dever, a professor at Oregon State University who helped lead the OOI effort in the Pacific Northwest, described the decision plainly: "It's a crippling loss of information." Dr. Helen Palevsky noted the irreversibility of what is being lost: "One of the real tragedies here is that collecting data effectively at this site was a huge engineering challenge, and it's not the kind of thing where you can just leave your notes for the next person." Chris Robbins of the Ocean Conservancy warned the removal will "create an irreparable blind spot for our country in predicting earthquakes, fishery health, storm forecasting, coastal flooding and more." The NSF described the change as a move toward a "nimbler approach to prioritize support for evolving scientific priorities and emerging technologies." Congress had blocked Trump administration proposals for 80% OOI budget cuts in both 2025 and 2026. NSF proceeded with decommissioning regardless.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation is the ocean current system that moves warm tropical water northward, where it releases heat into the atmosphere, cools, and sinks to flow south along the ocean floor. This circulation keeps Western Europe 5–10°C warmer than it would otherwise be, drives rainfall patterns in West Africa, and regulates sea levels along the entire U.S. East Coast. NOAA's dedicated AMOC monitoring and the Copernicus Marine Service both track this system as one of the highest-priority climate variables on Earth.

Research published in April 2026 documented AMOC decline across four different latitudes over the past two decades. A May 2026 projection placed the slowdown at 51% ±8% by 2100 under mid-range emissions. Today's reanalysis adds further weight: the Atlantic cold blob south of Greenland, long suspected as a fingerprint of AMOC weakening, is now confirmed to result from a declining circulation. The consequences are concrete: up to one additional metre of sea level rise in the North Atlantic beyond warming-driven rise, doubled flood costs for Miami and New York City metro areas, deep winter freezes across Europe, and prolonged droughts across sub-Saharan Africa. Fisheries in the North Atlantic are also directly affected, as record sea surface temperatures alter species distribution and productivity. These are precisely the processes the OOI arrays were designed to track continuously. A physics-based early warning study previously published in peer-reviewed literature had identified the AMOC as being on a tipping course — and flagged the critical need for sustained ocean observation. The US CLIVAR research program has separately documented links between AMOC weakening and changes in marine heatwave patterns across the Atlantic basin.

Four of the five Ocean Observatories Initiative arrays are in the process of being removed or scheduled for removal within 15 months, following NSF's May 21, 2026 announcement. The Cabled Array off Oregon remains in operation. All data collected over the past decade remains freely accessible through the OOI Data Center. The European Commission has since launched OceanEye, with an initial €100 million investment and a target of 35% contribution to the Global Ocean Observing System by 2035. The High Seas Treaty entered into force on January 17, 2026, with 82 ratifications — the United States has not ratified. Separately, ocean heatwaves and glacier retreat continue to be documented globally. A UN assessment published this month addressed the relationship between ocean protection and humanity's future. The observational gap created by the OOI removals is expected to affect climate modeling, fishery management, flood prediction, and earthquake monitoring for years to come.
