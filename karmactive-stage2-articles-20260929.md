The RBA's September statement contains a tension that's worth understanding. Housing prices have fallen in most capital cities. New housing loans have declined noticeably. Consumer spending is easing. In most circumstances, those signals argue for holding rates — or cutting them.

Borrowers on fixed rates aren't directly affected by Tuesday's announcement, but fixed terms set in 2022 and 2023 will continue rolling off through 2026 and 2027. ## Kelvin Wave Heading for California Could Raise Coastal Sea Levels This Fall as El Niño Strengthens

If you live on or near the California coast, a large-scale ocean phenomenon is heading your way this fall that most people don't know how to think about. A Pacific Kelvin wave is moving toward the US West Coast as a strong El Niño develops, and California's governor has already declared a state of emergency to prepare for what officials described as a potentially historic season. The wave itself can temporarily add up to around a foot of water above your normal tide line — and that extra foot changes flood risk calculations for hundreds of coastal communities.

A Kelvin wave is a slow-moving pulse of warm water that travels eastward across the Pacific Ocean during El Niño events. It spans thousands of kilometres and moves primarily below the ocean's surface. When it reaches the West Coast, it becomes a "coastally trapped Kelvin wave" — a feature NOAA specifically monitors because it compresses against the continental shelf and temporarily elevates sea levels along the coastline. NOAA's Fisheries division has confirmed it is actively tracking these waves as part of its El Niño arrival monitoring for the current season.

That temporary sea-level rise does not cause flooding by itself. What it does is shift the threshold at which ordinary events — a winter storm, a king tide, a large swell — become flooding events. Streets that drain normally under a standard high tide no longer do. Low-lying areas that rarely take water start to. For California's extensive coastline, much of which was developed before compound-risk thinking became part of planning, a Kelvin wave arriving during peak winter storm season adds a layer of risk onto an already elevated baseline.

Governor Gavin Newsom declared a state of emergency on 21 September 2026 to prepare for what the state described as a potentially historic El Niño season. The declaration allows California to pre-position emergency resources, fast-track protective measures and activate mutual aid agreements before conditions deteriorate.

### What El Niño Does to California's Coast

El Niño is a periodic warming of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean that reconfigures global weather patterns. For California, a strong El Niño year typically means wetter winters in the south, more intense storms along the coast, and elevated flood risk for rivers, creeks and coastal-zone properties.

The 2026–27 event is tracking as a strong El Niño event. California's Coastal Commission notes that strengthening El Niño conditions can add periodic sea-surface-height increases and increase the vulnerability of low-lying coastal areas during the period of impact. The combination of Kelvin wave sea-level rise on top of winter storms on top of long-term background sea-level rise creates layered risk that is harder to manage than any single factor alone.

This layering is the element that often gets lost in reporting on individual phenomena. NOAA scientists describe it clearly: the Kelvin wave is temporary — it lasts weeks, not permanently. But "temporary" only helps if there are no major storms during those weeks. If a strong atmospheric river makes landfall while the Kelvin wave is still pushing sea levels up, the damage potential is substantially higher than during a standard winter storm.

### The Background Sea Level Already Rising

California's Coastal Commission notes that the state's shoreline faces ongoing background sea-level rise separate from El Niño. The Kelvin wave effect is an acute event on top of a chronic problem. Communities that have adapted planning and infrastructure to historical flood levels are now facing baseline conditions that those plans didn't account for.

Low-lying areas in San Francisco Bay, the Los Angeles Basin coast, and the Central Coast — including Malibu, Santa Cruz and the Monterey Bay region — are among the areas the Coastal Commission identifies as facing elevated risk during El Niño conditions.

### What Coastal Residents Should Do Now

California's Office of Emergency Services recommends coastal residents in flood-risk zones review their insurance coverage before winter. Standard homeowners insurance typically does not cover flood damage — that requires separate flood insurance, often through the National Flood Insurance Program. Processing times for new NFIP policies run 30 days, so waiting until a storm is forecast is too late.

Residents should also understand their evacuation routes and confirm that local emergency alerts are enabled on their phones. The governor's emergency declaration makes additional preparedness resources available through county offices of emergency services. NOAA's National Ocean Service publishes daily tide predictions that will show elevated readings when the Kelvin wave arrives at specific coastal gauge stations — San Francisco, Los Angeles and San Diego are among the monitored locations.

## Card Surcharges Banned in Australia From 1 October 2026: What Businesses Can Still Charge You

The credit card surcharge on your morning coffee, your online order and your phone bill payment disappears from Thursday. From 1 October 2026, adding a percentage-based checkout fee for paying by Visa, Mastercard, American Express or eftpos becomes illegal across Australia. But the ban has boundaries worth understanding before you assume every hidden payment cost is gone.

The Reserve Bank of Australia's payment system reforms take effect 1 October 2026. From that date, merchants are prohibited from charging customers a surcharge specifically for using a designated card network. This covers Visa, Mastercard, American Express and eftpos — meaning the 1.5% or 2% fee that has been appearing at checkout on card payments is removed. The ACCC is the enforcement authority. The reforms also reduce interchange fees to help smaller businesses absorb card processing costs. The RBA has confirmed these changes apply across all registered merchants in Australia.

Before this week's change, Australians were paying an estimated $4 billion in card surcharges annually. That line-item cost disappears from individual transactions. It does not disappear from the economy — businesses still pay terminal fees and bank processing charges. The reform changes how those costs can be recovered; the costs themselves remain.

### What Businesses Can Still Legally Charge After 1 October

**Weekend and public holiday surcharges in hospitality remain.** These cover higher staff wage costs on penalty-rate days and have nothing to do with card payment processing. They are a separate legal category and are unaffected by Thursday's change.

**Cash discounts are legal.** The ACCC explicitly permits businesses to charge a lower price for cash payment, provided the standard advertised price is the card price. Some small retailers are already moving to this model rather than absorbing processing fees.

**Refusing to accept cards entirely is legal.** The RBA's surcharge ban only applies if a business chooses to accept card payments. A business that decides to accept only cash or direct bank transfer is operating within the rules. Customers cannot compel a merchant to accept cards. Some small cafes and market stalls are moving in this direction to avoid processing overhead entirely.

**Platform handling fees and transaction minimums are an active grey area.** Some school canteen platforms, childcare payment systems and council payment portals have introduced flat handling fees or minimum transaction thresholds — such as a $10 minimum for school canteen orders. These are not technically card surcharges because they apply to all transactions on the platform regardless of payment method. The ACCC has indicated it will monitor whether businesses use these structures to effectively recreate the banned surcharge. Whether specific platform fees breach the spirit of the reform is a question the ACCC's enforcement activity over the next few months will start to answer.

One detail from the RBA's own documentation that most coverage hasn't addressed directly: the ban only prohibits charging a fee if cards are accepted. It does not require businesses to accept cards at all. If a small business withdraws card acceptance entirely to avoid the cost question, that is a legal response to the reform.

For Australian small business owners managing the transition, [how the RBA's interchange fee caps work and what they mean for merchant processing costs](/rba-interchange-fee-caps-explained) covers the cost side in more detail. And for consumers wondering [how the surcharge ban affects reward credit card programs](/reward-credit-cards-surcharge-ban), that story also has some changes worth checking.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a shop still refuse to accept card payments after 1 October?**
Yes. Australian merchants have the legal right to decide which payment methods they accept. The new rules prevent businesses from adding surcharges if they accept cards — but they do not require businesses to accept cards at all. A shop that moves to cash or bank transfer only is fully within its legal rights.

**Do weekend restaurant surcharges disappear on 1 October?**
No. Weekend and public holiday surcharges in hospitality are separate from the card surcharge ban. They cover penalty-rate wage costs for staff, not payment processing fees, and remain legal after 1 October.

**Will the card surcharge ban make everyday purchases cheaper?**
In most standard retail settings, yes — the 1–2% card fee should stop appearing at checkout. However, some platforms for school and childcare payments are introducing flat handling fees or transaction minimums that may offset some of that saving on small purchases.

From 1 October, checking your receipts for a few weeks will show whether your regular merchants have removed card fees cleanly or introduced alternative charges. Consumers who continue to see non-compliant percentage-based surcharges after Thursday can report them to the ACCC at accc.gov.au.

Throsby, 84, underwent emergency hip surgery after being struck by an electric fat bike on an Illawarra shared path. The crash has also put real legal questions on the table — not for the police, but for parents.

Former ABC Classic presenter Margaret Throsby was walking on Sandon Drive in Bulli, on NSW's Illawarra coast, on 28 September 2026, when she was hit by a large electric fat bike ridden by a 16-year-old with a passenger. She suffered a broken hip, fractured thumb, severe facial bleeding and two head haematomas, and was taken to Wollongong Hospital where emergency surgery was performed. Her daughter, Holly Throsby, said her mother remains in hospital and described the impact as devastating. Holly has called publicly for mandatory licensing, age limits and training requirements for high-powered electric bikes.

When a pedestrian is struck on a shared path by an unregistered electric bike, standard compulsory third-party motor insurance does not apply — it only covers registered motor vehicles. Any medical costs, surgery fees and long-term rehabilitation become a private civil matter. Parents of a minor operating an overpowered or unregistered e-bike on public infrastructure face personal liability for all of those costs if a court finds negligence. Holly Throsby has said she does not intend to pursue criminal charges against the rider, but that choice does not eliminate civil liability.

### What NSW Law Requires on Shared Paths

NSW Road Rules and Transport for NSW micro-mobility guidelines are specific about what a legal e-bike looks like. A compliant motor-assisted bicycle in NSW must have a maximum continuous-rated power output of 250 watts, and the motor must cut out when the bike reaches 25 km/h. These rules apply to bikes sold and marketed as e-bikes for road use under Australian consumer law.

Fat bikes — the type of heavy-framed electric cycle involved in the Throsby collision — are commonly sold as off-road recreational equipment with power outputs ranging from 750W to over 1,000W. Many have throttle controls that operate the motor without pedalling, which is not permitted on public paths under NSW rules. When ridden on shared pedestrian infrastructure, they are illegal under NSW road rules regardless of how they were packaged at point of sale.

On shared paths specifically, riders must keep left, give way to pedestrians, and ride at a speed that is safe for conditions. Adults over 16 cannot ride on footpaths (as distinct from shared paths) unless they are accompanying a child. Riding with a passenger on a shared path is a separate offence under NSW road rules.

NSW Police conducted Operation Spoiler in the Wollongong area on 29 September 2026 — the day after the Throsby collision. ## Europe's Gas Storage Is 90% Full — But the Commercial Model That Kept It That Way Has Collapsed

European households are heading into winter with gas storage reservoirs above 90% capacity. That number is real, and the European Commission confirms there is no immediate supply-security risk for the coming season. The problem is that the economic system that used to fill those reservoirs voluntarily, profitably, and without government subsidy no longer exists — and that structural change has direct consequences for your energy bill regardless of whether there is a shortage.

EU underground gas storage facilities reached approximately 90% capacity ahead of the November mandatory fill requirement, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe data. The European Commission's Gas Coordination Group assessed on 3 September 2026 that there is no immediate security-of-supply risk heading into winter, citing increased LNG import capacity, diversified supply sources and reduced industrial gas demand. Storage levels are higher than the equivalent point during the 2022–23 crisis winter.

Those facts describe the supply position. They don't describe who paid to achieve it, or at what cost. The seasonal gas storage market — in which private traders bought gas cheaply in summer, injected it into underground caverns, and sold it at a premium in winter — effectively stopped working after Russia cut European pipeline gas supplies in 2022. Private operators are no longer willing to inject gas into storage without government price guarantees because the summer-to-winter price spread that once made storage commercially viable has collapsed. European governments are now directly funding the injection costs, and that money flows back to consumers through elevated network tariff charges embedded in energy bills.

For households budgeting for winter heating, the practical risk is not a shortage. It is a price spike. Full storage provides a buffer; it does not fix the commercial fragility behind how that storage gets filled — or refilled — if a cold snap draws it down faster than forecast.

### Why the Commercial Model Broke Down

Before 2022, European gas storage economics followed a predictable pattern. Gas was cheaper in summer because heating demand was low. Traders bought at summer prices, paid to inject the gas into underground facilities — salt caverns, porous rock reservoirs, depleted oil fields — and sold in winter when heating demand pushed prices up. The summer-to-winter price spread covered storage operating costs and generated a margin.

Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the subsequent cutoff of most Russian pipeline gas eliminated the conditions that made this work. LNG, which replaced pipeline volumes, is priced on global markets and responds to weather events and industrial demand spikes in Asia, the Middle East and Latin America — not to European seasonal heating cycles. The summer-to-winter price spread in European gas markets flattened or inverted. Private storage operators stopped financing injection programs because there was no margin in doing so.

As Bloomberg and the Financial Times reported in detail this week, several privately owned underground reservoirs in Central Europe are now operating under emergency state receivership or government-mandated fill requirements. European governments are assuming the full cost of the price risk that private traders used to absorb. The cost goes back to consumers through network distribution charges that most households see as a line item on their energy bills rather than a policy decision.

This is the structural change that 90% storage headlines don't capture: the EU has achieved the same physical storage number as previous years, but it has done so by nationalising the economics of filling it.

### The LNG Buffer and Its Limits

LNG replaced Russian pipeline gas as Europe's primary alternative supply source. European LNG import terminal utilisation is running above 75%, with capacity expansions in Germany, the Netherlands and Italy having added meaningfully to import capability since 2022.

The limitation is flexibility. Russian pipeline gas arrived at contracted volumes on a fixed schedule, regardless of what was happening in Asian energy markets. LNG tankers are global commodities — they go wherever prices are highest. During a cold snap, if Asian buyers are bidding aggressively for LNG cargoes, European importers must bid against them in real time. A cargo en route for a Netherlands terminal can, in principle, divert to South Korea or Japan if the price differential is large enough.

This isn't a theoretical risk. It happened in the winter of 2022–23, and the dynamics are structurally unchanged. Full storage provides insulation — if winter runs roughly to forecast temperatures, Europe has adequate supply. A prolonged deep freeze in January or February combined with a simultaneous Asian demand spike can deplete storage faster than replacement cargoes can arrive.

### What European Households and Businesses Should Do

For household consumers, the most direct hedge against winter bill spikes is a fixed-rate energy contract. Variable tariffs track spot prices in near-real-time; fixed tariffs don't. Locking in a fixed rate before the heating season begins protects against the scenario where cold weather drives spot-market prices sharply higher in January or February.

Industrial and commercial energy buyers across Germany, the Netherlands and Italy — Europe's three largest storage markets — face a similar calculation. Current forward-curve prices for Q1 2027 reflect an expectation of managed supply. If that expectation is tested by weather, forward prices will move rapidly.

Heat pump investment also becomes more relevant in this context. A household that has reduced gas consumption through electrification is exposed to less gas-price risk regardless of what happens on the spot market.

For background on [how European energy markets restructured after the 2022 gas crisis](/europe-energy-market-restructuring) and [Australia's growing role as an LNG exporter to European markets](/australia-lng-europe-exports), both are covered in depth on Karmactive.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Europe have enough natural gas for the 2026–2027 winter?**
Yes, under average winter weather. European gas storage exceeds 90% capacity heading into the heating season, and the European Commission's Gas Coordination Group confirmed no immediate supply-security risk as of September 2026. A prolonged cold spell combined with strong Asian LNG demand could draw down storage faster than new cargoes can replace it, pushing spot-market prices higher — but a supply cutoff under normal conditions is not the forecast.

**Why is Europe still at risk from energy prices if storage is nearly full?**
Because the system that keeps storage full now depends on government subsidies rather than market economics. Private gas traders stopped financing storage injection when the summer-to-winter price spread collapsed after Russia cut pipeline supplies. European governments are covering those costs, and consumers pay through elevated network tariff charges. Full storage does not equal cheap storage.

**Will European heating bills rise this winter?**
Probably yes on the fixed-cost component, which includes network distribution fees that have risen to cover subsidised storage. Spot-market exposure for variable-rate customers depends on winter weather and global LNG demand. A mild winter keeps spot prices manageable; a prolonged cold snap would not.

## Daylight Saving Starts 4 October 2026 in Australia: Which States Change and What Night Shift Workers Should Know

Australian daylight saving starts this Sunday, 4 October 2026. If you're in one of the five participating states or territories, you lose an hour of sleep this weekend. At 2:00 am on Sunday 4 October 2026, clocks in New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania and the Australian Capital Territory move forward to 3:00 am. Queensland, Western Australia and the Northern Territory do not observe daylight saving and do not change their clocks. From Sunday morning, the time difference between Sydney and Brisbane shifts from zero to one hour for the first time in six months. Daylight saving ends on Sunday 4 April 2027, when clocks move back one hour.

The practical effect for most people is losing one hour of sleep on Saturday night and gaining an extra hour of evening daylight through summer. Analogue clocks, appliances with built-in clocks and some older software systems don't update automatically — those need manual adjustment.

### What Happens If You're Working an Overnight Shift

An eight-hour night shift starting at 10:00 pm Saturday 4 October will end at 5:00 am clock time rather than 6:00 am — because the clock jumped forward at 2:00 am, effectively removing an hour. That shift covers seven actual hours of work. Whether you're paid for seven or eight depends on your employment contract.

Under Fair Work guidelines, unless your employment award or enterprise agreement explicitly specifies that payment is based on actual hours worked, employees are paid "by the clock." That means your roster shows eight hours, you get paid for eight, even though only seven passed. Workers whose agreements specifically pay for hours worked rather than rostered hours receive pay for seven. If you're on an award or enterprise agreement and aren't sure which applies, check with your employer's payroll team before the weekend.
