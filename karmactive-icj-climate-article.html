What Happened

A Student Dream Became International Law

On May 20, 2026, the United Nations General Assembly voted 141 to 8 to formally endorse a landmark ruling by the International Court of Justice — the world's highest court — confirming that governments have binding legal obligations under international law to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, protect people from climate harm, and potentially pay reparations to countries already suffering the consequences.

The resolution, led by the Pacific Island nation of Vanuatu and 12 co-sponsoring states, puts the ICJ's July 2025 advisory opinion on climate change and islands into action within the UN system. It tells every UN member state that climate inaction is not simply bad policy — it can be a violation of international law.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the vote "a powerful affirmation of international law, climate justice, science, and the responsibility of states to protect people from the escalating climate crisis," adding: "The world's highest court has spoken. Today, the General Assembly has answered."

What makes this especially striking is where the legal journey started: not in the chambers of a government ministry or the offices of a powerful NGO, but in a classroom in Fiji, where 27 University of the South Pacific law students from eight Pacific Island nations decided in March 2019 that they needed to take the climate crisis to the world's top court.

The Journey

Students from 8 Pacific nations at the University of the South Pacific in Fiji form the Pacific Islands Students Fighting Climate Change (PISFCC), beginning a campaign to bring climate obligations to the International Court of Justice.

96 states and 11 international organizations deliver oral statements — the most in ICJ history. The Court also meets with IPCC scientists to review best available climate science.

All 15 judges agree: climate inaction can constitute an internationally wrongful act. Fossil fuel subsidies and exploration licences may violate international law. States can owe full reparations — including compensation and restitution — to injured nations.
May 20, 2026
UN General Assembly votes 141–8 to back the ICJ ruling
The UNGA resolution formally endorses the advisory opinion, calling on all member states to align their climate policies with international law and their Paris Agreement commitments. Eight countries — including the US, Russia, and Saudi Arabia — vote against.

Why It Matters

The Nation at the Centre of This Case Is Also Its Most Vulnerable

Vanuatu, an archipelago of 83 islands scattered across the South Pacific, is consistently ranked the world's most climate-vulnerable country by the World Risk Index. Its 330,000 people contribute less than 0.01% of global greenhouse gas emissions, yet they face sea level rise running at roughly 6 millimetres per year around their shores — faster than the global average. In some areas, tectonic activity doubles that rate. Entire coastal villages are being considered for relocation.

The same story plays out across the Pacific. The Marshall Islands, Kiribati, Tuvalu — low-lying nations that have contributed almost nothing to the atmospheric buildup of carbon dioxide now measured at over 420 parts per million globally — are preparing for the possibility that parts of their territory may become uninhabitable within decades. These nations understand in a deeply practical way what the UN News coverage of this vote describes as an existential stakes in international climate law.

The ICJ's advisory opinion, in a notable departure, also confirmed that the principle of statehood continuity applies even if territory is lost to sea level rise — meaning a nation does not automatically cease to exist as a legal entity if part of its land disappears under water. This was a direct response to concerns raised by Pacific governments about their future legal standing.

In 2025, Vanuatu became the first Pacific Island nation to adopt a national Loss and Damage policy with a 10-year implementation roadmap — signalling that these governments are preparing for the long-term legal and policy work that this ruling now enables. Camile Cortez of Amnesty International said: "By adopting this resolution, states have recognised that they have legal duties to address the profound human rights crisis posed by climate change."

The Legal Details

What the ICJ Actually Ruled — In Plain Language

The ICJ advisory opinion is not automatically enforceable as a court judgment — it is an advisory opinion, meaning it clarifies the law rather than ordering specific states to act. However, the legal obligations it identifies — under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, the Paris Agreement, and customary international law — are binding. The advisory opinion confirms these obligations exist and that breaching them constitutes an internationally wrongful act. The UNEP notes that this distinction matters less than it may appear: advisory opinions from the ICJ carry enormous moral and legal authority and are routinely cited in domestic and international courts.

This is one of the opinion's most far-reaching elements. The ICJ held that the production and consumption of fossil fuels, the granting of exploration licences, and the provision of fossil fuel subsidies can all constitute internationally wrongful acts attributable to a state. That means a government that continues to expand fossil fuel infrastructure or hand out exploration permits, while failing to meet its emissions obligations, may be in breach of international law. This is an unprecedented framing that opens the door to climate litigation in national and international courts.

The ICJ confirmed that states may seek full reparations — including restitution, compensation, and satisfaction for moral or non-material harm — from states that have breached their climate obligations. The Court identified these as erga omnes obligations, meaning they are owed by all states to the entire international community — a duty running to every nation, bilaterally and beyond. This means Vanuatu, the Maldives, Bangladesh, or any other climate-vulnerable country could, in principle, bring a reparations claim against major emitters. The Court acknowledged that attributing specific harm to specific emissions is complex and would need to be determined case by case.

The ICJ addressed this directly and decisively: once a state is established, the loss of part of its physical territory — even to sea level rise — does not automatically end its existence as a state under international law. This principle of statehood continuity was a direct response to concerns from Vanuatu, Kiribati, Tuvalu, and other Pacific nations that they could be legally extinguished by the very crisis they did least to cause. It means their governments, treaties, rights, and international standing continue even as coastlines change. This was described by Pacific leaders as one of the most important elements of the ruling for their long-term sovereignty.

The ICJ determined that the 1.5°C temperature limit — established in the 2015 Paris Agreement — is the legal standard against which all climate policies must be measured. All states, particularly the largest emitters, are required to take ambitious mitigation measures aligned with best available science to stay within this boundary. Importantly, the Court also confirmed that obligations flow from both treaty law (the UNFCCC, the Paris Agreement, and the Kyoto Protocol) and customary international law — meaning even countries that have not ratified these treaties are bound by the underlying obligations. The ICJ held that states have a due-diligence obligation to regulate private actors — including corporations — operating within their jurisdictions. If a government fails to regulate a fossil fuel company's emissions adequately, that failure can itself constitute a breach of international law attributable to the state. This extends the reach of the ruling well beyond government-owned energy systems and into the regulation of private industry. Combined with the World Bank's finding that carbon pricing now covers nearly one-third of global emissions, this adds direct pressure on states to show they are using every available regulatory tool.

The Policy Landscape

How Far Has Carbon Pricing Come? (World Bank, May 2026)

The same week as the UNGA vote, the World Bank released data showing governments raised a record $107 billion from carbon pricing in 2025, covering nearly 29% of global emissions. But the ICJ ruling places all states under pressure to do far more.

Historical Context

The ICJ's July 2025 climate advisory opinion was the third such opinion on climate law from an international tribunal — following the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea's advisory opinion in May 2024, and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights' advisory opinion in July 2025. Together, these three opinions form an interlocking body of international climate jurisprudence that no major government can now claim does not exist.

The ICJ proceedings drew 91 written statements — more than any advisory proceeding in the Court's history since its founding after World War II. The December 2024 public hearings saw 96 states and 11 international organizations give oral statements in the Peace Palace in The Hague.

Where Things Stand

A Ruling Seven Years in the Making — Now Part of the International Legal Record

The UN General Assembly's vote on May 20, 2026 placed the ICJ's advisory opinion formally on the record of international law. The UN human rights experts who urged states to support the resolution noted that it converts legal clarity into a framework for action — one that covers emissions reductions, climate finance, the rights of vulnerable communities, and obligations to future generations.

For Pacific Island nations, the ruling addresses both the legal and the existential: it confirms their continued statehood, opens reparations pathways, and places the legal responsibility for the emissions warming their waters where climate science has always pointed — on the states that produced them. Vanuatu's government has been among those most vocal about using the opinion to push for stronger climate regulation and away from subsidised fossil fuel expansion.

The process that led here — from 27 students meeting in a classroom in Fiji in 2019, to a unanimous UNGA referral in 2023, to a record-breaking legal proceeding, to a 141-country endorsement in 2026 — has been documented across legal, diplomatic, and civil society channels. The next phase, as campaigners and legal scholars note, lies in how governments, courts, and institutions translate this legal record into measurable climate action, financing, and accountability. Whether those steps follow is what the coming years will show. The legal foundation, as of May 21, 2026, is now in place. For more on the broader ecological pressures accelerating alongside climate change, the stakes of that next phase extend far beyond human borders.
