The Bureau of Meteorology confirmed the tornado touched down in Ulladulla, generating a wind-gust front of approximately 98 km/h. The SES logged 34 damage reports across the town. Fifteen properties were affected, at least six severely, and two homes were left uninhabitable. SES crews were active for more than 24 hours following the event. No injuries were reported.

Despite the extent of property damage, BOM classified the tornado as sub-severe — a distinction that matters because it affects how emergency declarations and recovery funding are triggered. The damage was real; the official meteorological classification was not.

If your home or business was in the storm path, do not start structural repairs before photographing all damage and lodging a storm damage claim. Standard Australian home and contents policies cover tornado and gale damage under storm and tempest provisions without requiring a separate tornado clause or a declared natural disaster zone. For emergency roof tarping, tree removal and safety stabilisation, contact the NSW SES on 132 500 before private contractors clear or remove debris that could form part of your insurance assessment.

The sub-severe classification reflects a wind-gust front of around 98 km/h — below the 125 km/h threshold typically used to define a severe wind event — yet destructive enough to compromise roofing structures. Coastal NSW residential construction is engineered for straight-line wind loads rather than the rotational force of a tornado vortex. That is why properties directly within the tornado's narrow corridor suffered catastrophic roofing failure while homes just metres away survived intact. The 300-metre damage path visible in aerial assessments reflects that concentrated rotational energy rather than broad storm damage.

Ulladulla sits in the Shoalhaven region, where tornadoes are uncommon. Spring sea-breeze convergence and coastal thermal gradients can produce intense convective cells capable of brief tornado activity, though events of this scale are rare on this stretch of the South Coast. Information on [extreme coastal weather patterns](https://karmactive.com/climate) and [emergency storm preparation guidelines](https://karmactive.com/emergency-preparedness) is available for residents across the region.

**Does Australian home insurance cover tornado damage?**

Most comprehensive Australian home and building insurance policies cover tornado damage under standard storm and tempest provisions. You do not need a specific tornado rider. Photograph all damage — debris, unroofed sections, water ingress — immediately and before emergency clean-up crews arrive to tarp or clear the property.

The SES is continuing property assessments across the affected area. Further damage inspection tallies from Shoalhaven City Council are expected in coming days. If you have unassessed damage, register with the NSW SES at 132 500. Check back as official damage figures are updated.

The dispute centres on the Sunshine Protection Act, which would make daylight saving time permanent across the United States and end the twice-yearly clock change. Trump has publicly backed the legislation. The White House issued an administration policy statement supporting H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act of 2025, in July 2026. AP reported that Cotton's office was overwhelmed with calls following Trump's Truth Social post. The Sunshine Protection Act has been through multiple congressional sessions. A previous version passed the Senate by unanimous consent but stalled in the House at that time. In the current session, it has cleared the House while Senate resistance has mounted.

For the millions of Americans who find the clock change disruptive, this public escalation makes federal legislation to end it less likely to move quickly, not more. Lawmakers subjected to a wave of constituent calls tend to entrench rather than shift. The November clock change is expected to proceed on schedule. There is no Senate floor vote on the Sunshine Protection Act listed in the immediate term.

**The Battle Behind the Clock Change**

Cotton's opposition reflects sustained lobbying from medical groups including the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, which opposes permanent daylight saving time and instead advocates for permanent standard time. The argument centres on winter morning darkness: when clocks stay on daylight saving time year-round, winter mornings become darker later, which the medical lobby argues creates health and road safety risks — particularly for school-age children.

Agricultural committees have raised separate objections tied to sunrise-aligned working patterns. Transportation lobbies have pointed to international aviation scheduling: when one country permanently shifts its effective time zone, it disrupts commercial flight timetable coordination with airlines in neighbouring regions.

The result is that the Trump Tom Cotton daylight saving dispute has landed on a bill that is popular with voters, supported by the White House and already through one chamber, but blocked by a cluster of health, logistics and agricultural objections that political pressure has not resolved. Information on [federal clock change legislation](https://karmactive.com/policy) and the ongoing Senate debate is available for readers following the issue.

Trump's decision to publish Cotton's alleged personal number bypassed standard Senate procedural channels and turned an internal committee disagreement into a Capitol Police threat-monitoring matter, with officers tracking harassment directed at Cotton's office. That escalation has not produced any public movement from Cotton on his position.

**Will daylight saving time become permanent in the US?**

No. Permanent daylight saving time is not taking effect in the near term. The Sunshine Protection Act has not passed the Senate, and Cotton and other senators remain opposed. Americans will change their clocks in November under the existing schedule. Senate passage would still require presidential signature to become law, making any realistic timeline extend well into 2027 at the earliest.

Cotton has not publicly reversed his position following the post. Check back as the Senate's response to the escalation develops and as the November clock change approaches.

A magnitude 4.8 earthquake struck near Leongatha in Victoria's Gippsland region at 2:01am on October 3, waking residents across south-eastern Melbourne and the surrounding region. Geoscience Australia logged close to 21,000 felt reports — a high count for an event that produced no major structural damage or injuries.

The Victoria earthquake was centred near the town of Arawata, close to Korumburra and Leongatha, at a depth of approximately 10 kilometres. Two aftershocks followed: a magnitude 3.1 at 2:56am and a magnitude 2.5 at 12:25pm on the same day. No significant structural damage was reported and no injuries were recorded.

After any earthquake, smaller aftershocks are possible in the days and weeks that follow. Geoscience Australia has already recorded two. Further minor aftershocks remain possible but are not certain. The 4.8 event itself was a moderate-sized tremor by Australian standards — large enough to be felt widely but well below the threshold associated with widespread structural damage.

If you felt the earthquake and are uncertain whether your property sustained damage, check for visible cracks in walls, ceilings and foundations — particularly at corners and around door and window frames — and inspect chimneys, roof tiles and external brickwork. If anything concerns you, contact a licensed structural assessor before assuming the damage is cosmetic. Older brick and masonry structures are more vulnerable to minor seismic movement than modern timber or steel-framed construction.

A shallow depth of 10 kilometres amplifies surface shaking relative to a deeper quake of equivalent magnitude. The energy has less ground to travel through before reaching the surface, which spreads the felt area more widely and increases intensity at the epicentre. This explains why the Melbourne earthquake registered nearly 21,000 felt reports despite causing limited physical damage — the shaking was widespread without being severe enough to compromise engineered structures.

The Gippsland region sits within one of south-eastern Australia's more seismically active zones. Victoria experienced a 5.9 earthquake near Mansfield in September 2021 — nearly four times more powerful in energy release than Friday's event — which caused damage to some older structures in regional towns. The Leongatha area has its own history of minor seismic activity along faults running through the South Gippsland hills.

Australia sits on one of the world's more stable continental plates, but intraplate earthquakes occur regularly in the south-east. Geoscience Australia tracks and publishes real-time seismic data nationally.

Geoscience Australia is continuing to monitor the region for aftershocks. If you experience further shaking in the Gippsland area, you can submit a felt report through the Geoscience Australia website. Updates will follow if significant aftershocks are recorded.

Queensland Police seized 558 e-bikes, e-scooters and other electric mobility devices and issued more than 2,400 infringement notices in three months under a statewide enforcement operation. If you ride any kind of e-mobility device in Queensland, the rules generating the bulk of those fines may not be the ones you are expecting.

Operation Yankee Surety ran from July to September 2026 as Queensland Police's dedicated e-mobility enforcement program. The three-month results: 558 device seizures, more than 2,400 infringement notices, 1,500 of which related to helmet non-compliance, 157 speeding offences and 68 drink-riding prosecutions. The Queensland Government granted police seizure powers for illegal and non-compliant devices from July 1, 2026.

Helmet non-compliance generated more than 60 per cent of all fines. Of the 2,400-plus notices issued, roughly 1,500 were for helmet violations — a figure that suggests many riders either did not know helmet use was compulsory on e-mobility devices or chose to ignore it. Queensland law requires an approved helmet for all e-bike and e-scooter riders regardless of rider age, device type or riding location.

If you ride an e-bike or e-scooter in Queensland, understanding what gets a device seized matters now. Queensland Police can seize any e-mobility device they deem non-compliant during a public-place inspection. A seized device goes into the QPS database of lost, stolen and seized items. Getting it back requires demonstrating compliance, and if the device was modified to exceed legal limits, it may need to be brought into compliance before release is possible — at the owner's cost.

**What Queensland's E-Mobility Rules Require**

The speed limits are specific. Compliant e-bikes and e-scooters are capped at 25 km/h on roads and shared paths. When passing pedestrians on footpaths or shared paths, the limit drops to 12 km/h. Devices modified to exceed these limits, or that do not meet Australian design rules for e-mobility devices, are classified as non-compliant and are subject to seizure.

Motor output matters too. Devices with motors exceeding 250 watts — combined with speed capability above 25 km/h — can be reclassified as motor vehicles under Queensland law. Once reclassified, standard vehicle registration and licensing requirements apply. A rider on a non-compliant high-power device without a licence can face both a vehicle-use offence and a device seizure.

Most compliant e-mobility devices do not require a driver's licence to operate, and the Queensland Government has published specifications on [Queensland e-bike laws](https://www.qld.gov.au) for riders wanting to confirm their device's status. The QPS also maintains an official database where seized devices can be checked by serial number.

The enforcement pattern emerging from Operation Yankee Surety suggests that police are applying the full suite of powers introduced in July 2026 — not only targeting illegal devices but enforcing helmet use, speed limits and drink-riding rules as broadly as they would for any road user. The 68 drink-riding prosecutions in three months indicate that alcohol testing is being actively applied to e-mobility riders, not just bicycle and motor vehicle drivers.

Queensland is not the only Australian state reviewing e-mobility rules, but the combination of seizure powers and active enforcement under a named operation makes the Queensland framework one of the most actively enforced in the country. Riders in other states should check local regulations, as rules vary significantly between jurisdictions. You can find further coverage of [urban transport policy and safety regulations](https://karmactive.com/transport) for context across Australian states.

Operation Yankee Surety has concluded its initial three-month phase, but Queensland Police have indicated e-mobility enforcement will continue as a standing priority. Riders should check their device against Queensland Government specifications and confirm helmet compliance before their next ride.
