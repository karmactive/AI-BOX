# Air India A320neo Altitude Drop: When a 'Turbulence' Excuse Masked a Triple Hydraulic Failure

## The Immediate Crisis: August 4, 2026

On the afternoon of August 4, 2026, Air India flight AI2379 — an Airbus A320neo flying from Phuket to Delhi — experienced what passengers described as a sudden, terrifying drop of nearly 300 feet in altitude. Seventeen people were injured, including four cabin crew members, as the aircraft lurched mid-cruise two-and-a-half hours after takeoff.

Initially, Air India's public statement attributed the incident to "in-flight turbulence." But multiple sources familiar with the investigation now tell The Hindu that what passengers experienced was not weather-related at all. It was a cascade of technical failures — a triple hydraulic system malfunction, autopilot disconnection, and faults in both elevator control systems — that triggered a stall warning and forced the pilots to take manual control to recover the aircraft.

### What Happened in the Cockpit

According to sources who have reviewed the automated post-flight maintenance report, the aircraft's three independent hydraulic systems began failing in rapid succession. The A320neo's hydraulic architecture is designed for redundancy — three separate systems powering critical functions including primary flight controls, landing gear, nose-wheel steering, and braking. The loss of all three simultaneously is classified by aviation experts as extremely rare.

Within one minute, the maintenance report recorded nine distinct warning messages — low-pressure indications and failures affecting different combinations of hydraulic systems as the cascade unfolded. The autopilot disconnected during this sequence, and the co-pilot took manual control. Simultaneously, the aircraft triggered a flight-control stall warning, indicating a loss of lift or altitude.

As the aircraft pitched upward, one of the pilots applied significant nose-down input manually in an attempt to recover from the stall condition. This aggressive recovery maneuver — the standard protocol for an aerodynamic stall — is what caused the aircraft to lose approximately 300 feet in altitude in a matter of moments, throwing unsecured passengers and crew upward against overhead panels.

### Why the Turbulence Cover Story Matters

Neither Air India nor the Ministry of Civil Aviation has mentioned the technical fault indications in their official press statements. Air India's initial statement blamed "in-flight turbulence," and subsequent statements dropped the turbulence reference entirely without explanation. The Ministry's statement similarly made no mention of technical failures.

This discrepancy between official narratives and the technical reality has raised serious questions about transparency in India's aviation safety communication. When a serious incident occurs, accurate public communication is critical not just for passenger confidence, but for ensuring that systemic issues are identified and addressed before they lead to catastrophe.

The reclassification from a turbulence event to a "Serious Incident" by the Directorate General of Civil Aviation fundamentally changes the scope of the investigation. Under ICAO Annex 13, which governs aircraft accident and serious incident investigations, this reclassification means Airbus and France's Bureau of Enquiry and Analysis for Civil Aviation Safety (BEA) are now required to participate in the probe — something that would not have happened under a turbulence classification.

### Technical Deep Dive: The Three Hydraulic Systems

The A320neo is equipped with three independent hydraulic systems — typically designated as System A, System B, and the Alternate/Green system. Each system operates at different pressures and powers different sets of flight controls:

- **System A (Green)**: Primary flight controls, landing gear, nose-wheel steering, thrust reversers
- **System B (Blue)**: Backup flight controls, spoilers, normal braking
- **System C (Yellow)**: Secondary flight controls, emergency braking, ground operations

When all three systems begin to fail, the aircraft essentially loses most of its powered flight control capability. Modern fly-by-wire systems have mechanical backups, but the loss of hydraulic power makes precise control extremely difficult. The A320neo does have a mechanical backup flight control system, but it provides only basic pitch and roll control.

Aviation safety experts note that while dual hydraulic failures have occurred in commercial aviation, simultaneous failures across all three systems are extraordinarily rare. The most closely watched precedent is the 2008 British Airways Flight 38 incident, where an Airbus A330 experienced a dual hydraulic failure but retained enough backup control for an emergency landing.

### What Comes Next: The Investigation

The Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau (AAIB) has opened a full investigation under ICAO Annex 13 protocols. The investigation will examine:

1. The flight data recorder and cockpit voice recorder (both "seized" by investigators)
2. The sequence and timing of hydraulic system failures
3. Autopilot disconnection and flight-control system behavior
4. Pilot response and recovery actions
5. Maintenance history and any prior system anomalies
6. The psychoactive substance screening results

Airbus has confirmed that a team of specialists is being dispatched to assist with the investigation, as required under international aviation safety protocols. The BEA will likely coordinate with AAIB given Airbus's French nationality.

The final report, expected to be completed within 12 months under ICAO guidelines, could have far-reaching implications for Airbus A320neo fleet operations globally, as well as Air India's own safety protocols and maintenance oversight.
