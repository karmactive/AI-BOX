The GAO covered the period following the Office of Personnel Management's instruction to agencies to place workers on paid administrative leave as part of the DRP rollout. The $9.5 billion figure appears in nearly every news report about this study. What appears in fewer of them is the GAO's own caveat: the data may be overstated. **What the Data Can and Cannot Tell You**

The GAO report does not conclude that $9.5 billion was wasted. **Polar Vortex Winter 2026-2027: Models Signal Strong Start With Possible Mid-Winter Disruption**

If you're budgeting for heating costs or planning winter travel between January and March, the current stratospheric forecasts are worth tracking. Models show a strong polar vortex forming in the upper atmosphere this fall — but the same models suggest that vortex could weaken significantly around mid-winter. Whether that weakening produces the cold outbreaks that often follow is not yet established. What is established is the atmospheric setup that would make them possible.

The polar vortex is a band of cold, fast-spinning air high in the stratosphere — roughly 30 kilometers above Earth's surface — that circulates around the North Pole during winter. When it holds together, it keeps Arctic cold air bottled up near the pole. When it weakens or collapses, that cold air can spill southward into the United States, Canada, and Europe over a period of days to weeks.

Current modeling from the UK Met Office shows the vortex entering winter 2026–27 in a strong state. The 10-millibar pressure zone over the polar region is forecast to show the pattern typically associated with a tightly organized vortex in early winter. That is the good news for people who prefer milder conditions.

The concerning signal comes later. Forecasts indicate the potential for a Sudden Stratospheric Warming event — a rapid rise in stratospheric temperatures that can reverse the winds holding the vortex together — sometime in January or February 2027. These events have a surface impact, but with a lag: cold-air outbreaks linked to a stratospheric warming typically arrive 10 to 30 days afterward, not immediately. This means late January through February would be the watch window for disruption, not early winter.

**Why El Niño Matters Here**

A Super El Niño event is strengthening in the Pacific. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center puts the probability of a very strong El Niño continuing into fall and winter 2026–27 above 90%. That is relevant to the polar vortex because El Niño strengthens atmospheric waves that travel from the tropics into the stratosphere. Strong enough waves can disrupt the vortex from below — a pathway documented in atmospheric research on the ENSO–stratosphere relationship.

This is the mechanism researchers believe links a strong El Niño to higher odds of mid-winter polar vortex disruptions. It does not make a disruption certain. It raises the probability of the conditions that would produce one.

For the US, Canada, and much of Europe, the potential impact of a January–February vortex weakening is elevated risk of cold-air outbreaks. The US and Canada, particularly the Midwest and East Coast, would be most directly exposed. For Europe, models show a more mixed picture — the same period could bring cold disruptions in parts of eastern and central Europe while western Europe sees mild, wet conditions.

What does polar vortex weakening mean? When the vortex loses its intensity, the circulation that keeps Arctic air in place breaks down, and cold air becomes more mobile. Surface temperature drops in affected regions typically follow about two to four weeks after the stratospheric warming event, not at the same time.

These are model signals from forecasts made weeks to months in advance. The same models that show a January weakening could revise significantly as the season approaches. The most reliable update window is October, when NOAA publishes its official winter outlook based on [seasonal climate models](link-to-noaa-winter). Check back then for a firmer picture, and track the [El Niño winter impact](link-to-el-nino-explainer) as conditions develop.

**New Moon Crater 2026: NASA Finds 728-Foot McGetchin Impact Site That Formed in 2024**

Scientists using NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter have confirmed the existence of a new crater on the Moon — one that formed sometime in spring 2024 and went undetected for nearly two years until a researcher noticed an anomaly during a routine data review. The crater is 728 feet across and 141 feet deep, and it came with a thermal fingerprint that extends four miles in every direction.

The McGetchin crater formed between April 11 and May 22, 2024, when an object estimated at three to six stories tall struck the Moon's surface. The impact was large enough to excavate a crater roughly the size of a New York City block — wide enough that if it appeared in a populated area, it would be unmistakable. Impacts of this scale occur on the Moon approximately once every 132 years, according to NASA's assessment. The last time one was recorded, the first transatlantic radio transmission was still decades away.

NASA's instruments didn't catch the impact as it happened. The crater was found during a routine data quality check by researcher Robert Wagner, who paused what he was doing when he noticed an unusually bright patch in LRO imagery. That brightness — freshly exposed lunar material reflecting more sunlight — led to a full investigation that confirmed the crater's formation window and dimensions.

This discovery is not just about a hole in the Moon. When an impactor strikes lunar regolith — the layer of fragmented rock and dust covering the surface — it doesn't just dig a crater. It loosens the material around the impact zone, making it less dense. Less dense regolith holds heat differently. NASA's Diviner thermal instrument measured a 4-mile-wide cold region around the McGetchin crater that runs about 16°F cooler than the surrounding surface at night. That cold spot is the thermal signature of disturbed, fluffed-up soil.

**Why the Cold Spot Matters for Future Missions**

For space enthusiasts, a 728-foot crater is the headline. For NASA's Artemis program — which is working toward establishing a long-term presence on the lunar surface — the four-mile cold spot is equally important. Looser, disrupted regolith behaves differently under rover wheels and human foot traffic than undisturbed surface material. Understanding how far an impact event affects the surrounding terrain is directly relevant to where future landing zones and base sites are safe to operate.

The LRO has been surveying the Moon since 2009 and has catalogued over 100,000 surface changes. The McGetchin discovery stands out because of the scale — it is the largest new crater confirmed since the mission began — and because nearly two years passed between the impact and its detection. That delay is a function of how LRO works: it revisits surface areas on a schedule, not in real time. Its value is in the archive, not in live monitoring.

How big is the new Moon crater? The McGetchin crater measures 728 feet wide and 141 feet deep, formed between April 11 and May 22, 2024. It was created by an impactor estimated at three to six stories tall — the kind of impact NASA says happens roughly once per century.

The full findings were published in Science Advances. Follow [NASA's Artemis program updates](link-to-nasa-artemis) for how this and similar discoveries inform mission planning. More on how the [LRO monitors the lunar surface](link-to-lro-mission) is available directly from NASA's science team.

Water from the feature's storage tank tested positive for Naegleria fowleri DNA.

Naegleria fowleri does not infect through swallowing water or through skin contact. Infection occurs when contaminated water enters the nose and the amoeba travels to the brain, causing primary amebic meningoencephalitis — a condition that is almost always fatal. The infection has a death rate exceeding 97%.

If your children have played in outdoor water features, splash pads, or warm freshwater this summer — especially features that do not look like maintained swimming pools — the relevant precaution is preventing water from entering the nose. A nose clip is the most reliable protection during any submersion or face-splash activity in warm, unchlorinated water. You do not need to avoid all water. **18 Days: From First Splash to Fatal Outcome**

The timeline in this case is specific and documented. A 17-year-old was exposed at the water feature on August 13. Symptoms began on August 19 — six days later, consistent with the typical incubation period for Naegleria fowleri infection. The teen was diagnosed on August 26 and died on August 31. From first contact with the water to death: 18 days.

That timeline is not unusual for this infection. Naegleria fowleri infections progress quickly. Symptoms in the early days — headache, fever, nausea — resemble other illnesses. By the time the diagnosis is confirmed, treatment options are severely limited. Even with the best available care, the vast majority of recorded cases end in death. The speed of progression is why this infection generates public health attention even though reported US cases remain rare in any given year — typically under ten annually.

The El Futuro water feature has been closed. NCDHHS has not indicated that other water locations in North Carolina are currently under investigation in connection with this case. The investigation is ongoing.

How does Naegleria fowleri infect people? The amoeba enters through the nose — not by swallowing water. It travels along the olfactory nerve to the brain. Infection requires water directly entering the nasal passage. Submersion activities, face splashing, and jumping into water carry higher risk than simply swimming.

**Winter 2026-2027 Snowfall Forecast: When New York City Typically Gets Its First Snow**

Planning around the first snowfall in New York State this season means understanding two different things: what historical averages show about timing, and what the current seasonal forecasts actually say. Upstate arrives significantly earlier: Saranac Lake averages October 23 for its first measurable snow. Binghamton averages November 1. Syracuse averages November 5. Buffalo averages November 8. Albany averages November 16. Poughkeepsie averages November 30. Long Island's Islip averages December 14, generally after the city.

These are averages across many decades — meaning half of years see the first snow earlier, half later. They are not forecasts for this specific winter.

**What "First Snow" Actually Means**

The phrase "first snow" covers three meaningfully different events that can arrive weeks apart. Meteorologists distinguish between the first snowflakes observed (which often melt on contact and leave no accumulation), the first measurable snowfall (defined as at least 0.1 inches of accumulation), and the first significant snowfall (typically defined as one inch or more). The December 7 Central Park average refers to the first measurable snowfall — not the first flake, and not the first storm.

For commuters and school schedules, the first significant snowfall is the more relevant figure, and it typically arrives weeks after the first measurable event. For drivers deciding when to switch to winter tires, the first measurable snowfall is usually the trigger.

The larger climate context for this winter involves El Niño. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center puts the probability above 90% that a very strong El Niño continues through fall and winter 2026–27. Strong El Niño winters tend to produce warmer-than-average temperatures in the northern tier of the US and wetter-than-average conditions in the South — a pattern that historically has meant less severe winters in the Great Lakes and Northeast, though there is always year-to-year variation. This aligns with some model signals but is a general tendency, not a guarantee for any specific region or date.

The National Weather Service publishes its official winter outlook in October. That release, based on current sea surface temperatures and atmospheric conditions, will give the most reliable seasonal guidance available. Farmers' Almanac predictions and long-range model signals are inputs worth noting; the NWS outlook is where to anchor your planning.
