### NHS Psychiatric Wards Are Fitting AI Room Sensors to Monitor Patients Overnight — What They Record and What You Can Ask

Optical sensors are now installed in inpatient psychiatric bedrooms across NHS England. They track breathing and pulse through the night without physical contact and without video. Most patients first find out when ward staff mention the device at admission — not before. Whether a patient can refuse it, how data enters clinical notes, and whether the technology actually reduces harm are questions regulators have not definitively answered.

NHS hospitals are rolling out the Oxehealth Oxevision optical monitoring system in psychiatric inpatient rooms. The device uses infrared light to detect chest-wall movement and calculate pulse and respiratory rate continuously. No video image is captured or stored. The manufacturer describes the system as a replacement for manual 15-minute observation checks — the overnight routine in which a nurse opens a bedroom door to confirm a patient is safe. The technology is registered as a medical device with the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA). NHS trusts across England have expanded deployment over the past two years, with adoption accelerating during acute mental health nursing shortages. The Care Quality Commission (CQC) inspects facilities where the systems operate.

If you or a family member is admitted to an NHS ward with this system installed, ask ward staff directly for the trust's consent policy. Voluntary patients can formally request a physical privacy shutter or a room reassignment — that request must be recorded in the individual care plan. Patients detained under the Mental Health Act on sections 2 or 3 face a different threshold: clinical staff can determine that monitoring is required for self-harm prevention. Ask specifically how vital sign alerts are logged in clinical notes and who can review those records.

### What the evidence actually shows — and what NHS England's guidance states

The Oxevision sensor shines infrared light onto skin and reads micro-movements produced by breathing and heartbeat. A computer-vision algorithm converts that optical signal into vital sign numbers. No image is generated. If a reading falls outside a clinical threshold, an alert sounds at the nursing station.

Ward managers most often cite two benefits: detecting medical emergencies overnight before a check round would reach the room, and reducing the disruptive effect of repeated door openings on patients trying to sleep. Patient advocacy groups counter that continuous biometric monitoring — even without a video feed — is still surveillance conducted on people who are frequently admitted involuntarily and in acute psychological distress.

The legal position is genuinely unsettled. NHS trusts operate under Regulation 10 (Dignity and respect) and Regulation 12 (Safe care and treatment) of the Health and Social Care Act 2008, which require balancing patient rights against duty-of-care obligations. CQC inspection reports have found inconsistent written consent documentation across trusts — some treat sensor use as a standard care component, others as an opt-in arrangement. No single national protocol exists.

A detail that most media coverage has not addressed: the operational justification recorded in hospital management filings consistently emphasizes staffing economics — specifically, offsetting the legal requirement for physical observation rounds when nurse-to-patient ratios fall below required minimums. That is a different purpose from the patient safety claims made in manufacturer communications and public-facing NHS statements.

NHS England's published principles for digital technologies in mental health inpatient care state that the current evidence base does not establish that surveillance technology improves patient safety, care quality, or costs as a proven intervention. That is a stronger position than most reporting on the Oxevision rollout has reflected. If you want to cite a specific source when speaking with ward staff, the NHS England guidance on inpatient digital monitoring is publicly available at england.nhs.uk.

For patients and families, the practical questions to put to ward staff are: does this trust classify the sensor as mandatory or optional, can a physical shutter be activated and by whom, and how do system-generated alerts appear in the formal care plan?

[Understanding what the Mental Health Act says about patient rights to privacy and care plan participation](https://www.karmactive.com/mental-health-act-patient-rights-uk/) can help you know which requests clinical teams are legally required to document. A separate [guide to NHS complaint and advocacy pathways](https://www.karmactive.com/how-to-make-nhs-complaint-mental-health/) covers the formal routes available if your requests are not recorded appropriately.

The CQC has announced a thematic review of digital health technologies in NHS mental health inpatient settings. When published, it will establish clearer national standards on consent requirements, data access, and patient rights for non-video biometric monitoring. Until those standards exist, individual trust policies remain the determining factor. Check back when the CQC review findings are released.

### COVID Cases and Hospital Admissions Are Rising Again in the UK: What XFG Means, Who Faces the Most Risk, and What to Do Now

Hospital admissions linked to COVID-19 have been climbing week on week in England. The variant most responsible is XFG, which has also been called "Stratus" in health media coverage. If you are over 65, immunocompromised, or live with someone in either group, the practical questions are specific: how does XFG behave clinically, whether you qualify for a booster, and when symptoms warrant urgent care rather than home rest.

XFG is a recombinant descendant of previous SARS-CoV-2 FLiRT variants — it is not a new disease, it is the latest evolution of the coronavirus that has circulated since 2020. UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) sequencing data shows XFG now accounts for a substantial proportion of circulating COVID-19 cases in England. Hospital admissions have increased week on week through September and into October 2026, with rises concentrated among older adults and those with underlying health conditions. The NHS autumn booster programme is running now for eligible groups. Intensive care admissions remain low and stable compared with earlier wave peaks.

The XFG variant frequently presents with a notably hoarse or rough voice, intense sore throat, muscle aches, fatigue, and fever. Gastrointestinal symptoms — nausea, stomach cramps, loose bowel movements — are also reported more frequently than with earlier variants. If you are aged 65 or over, live in a care home, or have an immunocompromising condition, you qualify for an updated NHS autumn booster. Appointments can be booked through the NHS National Booking Service or at participating community pharmacies. A recent booster does not prevent infection, but it reduces your risk of serious illness.

### Does XFG cause more severe illness than earlier variants?

Current evidence does not establish that XFG causes more severe disease by itself. Clinical risk remains driven by age, underlying conditions, and vaccination status — not by any unique property of this specific variant. The same protective approaches that applied to previous autumn waves remain effective.

UKHSA surveillance data shows that the current rise in hospitalizations is predominantly patients admitted *with* a positive COVID test rather than *for* acute respiratory distress. Many are elderly patients admitted for other conditions who test positive during routine hospital screening. That distinction matters when interpreting the headline admission numbers: the figures reflect broad community circulation, not a step change in disease severity for healthy adults.

Loss of smell or taste — a hallmark of earlier variants — is reported far less commonly with XFG. The dominant presentation is upper-respiratory, closer in character to a severe seasonal cold or flu, with the gastrointestinal component as the clearest differentiator for people trying to identify whether they have COVID rather than influenza.

The colloquial label "American COVID" is an epidemiological misnomer. XFG was identified in multiple countries simultaneously. Its prevalence in both the US and UK reflects shared seasonal patterns — people spending more time indoors as temperatures drop — and naturally declining antibody levels across vaccinated populations over time.

If you test positive, stay home until you have been free of fever for at least 24 hours and your symptoms are clearly improving. Keep distance from elderly or clinically vulnerable contacts during that period. Call NHS 111 if symptoms worsen sharply — particularly breathlessness, a high fever that fails to reduce with standard medication, or confusion.

### Prince William and Ross Kemp Launched a Community Emergency Toolkit in Somerset — Here Is the Practical Guidance Behind It

Prince William traveled to Somerset this week alongside actor Ross Kemp to launch a national emergency preparedness initiative. Coverage focused heavily on a joke about Kemp's appearance on a television programme. The actual purpose of the visit — a research-backed toolkit designed to help UK households prepare for floods, power cuts, and other crises before they happen — received considerably less attention. The guidance is practical and directly relevant to millions of UK households approaching winter storm season.

The Ready Together toolkit was developed by the National Emergencies Trust (NET), which holds a royal charter with the Prince of Wales as patron. Nottingham Trent University conducted the underlying evidence review, examining more than 19,500 sources and narrowing them to 97 key studies, drawing on accounts from people with experience of more than 155 real emergencies. The Somerset launch site was chosen because the region is one of the most flood-vulnerable areas of England. Local grassroots emergency volunteer teams there are piloting the NET's community communication tools as part of a live trial. The Civil Contingencies Act 2004 framework and Local Resilience Forums provide the national infrastructure within which the toolkit operates.

UK government figures show that fewer than 15% of flood-risk households have a written emergency and evacuation plan. More than five million UK properties sit in areas with a recognised flooding risk. Homeowners and renters in coastal zones, river valleys, and areas historically prone to flash flooding should verify their risk on the Environment Agency flood map at check-for-flooding.service.gov.uk and register for automated telephone flood warnings. That registration is free and takes under five minutes — it can be the difference between hours of warning and none at all.

### What the Ready Together toolkit actually covers

The toolkit is organized around four areas: preparing before an emergency, responding during one, supporting recovery afterward, and identifying neighbors who may need help. It is designed for individual households, community groups, faith organizations, and volunteer networks — not only for emergency services.

For households, the most immediately actionable guidance addresses what to have ready before an event disrupts access to utilities, medicines, or communications:

**Essential contacts**: Write down numbers for your utility providers, GP surgery, a trusted neighbor, and your local council on paper — not only in a phone that may be flat or broken when you need them.

**Medications**: Keep at least a three-day supply ahead of your usual repeat prescription cycle, particularly for any medication that requires continuous daily administration.

**Power and communications**: Identify a battery-powered or hand-crank radio. A portable power bank large enough to charge a phone at least once is a minimum preparation for any multi-day event.

**Evacuation route**: Agree in advance where your household would go if the property became unsafe or inaccessible. If you live in a flood-risk area, confirm whether your most direct route uses roads that flood early — many do.

**Vulnerable neighbors**: If you know an elderly, isolated, or disabled neighbor, confirm that someone has their contact details and a plan to check on them within the first 12 hours of a major disruption.

Somerset was chosen deliberately for the launch. The region experienced the prolonged 2013–14 flooding of the Somerset Levels and has since built one of the UK's more developed local volunteer emergency response networks. Those groups are now piloting the NET's digital communication tools, designed to link community volunteers with Local Resilience Forum coordinators during an active emergency.

The National Emergencies Trust acts as a central fundraising and distribution coordinator during national-scale crises. It works with the British Red Cross and community foundations to channel relief funding to local groups within 48–72 hours of a national emergency declaration.

[Previous Karmactive coverage of UK flood risk zones and the Environment Agency warning system](https://www.karmactive.com/uk-flood-risk-preparation-guide/) provides more detail on how to interpret your property's risk band. For readers in Scotland, the [Scottish Environment Protection Agency (SEPA) flood alert system](https://www.karmactive.com/scotland-flood-alert-sepa-guide/) operates separately from the England and Wales service.

Winter storm season begins formally in November. The Ready Together toolkit and community sign-up materials are available at the National Emergencies Trust website. Local Resilience Forums publish area-specific risk maps and volunteer registration portals. NET grant allocations to regional forums across England and Wales are expected later this autumn — we will update this piece when those are announced.

Organ transplantation involves assessment, formal listing, waiting, and matching processes that can take months to years. Patients assessed as clinically suitable must meet specific criteria, demonstrate sufficient overall health to survive the procedure, and be monitored closely as their condition changes. International patients traveling to the US for a lung transplant — as O'Brien had planned — are not unusual among those seeking access to specific surgical programs, particular surgical volumes for complex cases, or different organ allocation timelines. ### Astronomers Found a Planet That Formed After Its Star Died — Here Is What the Discovery Changes About How Planetary Systems End

A paper published in the journal *Nature Astronomy* describes a planet orbiting a white dwarf star roughly 790 light-years from Earth. Exoplanets orbiting stellar remnants have been detected before, so that alone is not what makes this finding notable. What the evidence in the paper indicates is that this planet could not have survived the star's red giant expansion phase — it formed afterward, built from debris left when the star collapsed. If that holds under further observation, it means planetary birth is not limited to the formation of a solar system. It can happen twice.

Researchers identified the planet orbiting white dwarf WD 0145+234, approximately 790 light-years from Earth. The planet is roughly Jupiter-sized and completes one orbit approximately every 4.4 days, placing it extremely close to the stellar remnant. The study was published in the peer-reviewed journal *Nature Astronomy*, led by teams at the University of Warwick and international partner institutions. The white dwarf shows atmospheric metal pollution — absorption lines from heavy elements that should not be present in a cooling white dwarf unless rocky material is actively falling into it. That metal pollution is the physical signature of a debris disc, which the researchers propose supplied the material from which the planet formed.

This discovery changes a foundational assumption in planetary science: that star death permanently ends the story of a planetary system. If secondary planets can form around cooling white dwarfs from reprocessed rocky debris, then the eventual collapse of our own Sun — expected in roughly five billion years — does not necessarily mean permanent elimination of all bodies in this solar system. Material ejected during the Sun's red giant phase could, in principle, seed a new generation of worlds around the white dwarf that would remain. Whether those worlds could ever support conditions for life is a separate and entirely open question.

### How a planet forms after its star has already died

To understand why this discovery is unusual, the sequence matters. Stars like our Sun spend most of their existence fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. When hydrogen runs out, the core contracts and the outer layers expand — the star swells into a red giant many times its original size. In our solar system, the Sun's red giant phase is expected to engulf Mercury and Venus and may reach Earth's current orbital position.

After the red giant phase, the outer layers disperse as a planetary nebula. What remains is the compressed stellar core — a white dwarf, roughly Earth-sized but carrying about half the original star's mass. Its gravitational influence on the surrounding system is still considerable.

The inner orbits of a system undergoing this transformation are chaotic. Nearby asteroids, moons, and inner planets are gravitationally disrupted. Some are ejected. Others are pulled apart by tidal forces. Shredded material settles into a disc of rocky debris around the cooling stellar remnant.

Previous exoplanet discoveries around white dwarfs were explained as survivor planets — gas giants in wide orbits, far enough from the red giant's expansion to escape destruction. The planet around WD 0145+234 cannot be a survivor. Its current orbital distance is well inside the radius the star would have occupied at its red giant maximum. It must have arrived at its current position after the star's death — condensing from the debris disc that formed when the inner system was disrupted.

The metal pollution in the white dwarf's atmosphere confirms this picture. Heavy element absorption lines indicate that rocky material is still actively falling into the star. That ongoing infall is direct evidence of tidal disruption — the same process that would have supplied raw material to coalesce into a second-generation planet over millions of years.

The research team describes the system as a natural laboratory for observing planetary recycling in real time. The paper proposes follow-up observations using the James Webb Space Telescope to characterize the debris disc's composition and total mass — data needed to confirm the planet's formation history and estimate whether the accretion process is ongoing or complete.
