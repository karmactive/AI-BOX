### INTRO TEXT (WordPress block)

Football has always moved the world. But the 2026 FIFA World Cup — spread across the United States, Canada, and Mexico — is moving something else into the atmosphere: somewhere between 7.8 and 9 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent. That makes it, by a significant margin, the most carbon-intensive sporting event ever recorded.

The tournament began on June 11, 2026. Forty-eight nations are competing across 104 matches in 16 cities. While billions of fans watch on screens and millions fill stadiums, researchers at the Scientists for Global Responsibility and the carbon accounting platform Greenly are tracking a different kind of score. According to research on sustainable travel choices, the decisions fans make — on how to get there, where to stay, what to eat — carry real environmental weight.

This piece covers what the numbers mean, what independent researchers found, what FIFA has promised, and what the world's most-watched sporting event looks like when you measure it in carbon rather than goals.

### BODY TEXT (WordPress block)

**The Aramco Deal: A Sponsorship With a Carbon Shadow**

What most coverage of the 2026 World Cup's carbon footprint doesn't discuss at length is the FIFA-Aramco partnership. In 2024, FIFA announced a four-year, $400 million deal making Saudi Aramco — the Saudi national oil company — a Major Worldwide Partner through the end of 2027, covering both the 2026 men's and 2027 women's tournaments.

Aramco is responsible for over 4% of global CO₂ emissions annually. According to the SGR/EDF joint report, the sponsorship is estimated to induce approximately 30 million additional tonnes of CO₂e — more than three times the tournament's own direct footprint — through increased fossil fuel sales stimulated by global brand promotion.

When the deal was announced, 130 women players from 27 nations signed an open letter opposing it. The sports governance nonprofit FairSquare described it as "the most dangerous example of fossil fuel advertising and sponsorship the world has ever seen." The Sierra Club organised protests at World Cup stadiums across the US and Canada in June 2026.

Dr. Madeleine Orr, a leading academic in sport and climate, stated publicly that the 2026 World Cup "sends a dangerous message about the intentions of FIFA and other events to just get bigger and bigger" and is "completely misaligned with the promises they have made publicly to reduce emissions."

FIFA's stated targets — a 50% emissions reduction by 2030 and net-zero by 2040, commitments made at COP26 — contain no specific cap for the 2026 tournament and no strategy that directly addresses spectator travel, the dominant emissions source. Carbon Market Watch noted in June 2026 that "magical thinking will not solve the World Cup's giant climate problem."

**Heat Stress: A Risk Beyond Carbon**

The SGR/EDF report assessed climate risk at all 16 host stadiums and found that six face extreme heat stress — with Wet Bulb Globe Temperatures projected to exceed 80°F (26.7°C) during matches. That is the threshold above which physical exertion becomes medically dangerous. The affected venues include cities in the southern United States and Mexico. For players on the pitch and for thousands of fans in stands without full shade, this is not an abstract figure.

The past eleven years were the eleven hottest years on record, according to UN Secretary-General António Guterres. Staging a global event that draws millions of people into high-heat outdoor environments — and requires billions of air miles — is no longer a planning nuance.

**What Some Host Cities Are Doing**

Not all the news runs in one direction. Because existing venues are being used rather than new ones built, stadium construction accounts for only 3.1% of the projected footprint — compared to 24.6% for Qatar 2022. Houston's host committee committed to supplying 100% renewable electricity at all official World Cup sites. Lumen Field in Seattle diverts between 90% and 95% of stadium waste from landfills. Guadalajara's Estadio Akron has installed LED lighting and set a goal to reduce stadium waste by 50%.

Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) has been suggested for team travel, though availability at scale remains limited. For now, the gap between venue-level sustainability measures and the overwhelming footprint of spectator travel remains the defining tension of this tournament.

**What Fans Can Do — Practically**

For fans attending in person: choosing rail or ground transport over short-to-medium domestic flights cuts emissions substantially. Using official public transit routes, carpooling, and selecting plant-based food options at stadiums all contribute. Research shows that a growing share of travellers are willing to pay more for lower-impact options — but only when those options are visible and accessible.

For those watching at home: streaming devices and screens carry their own footprint. Turning off equipment when not in use, using energy-efficient screens, and choosing verified carbon offset programmes all make a measurable — if modest — difference. Merchandise and kits increasingly come in recycled-material versions from most major sports brands.

As individual nations build climate strategies, the role of global institutions — sporting or otherwise — in those strategies remains open.
