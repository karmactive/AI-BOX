After 40 Years, Mangrove Forests Are Growing Back Worldwide

A landmark Science journal study tracked four decades of satellite data and found global mangroves have shifted from net decline to net gain — one of the most hopeful ecological reversals in recent memory.

154,000 km² of mangroves globally (2023)

2010 year the global turning point began

Mangrove root systems advancing along a warming coastline — before 2010, agriculture and aquaculture operations had cleared 2,900 square kilometers of these forests globally. As coastal temperatures rise faster each decade, does the recovery hold, or does the same warming that's bringing mangroves north also threaten the mature forests that took centuries to grow? (Image: NASA Earth Observatory / Michala Garrison · Landsat 9 OLI-2 · USGS · March 2025 — Public Domain)

For most of the late 20th century, mangrove forests were disappearing faster than they could be saved. Shrimp farms, rice fields, and palm oil operations pushed deep into coastal wetlands across Southeast Asia, West Africa, and Latin America. Between the 1980s and 2010, nearly 2,900 square kilometers of these forests were lost globally. A study published in the journal Science in June 2026 — led by Tulane University and the University of Cambridge — has changed that story.

Using four decades of Landsat satellite images tracked by NASA and USGS, lead author Zhen Zhang and co-author Dr. Thomas Worthington analyzed every tropical and subtropical coastline on Earth. Their finding: by 2023, total global mangrove area had recovered to nearly 154,000 square kilometers — down only about 1 percent from the starting point four decades ago. The full picture of loss was far smaller than scientists had previously estimated, and the forests are actively growing.

"After decades of loss, we're finally seeing a global turning point for mangroves." — Zhen Zhang, Lead Author, Tulane University School of Science and Engineering

🌡️ Carbon Vault
3–5× more carbon stored per hectare than tropical rainforests. Despite covering less than 2% of tropical coasts, mangroves handle 30% of all coastal carbon burial — with over 6.4 billion tons locked in their soils.
🌀 Storm Shield
Up to 66% of wave energy absorbed within the first 100 meters of forest. During Hurricane Ian (2022), Florida's mangroves reduced storm damage by 30%. Each kilometer of mangrove reduces surge height by 20–50 cm.
🐠 Ocean Nursery
Over 70% of commercially important reef fish — snappers, groupers, shrimp — spend their juvenile phase inside mangrove root systems. Juvenile sharks here have 50% higher survival rates. No mangroves, no seafood.
🦋 Biodiversity Hub
Dozens of species depend exclusively on mangroves — from migratory shorebirds to rare crabs. Their dense root networks also trap sediment and pollutants before they reach coral reefs, keeping reefs alive.

Much of the recovery is driven by natural regeneration. Mangroves are recolonizing abandoned aquaculture ponds and expanding onto newly formed mudflats along river deltas where sediment accumulates. Along the U.S. Gulf Coast, including Louisiana, warming temperatures are now allowing mangroves to push into higher latitudes where they previously couldn't survive winters. China and northern Australia show similar trends. But the new forests are young — and young mangroves store far less carbon, offer weaker flood protection, and support fewer species than the mature systems that were cleared. Dr. Thomas Worthington, Head of Cambridge's Global Coastal Wetlands Lab, points to halting deforestation in existing mature forests as the single most effective protection available.

There is also a river sediment angle that most reporting on this story has bypassed. Mangroves expanding onto mudflats depend on a steady supply of river sediment. Upstream dams across Asia and Africa have been reducing sediment loads for decades — a structural constraint on how far natural recovery can go without deliberate human support. This means some regions that theoretically have the coastline for recovery may not recover on their own. The broader role of coastal wetlands in flood control depends precisely on this kind of physical integrity — root depth, canopy density, sediment stability — that takes decades to rebuild.

⚠️ What Still Threatens the Recovery

Despite global gains, deforestation continues in specific industries and regions

Rice, palm oil & agriculture ~36% of all mangrove deforestation
Shrimp aquaculture 26% of global loss (2000–2020)
Coastal development & urbanisation ~18% of total losses
Climate extremes (freezes, cyclones, sea-level rise) Growing in severity and unpredictability

The economic case for protection is now fully documented. A 2026 study from Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, published in PNAS, found that the global benefits of mangrove restoration are three to six times greater than their costs — with an estimated net value between $44 billion and $125 billion. Restoration could save $800 million in flood damage every year and reduce flood exposure for roughly 140,000 people annually. The communities that benefit most are low-income coastal populations in developing nations — the same people who historically bore the costs of being displaced by commercial farms and plantations. This aligns with findings on coastal vegetation's ability to dramatically reduce infrastructure costs for flood protection.

On the carbon side, mangroves store three to five times more carbon per hectare than tropical rainforests, and global mangrove soils hold more than 6.4 billion tons of carbon. Crucially, when those soils are disturbed or cleared, that stored carbon re-enters the atmosphere. Recovery does not automatically erase past emissions. Even as forests regrow, the carbon debt from decades of clearance persists in the climate system. This makes mature, standing mangroves far more valuable for climate targets than newly planted ones. Countries like Indonesia — home to the world's largest mangrove extent across 3.4 million hectares — have committed to restoring 700,000 damaged hectares as part of the global Mangrove Breakthrough initiative, which targets 15 million hectares by 2030. India's MISHTI initiative is funding restoration in the Sundarbans through The Nature Conservancy. More than 80 countries have signed commitments touching on coastal forest restoration under the 30x30 Global Biodiversity Framework.

The fishing connection is one of the least reported angles in this recovery story. Shrimp farming destroyed 26 percent of global mangroves between 2000 and 2020 — yet those same mangroves are the nurseries where shrimp, grouper, and snapper grow before reaching open ocean. According to IUCN, without functioning mangrove systems, global seafood supply chains would collapse. The industry that produces our seafood has simultaneously been among the most significant drivers of the very destruction that threatens its own future. Mangroves also filter the sediment and pollutant loads that flow from land before reaching coral reefs — meaning their recovery directly supports reef health. In a year when 60% of the world's reefs have been affected by bleaching, the connection between these two ecosystems matters more than ever.

Three Ways to Help This Recovery Continue

Everyday choices connect directly to what happens to these forests

🦐 Choose Certified Seafood MSC (Marine Stewardship Council) or ASC (Aquaculture Stewardship Council) certified products have strict controls against mangrove clearing. It directly reduces the market pressure that drove 26% of mangrove loss.
🌴 Reduce Palm Oil Palm oil expansion is the top driver of mangrove clearance in Southeast Asia. RSPO-certified products or palm-oil-free alternatives reduce the agricultural footprint at the coastal fringe where mangroves grow.
💙 Support Blue Carbon Projects Verified blue carbon projects fund mangrove restoration while paying coastal communities for conservation. Look for projects certified under the Verified Carbon Standard (VCS) with the Climate, Community & Biodiversity (CCB) standard.

The June 2026 Science study from Tulane University and the University of Cambridge tracked four decades of Landsat satellite data covering every tropical mangrove coast on Earth. The findings covered the decline from the 1980s through 2010, the turning point that followed, and a recovery to approximately 154,000 square kilometers by 2023. Carbon storage, fish nursery function, storm surge reduction, and biodiversity roles were quantified alongside the findings. The threats that remain — shrimp aquaculture, agricultural conversion, the fragility of young recovering forests, and climate extremes — were documented alongside the economic case for sustained protection, as detailed in the concurrent PNAS study from VU Amsterdam. The local fights over individual mangrove stands happening in cities like Navi Mumbai have always been part of this same larger story. So has the question of how financial instruments like debt-for-nature swaps can fund coastal forest protection at the scale the data now demands. And as with coral reefs that recovered when given space, the satellite data has answered the oldest question in conservation: these ecosystems do come back — if we let them.
