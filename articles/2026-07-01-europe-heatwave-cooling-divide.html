Europe's Heatwave Death Toll Tops 1,300 — WHO Calls It a "Dress Rehearsal"

Record temperatures, packed emergency rooms and a wide gap in who has air conditioning — here is what happened across Europe this June, and what health officials say people can do about it.

More than 1,300 people across Europe lost their lives in just over a week as a heatwave pushed temperatures past 45°C in Spain and broke national records in France. The World Health Organization's Europe office confirmed the toll on 30 June 2026, warning that the extreme heat now moving through Italy and the Balkans is only a preview of summers ahead. For millions of families, that meant shuttered schools, full emergency rooms and hard decisions about elderly relatives living without air conditioning. Here is what happened, why it turned deadly so quickly, and what health authorities say people can do about it, drawing on KarmActive's earlier coverage of Europe's heatwave pattern.

Spain's national weather agency, AEMET recorded 45.1°C in Andújar on 22 June, part of what its own meteorologists called the hottest opening days of any June since national record-keeping began in 1950. Daytime highs weren't the only concern — the nights of 22 and 23 June never cooled below an average of 20°C, the warmest June nights Spain has measured, denying the body the recovery time it needs after a scorching day. Across the border, Météo-France logged a national average of 30°C on 24 June, the hottest day the country has measured since 1947, with towns in the west topping 43°C. Red heat alerts eventually covered most of France, while Italy placed 22 cities — from Bolzano to Palermo — under similar warnings as the heat pushed east into the Balkans.

A rapid analysis by World Weather Attribution, a research collaboration of climate scientists, found that a heatwave this severe would have been virtually impossible in the Europe of 1976, and placed its intensity at roughly 3.5°C above what the same weather pattern would have produced fifty years earlier. The same researchers calculated that the overnight heat recorded this June is now about 100 times more likely than it was during Europe's 2003 heatwave, which killed more than 70,000 people across the continent that summer. WHO/Europe has separately tied heat to more than 200,000 deaths across the EU and neighbouring countries over the past four years, adding that nearly all of them were preventable. WHO Regional Director Dr Hans Henri Kluge told reporters the current heatwave is "a dress rehearsal" for what is coming, since Europe is warming at more than twice the global average.

Staying safe without waiting for a heatwave to end

💧Drink water regularly through the day — skip alcohol, sugary drinks and too much caffeine.
🪟Keep blinds and curtains shut during daylight hours if there's no air conditioning at home.
👕Wear light, loose-fitting clothing and use light bedding at night.
🤝Check on relatives, neighbours or anyone living alone — older adults face the highest risk.
🏥Seek help immediately for hot, dry skin, confusion or fainting — these need urgent medical attention.
🏛️Look up a public cooling space nearby — libraries, civic centres and pharmacies increasingly double as shelters.

Some cities are closing that gap without waiting for every household to install a unit. Barcelona expanded its public Climate Shelters Network to more than 500 sites this summer — libraries, civic centres, parks and, new for 2026, more than 60 neighbourhood pharmacies and shops acting as small-scale shelters. City figures show 99.2% of residents now live within a ten-minute walk of one of these spaces, each required to hold a comfort temperature of 26°C and offer free water. It's a model close to what Madrid has explored with its own underground cooling infrastructure, and it echoes the same practical steps in the World Health Organization's own public guidance: drink water regularly, wear light and loose clothing, keep blinds closed during the day, and check on relatives or neighbours who live alone, since older adults and people with chronic illness face the highest risk. For households without any of that infrastructure yet, simpler fixes are also being tested, including power-free cooling textiles designed as a low-cost alternative to mechanical air conditioning.

The heat also strained infrastructure well beyond hospitals. European fire-monitoring services flagged a high wildfire risk across Italy, the western Balkans and parts of Spain as dry conditions persisted through the month, while the World Meteorological Organization confirmed the event fits a broader pattern of European summers warming at more than twice the global average. The same agency has forecast that global temperatures are likely to stay at or near record levels through 2029. The IEA, meanwhile, projects that the number of air conditioners installed across the EU will more than double by 2050, reaching an estimated 275 million units, even as energy planners work out how to meet that demand without straining electricity grids further. WHO/Europe has found that more than half of the countries it tracks still lack a comprehensive heat-health action plan, even though its own estimates suggest existing adaptation measures already keep heat deaths roughly 80% lower than they would otherwise be — a gap KarmActive has tracked in earlier reporting on Europe's rising heat toll.

Europe's June 2026 heatwave was linked to more than 1,300 excess deaths, with national temperature records set in Spain and France and red heat alerts issued across dozens of regions. The World Health Organization's Europe office described the event as a preview of future summers and referenced its broader estimate of over 200,000 heat-related deaths across the region since 2022.
