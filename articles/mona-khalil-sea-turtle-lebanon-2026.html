The Woman Who Gave Her Life to Lebanon's Sea Turtles

Mona Khalil spent 25 years protecting the Mediterranean's most endangered turtles on Lebanon's Tyre coast. An Israeli airstrike on June 4, 2026, ended that watch. She died fifteen days later.

In a small orange house just steps from the sea, a 76-year-old woman had spent 25 years watching turtles come home. On June 4, 2026, an Israeli airstrike ended that watch. Mona Khalil — born in Lagos, rooted at last on a Lebanese beach — died on June 19, 2026, at the American University of Beirut Medical Center. She was the guardian of Lebanon's most important sea turtle nesting grounds, and a rare case study in what one person, with patience and resolve, can protect against forces far larger than herself. The story of her death — and what she leaves behind — is bound up with the wider question of what happens to species at risk when the people who protect them are lost.

Along a 20-kilometre stretch of Lebanon's Tyre coast, three nesting sites for the Mediterranean's most endangered turtles had been held together, for more than two decades, largely by her presence. The turtles are still returning to those beaches in the 2026 nesting season. Mona Khalil is not there to meet them.

A Turtle on the Beach Changed Everything

In 1999, Mona Khalil walked a beach near Tyre and watched a loggerhead sea turtle drag herself out of the Mediterranean to lay eggs in the sand. The turtle was doing what sea turtles have done for 100 million years — returning to the same beach where she was born, navigating thousands of kilometres of open ocean by magnetic memory. That night was ordinary for the turtle. It was not ordinary for Khalil.

She had been living in the Netherlands for over a decade. She had already known grief — she had lost her only son, a loss she carried quietly and channelled into the work that followed. Something about watching that turtle — ancient, determined, utterly defenceless on dry land — pulled her into a commitment she held for the rest of her life.

By 2000, she and her colleague Habiba Fayed had set up the Orange House — a bed-and-breakfast they painted the colour of her Dutch home, built right on al-Mansouri beach near Tyre — to fund their conservation through eco-tourism. Within three years, scientists from MEDASSET (the Mediterranean Association to Save the Sea Turtles, Athens) had trained Khalil and Fayed to run a formal monitoring programme. She made all her data freely available to the international scientific community. She presented her findings at MEDASSET's biennial conservation conferences. What had started as an encounter on a dark beach had become a body of scientific work.

Why These Two Turtle Species Cannot Wait

Only two sea turtle species nest in the entire Mediterranean — the loggerhead (Caretta caretta) and the green turtle (Chelonia mydas). The Mediterranean green turtle is classified as "Endangered" by the IUCN. Loggerheads are classified as "Vulnerable" globally, with their Mediterranean sub-population requiring sustained conservation even to maintain that rating, according to MEDASSET. Across the entire Mediterranean, roughly 8,000 loggerhead egg clutches are laid each year, and roughly 2,200 green turtle clutches — with the eastern Mediterranean, where Lebanon sits, hosting the highest concentrations.

Al-Mansouri beach, just south of Tyre, has been monitored by MEDASSET since 2001 and is considered Lebanon's most important sea turtle nesting beach. In one documented season, 61 nests were recorded there — 56 loggerhead and 5 green turtle nests. According to NOAA Fisheries, sea turtles take more than 20 years to reach breeding maturity, and some live up to 100 years. That long biological clock means every unprotected nesting season translates into gaps felt across decades. A turtle born in an unmonitored nest in 2024 that doesn't survive to adulthood won't be missed from the population until 2044 or later.

The threats on the Tyre coast are multiple and simultaneous: coastal development that erases nesting habitat, plastic pollution that entangles and poisons marine life, fishing nets that trap and drown turtles, artificial light that disorients newly hatched turtles before they can reach the sea. These are the same pressures affecting marine ecosystems across the globe. On Lebanon's Tyre coast, they now include armed conflict.

"Her work made her one of Lebanon's most respected voices for marine conservation and biodiversity protection." — Green Southerners, Lebanese environmental group

What War Does to a Nesting Beach

This is not the first time armed conflict reached these shores. Mona Khalil had already seen it once. After the 2006 Lebanon war, she documented the damage herself, in a report published by the British Chelonia Group: bombs had torn up the beach, and foxes driven from the nearby hills by the bombing subsequently raided 17% of loggerhead nests during the following 2007 nesting season. The data was specific. It was also a warning.

The 2024–2025 conflict was more severe. After Israeli airstrikes on southern Lebanon escalated in late September 2024, monitoring became entirely impossible. Volunteers could not reach the beach at night. Bomb craters appeared on the sand — some the size of a turtle body pit, others much larger. Debris and blue plastic bags from destroyed buildings scattered across the nesting zone — the same plastic pollution that conservationists had spent years clearing from the beach. A previous war had spilled approximately 15,000 tonnes of heavy fuel oil into the Mediterranean from bombed fuel tanks at the Jiyeh power station — a reminder that the coastline's environmental memory runs deep.

After the ceasefire, the damage continued. Lebanese army units deployed near the nesting area reportedly removed hundreds of bags of sand to build a defensive wall, and destroyed dune vegetation to create a football pitch. The nesting habitat that Khalil had spent years protecting continued to erode — not from war, but from the infrastructure of its aftermath.

"It is with deep sadness that we mourn the passing of Mona Khalil today." — Live Love Tyre, Lebanese environmental organisation — June 19, 2026

The Orange House and the Generation It Built

The Orange House was more than a home. Over 25 years, it became the operating base for something that Lebanon's official Tyre Coast Nature Reserve — the country's official 380-hectare protected area — could not have provided alone: a permanently present human consciousness, responsive and adaptive, watching the beach night after night across decades of seasonal change.

Khalil campaigned successfully against the privatisation of beach frontage, pushed for the ban of dynamite fishing that had been destroying marine habitat, and turned al-Mansouri into an officially recognised community-based conservation area. Every season she and her volunteers monitored the nests, recorded hatching success rates, and guided hatchlings to the sea. The beach she managed is part of a 20 km corridor near Tyre that includes three distinct nesting sites: the Tyre Coast Nature Reserve, El Mansouri and El Koliala, and El Aabbassiye beach — together the most significant concentration of sea turtle nesting habitat in Lebanon.

She mentored a generation of young Lebanese environmentalists, many of whom now call themselves "Mona's children." Several have already pledged to continue monitoring the sites she protected. Her family has said: "We hope to rebuild the Orange House one day and continue her legacy. The turtles will keep coming back; we need to support the people Mona trained to continue her work."

Who Carries the Watch Now

An Italian organisation, Blue Tyre, stepped in after the ceasefire with limited funding — covering fuel and basic monitoring equipment — to keep the 2026 nesting season patrols running. Volunteer Fadia Joumaa continues to guide newly hatched turtles toward the sea on al-Mansouri beach. "Mona's children" have pledged to maintain the nightly watch through the current season.

But the institutional gap is real. The kind of knowledge built over 25 years of nightly beach walks, of reading seasonal variation, of knowing precisely where turtles nest year after year — that knowledge does not transfer quickly. The turtles navigate thousands of miles of open ocean using the Earth's magnetic field to return to the exact beach where they were born. They need someone waiting for them when they arrive. For 25 years, that person was Mona Khalil. The broader pattern — of vulnerable wildlife losing their human protectors to violence, development, or neglect — is one of the less-told stories in global conservation.

The history of marine conservation shows that population recoveries are possible when monitoring continues consistently. They also show how quickly those gains can be undone when the chain of care breaks. Lebanon's sea turtle nesting sites now depend on whether that chain can hold through another season — and the season after that.

What the Record Shows

Mona Khalil was a Lebanese conservationist who, over 25 years, ran a monitoring programme for endangered sea turtles along the Tyre coast of southern Lebanon. She died on June 19, 2026, after an Israeli airstrike struck her home — the Orange House — on June 4, 2026. The Israeli military stated it had no indication the house had been struck and was reviewing its records. The two turtle species she monitored — the loggerhead and the green sea turtle — are classified as "Vulnerable" and "Endangered" respectively in the Mediterranean, as documented by the IUCN. Her nesting data, gathered over 25 years with scientific support from MEDASSET, was made freely available. The Italian organisation Blue Tyre and a group of trained local volunteers, including Fadia Joumaa, have continued beach monitoring through the 2026 nesting season. Lebanon's sea turtle nesting sites — across three beaches on a 20 km stretch of the Tyre coast — have been the subject of statements by Lebanese environmental groups including Live Love Tyre and Green Southerners following Khalil's death.

Sea turtles have returned to the same beaches for 100 million years. The loggerhead and green turtles of Lebanon's coast will come back again this season. What changes — and what has already changed — is who, and how many, are on that beach to meet them.
