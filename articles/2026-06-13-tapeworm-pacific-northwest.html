🐺 The Definitive Host: Coyotes & Foxes

Adult E. multilocularis tapeworms live in the intestines of coyotes and foxes, causing those animals no noticeable harm. The University of Washington's 2026 study found this parasite in 37 of 100 coyotes sampled around Puget Sound — a rate that surprised researchers, since this was the first confirmed detection of the parasite in wild animals on the contiguous US West Coast. Coyotes now routinely move through Seattle parks, backyards, and urban green corridors, shedding eggs continuously.

🦠 The Hidden Hazard: Eggs in the Environment

Coyotes shed microscopic tapeworm eggs in their feces. These eggs are exceptionally resilient — they survive in soil for months and resist freezing temperatures. They can contaminate garden produce, park grass, puddles, and wild berry bushes. Eggs are invisible to the naked eye, which means outdoor surfaces that look clean may not be. This is why unwashed foraged produce, gardening without gloves, and walking dogs in coyote habitat are all potential exposure routes.

🐭 The Bridge: Rodents as Intermediate Hosts

Small mammals — mice, voles, shrews — ingest eggs while foraging in contaminated soil. Inside the rodent, larvae hatch, travel to the liver, and form cysts. The rodent is now a living reservoir of infectious material. When a coyote eats that rodent, it becomes reinfected — completing the wildlife cycle. When a dog hunts or scavenges an infected rodent, the cycle is extended toward your household. The Pacific Northwest's abundant rodent population and active urban coyote presence create consistent opportunities for this chain to continue.

🐕 Your Dog: An Unintended Bridge

Dogs can become infected two ways: by eating an infected rodent, or by direct contact with coyote feces containing eggs. Since 2023, seven confirmed canine cases have been documented in the Pacific Northwest region. Dogs in wooded areas, off-leash parks, or yards where coyotes pass through carry elevated risk. An infected dog can then shed eggs in their own feces — potentially in your own yard. Neither the US nor Canada currently requires incoming dogs to be dewormed, unlike EU nations where dogs entering Finland, Ireland, Norway, Malta, or the UK must receive treatment against this specific parasite 24–120 hours before crossing the border.

🧑‍🦱 You: Long Incubation, Severe Outcome

Humans are an accidental, dead-end host — infection does not spread from person to person — but the consequences for the individual are serious. Exposure most commonly happens through handling an infected dog without washing hands, eating unwashed produce from contaminated soil, or touching outdoor surfaces. Once ingested, larvae migrate to the liver and form slow-growing, invasive cysts. These can take 5 to 15 years to produce symptoms. By then, the disease can resemble liver cancer and may have spread to the lungs or brain. The World Health Organization classifies this as the most lethal human helminthic infection known. There are currently no confirmed human cases on the US West Coast.

Geographic Spread

Where This Parasite Has Spread in North America

Historically confined to Alaska, the Arctic, and the north-central US, the 2026 University of Washington study now places E. multilocularis on the West Coast for the first time. The European strain detected suggests dogs in transit played a role.

New 2026 detection: Puget Sound, Washington State — first US West Coast case
Historical North American range: North-central US & Canada
Long-established: Alaska & Arctic regions

🐕 For Dog Owners

  • Ask your vet about monthly deworming (praziquantel or equivalent) if your dog has outdoor access
  • Prevent your dog from hunting, killing, or scavenging rodents and their carcasses
  • Use a leash in parks or green spaces where coyotes are known to be active
  • Wash hands thoroughly after handling your dog, especially after outdoor walks

🏡 For Your Household

  • Wash all fresh produce thoroughly — especially foraged berries and garden vegetables
  • Wash hands after gardening, hiking, or any outdoor soil contact
  • Remove outdoor pet food bowls — they can attract coyotes into your yard
  • If you see consistent coyote activity near your home, limit children's contact with that soil area
