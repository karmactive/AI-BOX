Why 2026 Is the Year Everything Changed

Smartphones became mainstream for teenagers around 2012. Rates of depression and anxiety among adolescent girls in the US, UK, and Australia began rising sharply around 2012–2014. Researchers Jonathan Haidt and Jean Twenge documented this parallel across dozens of countries — girls were hit hardest, with heavy social media users two to three times more likely to report poor mental health than light users. The US Surgeon General issued an advisory in 2023 warning that social media poses a profound risk to adolescent mental health.

"Social media is making our children unhappy and unsafe." — UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer, June 15, 2026

The evidence had been building. The question was always: what do governments actually do about it? Australia answered in December 2024. Malaysia answered on June 1, 2026. And on June 15, the UK became the third major country to announce a full ban. Today, Malaysia's Communications Minister Fahmi Fadzil confirmed that his government and the UK's Ofcom have been in bilateral discussions for over two years — turning what looked like isolated national decisions into the beginning of a coordinated international response.

The most recent NIH meta-analysis of 2025, covering over 18,000 studies, found significant reductions in depressive symptoms following gradual reduction in digital and social media usage by adolescents. The data, at this scale, is no longer ambiguous.

What the Research Actually Says

The data behind the laws — what it shows, and where scientists still disagree.

The most consistent finding in the literature is a gender gap. Research by Jonathan Haidt and Jean Twenge — drawing on large-scale national surveys in the US, UK, Australia, and Canada — found that adolescent girls who are heavy social media users are two to three times more likely to report poor mental health compared to light users. Boys show a smaller but still measurable effect. Girls' use involves more social comparison, appearance-based content, and cyberbullying, all of which are more damaging to self-image than the gaming-heavy patterns seen in boys.

The Gender Data Point Most Reports Miss

A 2026 study published in the Journal of Adolescence found international declines in academic performance and increases in loneliness specifically tracked electronic device and social media use. The effect was measurably larger for girls than boys across all 12 countries studied. This is why some researchers argue the ban, at its core, is a girls' mental health protection measure — even if it applies to all genders.

A 2024 systematic scoping review in the National Institutes of Health, examining 10 years of data from 2014 to 2024, found a complex but broadly negative relationship between heavy social media use and children's mental health. It identified cyberbullying as the single most consistent harm pathway. The WHO and UNICEF jointly released new guidance in October 2024 on improving access to mental health care for children and young people, noting that social media environments have become a significant structural factor.

There is, however, a dissenting body of research. Some studies find that moderate social media use has neutral or even positive effects — particularly for LGBTQ+ youth, children in geographically isolated areas, and those who use platforms primarily for community support rather than passive consumption. Critics of blanket bans argue that the design of platforms — infinite scroll, notification-driven engagement, algorithmic amplification of distressing content — is the problem, not access itself.

✅ The case for bans

  • Rates of depression in teen girls rose sharply with smartphone adoption and have not recovered
  • Platforms are designed to maximise engagement, which conflicts with healthy use patterns
  • Malaysia's Mental Health Association: 20% of youth suicides linked to cyberbullying
  • Lighter use or no use correlates with better wellbeing outcomes in large-scale studies
  • Self-regulation by platforms has consistently fallen short

⚠️ The concerns about bans

  • Age verification requires submitting sensitive biometric and ID data to platforms
  • Over 400 security researchers from 32 countries called for a moratorium on age-assurance tech
  • ARTICLE 19 warns that government-ID verification normalises surveillance of all users
  • VPNs and workarounds mean determined young users can still bypass restrictions
  • Social media is the main community space for some vulnerable youth (LGBTQ+, isolated, disabled)

What Every Family Needs to Know Right Now

Practical answers to the questions parents and teenagers are asking.

My child is under 16 and already has social media accounts. What happens?
▾
In Malaysia, age verification for existing users is being rolled out progressively over six months from June 1, 2026. Once a user is identified as under 16, they will be given one month to download or transfer their data — photos, videos, posts — before the platform applies restrictions or suspends the account. No penalties fall on parents or children. The law places all enforcement responsibility on platforms.

In Australia, the eSafety Commissioner has provided guidance that platforms should allow for a clear and kind account deactivation process, with a pathway to reactivate at 16. The UK ban is not yet in force — it is expected in 2027.
How does age verification actually work — and is it safe?
▾
In Malaysia, the requirement is among the strictest globally. New account creation requires verification against government-issued records — MyKad (Malaysia's national ID) or a passport — often combined with live facial biometric verification through MyDigital ID, Malaysia's national digital identity platform.

Privacy concerns are legitimate. ARTICLE 19, along with the Centre for Independent Journalism and Sinar Project, warned in June 2026 that submitting biometric and government ID data to private social media platforms creates a lasting digital footprint that users cannot control. Benjamin Loh, a social science lecturer at Monash University Malaysia, noted that "a user uploading their biometric data and government-issued documents on the platform cannot be certain that it will never be further shared or even sold."

Over 400 security and privacy researchers from 32 countries have called for a moratorium on age-assurance technologies until their effectiveness and societal implications are fully assessed. These are legitimate concerns that the laws have not fully resolved.
Does this apply to WhatsApp, YouTube, and messaging apps?
▾
It depends on the country and the platform. In Malaysia, the law applies to platforms with at least 8 million users, which captures most major social media services. In Australia, WhatsApp and YouTube Kids are specifically excluded. In the UK announcement, messaging services like WhatsApp and Signal are also excluded from the ban — but YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, Facebook, X, and Bluesky are included.

If you're unsure whether a specific app your child uses is covered, check with the relevant national communications authority — MCMC in Malaysia, eSafety in Australia, or Ofcom in the UK.
Can teenagers use VPNs to get around the ban?
▾
Technically, yes. A VPN can mask a device's location, and determined teenagers have always found workarounds. This is one of the most cited arguments against blanket bans — that they are more symbolic than effective for motivated users.

However, governments are addressing this differently. Malaysia's approach requires age verification at account creation, not just access — meaning it targets the platform's obligation to verify, rather than relying on location blocking. Australia's eSafety Commissioner has focused on requiring platforms to take "reasonable steps" with improving effectiveness over time, acknowledging that initial enforcement will be imperfect.

Most researchers agree: bans alone, without accompanying digital literacy education, parental guidance tools, and platform design reform, will have limited impact on the most vulnerable children.
Practical tips: How can families manage this transition well?
▾
Whether or not a ban applies in your country right now, the evidence consistently points to a few habits that actually make a difference:
  • Download data now if your under-16 child has existing accounts in Malaysia — they have one month from identification to export photos, videos, and messages.
  • Talk about why, not just what. Children who understand the reason for limits — not just the rule — are far more likely to cooperate and make better choices independently.
  • Set device-free times together, not just for children. Research consistently shows that parental modelling has more impact than restrictions alone.
  • Ask what they use it for. For some children — particularly those who are LGBTQ+, isolated, or dealing with health challenges — social media is a vital lifeline. Blanket removal without an alternative can do harm. Know what the platform means to your specific child.
  • Contact MCMC (Malaysia) or eSafety.gov.au (Australia) with specific questions about platform compliance or account issues.
