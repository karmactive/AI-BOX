Ocean Temperatures Just Broke Every June Record — And El Niño Is Only Getting Started

Global sea surface temperatures hit their hottest June on record as a strengthening El Niño builds beneath the Pacific — here's what the data means for coral reefs, monsoons, hurricanes and coastal communities this year.

The world's oceans just finished their hottest June on record. According to the Copernicus Climate Change Service and Copernicus Marine Service, the global average sea surface temperature reached 20.98°C last month, with a daily peak of 21.0°C on June 21 that beat the previous record set in 2023 and matched again in 2024. Outside the polar regions, the ocean has run between 0.35°C and 0.73°C above its long-term average for three years running, and June pushed that anomaly to a new high for the time of year. The record arrives as an El Niño strengthens in the equatorial Pacific — the same event KarmActive flagged earlier this year when NOAA put the odds at 61%. In its June 2 update, the World Meteorological Organization put the odds of El Niño conditions at 80% for June through August, rising to 90% after that. "The footprint of an El Niño travels far beyond its origins in the Pacific Ocean, impacting agriculture, energy supplies, trade, water resources, supply chains, and livelihoods across entire regions," WMO Secretary-General Celeste Saulo said.

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center made it official on June 11, upgrading its outlook from a Watch to a full El Niño Advisory. Its latest technical outlook puts the odds of the event growing into a "very strong" El Niño by the November-to-January window at 63% — a threshold that would place it among the largest since 1950. Some regions are already living the extreme end of that heat. The Mediterranean logged its warmest June on file, with sea surface temperatures averaging 24.3°C across a basin where 98% of the water sat inside marine-heatwave conditions. Carlo Buontempo, director of the Copernicus Climate Change Service, said current ocean conditions "could indicate the beginning of a new phase, leading, once more, to uncharted territory," adding that "with ocean temperatures at these levels and El Niño on the horizon, we are likely to see more temperature records fall in the coming months."

Days before that advisory, on June 8, the Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich satellite measured sea levels more than 15 centimeters above normal across a wide stretch of the equatorial Pacific, a sign of heat building beneath the surface. Severine Fournier, a sea-level researcher at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, said conditions in the western Pacific that week looked similar to the same time in 1997, the year an exceptionally strong El Niño reshaped global weather for over a year. This is not the ocean's first brush with record heat this decade. A jump in global sea surface temperatures between April 2023 and March 2024 was rare enough that a peer-reviewed analysis in Nature described it as a statistical outlier under current warming trends, and 2023, 2024 and 2025 rank as the three warmest years in NASA's 146-year instrumental record — a run KarmActive has tracked before in how scientists confirm a new global heat record. June 2026 extends that streak rather than breaking from it.

What you can do before your next ocean trip

🧴Choose mineral, reef-safe sunscreen before swimming, snorkeling or diving.
🐟Pick certified sustainable seafood to ease pressure on heat-stressed fish stocks.
🥤Cut single-use plastics — microplastics add extra stress to already-weakened corals.
🤿Avoid touching or anchoring on reefs; heat-stressed coral has less ability to recover from physical damage.
📡Check a live bleaching-alert map before booking a reef trip this summer.
🔌Small cuts to home energy use add up — lower demand means fewer emissions warming the ocean long-term.

Reefs are already feeling the heat build. NOAA Coral Reef Watch has placed the northern Pacific, including Hawai'i, along with Florida and Caribbean reefs, under a high risk of bleaching this summer. The program's coordinator, Derek Manzello, has pointed out that bleaching is now happening on a near-annual basis, which is starting to make it hard to say where one global bleaching event ends and the next begins. Reefs already carry the effects of the fourth global bleaching event, when heat stress affected more than 60% of the world's coral reefs between 2023 and 2025. A fifth global event, should NOAA confirm one, would arrive with less recovery time between rounds of heat stress than any point on record — a pattern KarmActive covered when marine heat pushed temperatures 2°C above normal at Ningaloo Reef.

The Arabian Sea and the wider Indian Ocean are known marine-heatwave hotspots, and this year's El Niño adds to that background warmth just as India's monsoon gets underway. The India Meteorological Department recorded the 2026 southwest monsoon reaching Kerala on June 4, with its own seasonal outlook pointing to a below-normal rainfall risk — a pattern KarmActive has followed since its earlier El Niño-linked monsoon drought-risk forecast and its coverage of six Indian cities crossing 46°C this year. Coastal fishing communities carry real financial exposure to this kind of heat: a global peer-reviewed assessment found measurable production declines in 322 fisheries — about 15% of those studied — during years of high marine-heatwave activity, with accumulated losses since 1985 topping 5.6 million tonnes and roughly $6.6 billion. Toxic algal blooms, which thrive in warmer water, add another layer of risk to that same coastline economy, a trend KarmActive has reported on separately.

Not every consequence of a warmer ocean points the same direction. Despite the record heat, NOAA's official 2026 Atlantic hurricane outlook favors a below-normal season, with a 55% chance of fewer storms than average, because El Niño tends to increase wind shear over the Atlantic that tears developing storms apart before they can organize. NOAA still expects 8 to 14 named storms and 3 to 6 hurricanes this season. UN Secretary-General António Guterres, responding to the WMO's June update, said "the world must treat it as the urgent climate warning it is." Saulo, the WMO chief, added that "advance seasonal forecasts and early warnings are vital to save lives and cushion the impact on our economies and our communities" — guidance the EPA's own reef-protection advice echoes at the individual level, from cutting single-use plastics to choosing certified sustainable seafood and reef-safe sunscreen before any ocean swim or dive this summer.
