People Dependent on Reefs

The fourth global coral bleaching event — confirmed by NOAA in April 2024 and believed to have concluded by mid-2025 — scorched 84.4% of the world's reef area across at least 83 countries and territories. In some parts of the Pacific, coral mortality reached 93%. For the billion people whose food, income, and coastlines depend on reefs, that fourth bleaching event landed as the worst in recorded history.

Then, on June 16, 2026, at the 11th Our Ocean Conference (OOC11) in Mombasa, Kenya, scientists presented numbers that shifted the conversation.

The 50 Reefs+ study — led by the Wildlife Conservation Society (WCS) and Macquarie University, with support from the Bloomberg Ocean Initiative — has identified 165,922 square kilometres of coral reefs across 71 countries and 100 territories with the strongest potential to survive the climate crisis. That is three times more than the original 50 Reefs initiative estimated in 2018.

The findings were drawn from over 45,000 field records collected between 1960 and 2025, cross-referenced against 42 environmental and human variables — including sea temperatures, heat stress events, cyclone activity, fishing intensity, and reef connectivity. The resulting map is 10,000 times more detailed than any previous reef resilience mapping exercise.

Where the Reefs Are — and Why It Matters to You

More than 61% of identified climate-resilient reefs are concentrated in five countries: Australia, the Bahamas, Cuba, Indonesia, and the Philippines. The remaining 39% stretch across 66 additional countries and territories — including parts of the Red Sea, the Western Indian Ocean, and the broader Pacific.

In India — where reefs span Lakshadweep, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, the Gulf of Mannar, and the Gulf of Kutch — researchers found pockets of resilience even within the fourth bleaching event. A study conducted during that period found a thriving coral reef in the northeast of Kalpeni Island, Lakshadweep Archipelago. India's reef systems support over five million people through fishing, tourism, and coastal protection, and the reefs of Andaman and Nicobar attract nearly two million tourists annually.

"Coral reefs are often framed as ecosystems beyond saving, but this research shows that there is a global set of reefs that have the potential to survive and recover from the climate crisis." — Dr. Emily Darling, Director of Coral Conservation, Wildlife Conservation Society (WCS), co-author of the 50 Reefs+ study

What "Climate Resilient" Actually Means

A climate-resilient reef, as defined in this study, is not one that cannot bleach. It is one that — over decades of observations — has either avoided, resisted, or recovered from marine heat stress. The research weighed thermal history, geographic positioning, reef connectivity, water flow patterns, and the degree to which existing human pressures had already weakened the coral community.

Reefs positioned in areas with cooler upwelling currents, or those that sit deeper in the water column (mesophotic reefs), scored higher. So did reefs in areas with lower fishing pressure, which tend to retain greater genetic diversity in their coral populations — a buffer that helps recovery after bleaching.

72% Are Left Exposed

The most direct finding from this research: nearly three-quarters of identified climate-resilient reefs currently fall outside any Marine Protected or Conserved Area. These are reefs that can survive climate change — but only if local threats like overfishing, pollution, and poorly managed tourism are also addressed.

At OOC11, 20 countries signed the High-Level Climate-Resilient Coral Reef Commitment. Five signed in Mombasa: Comoros, the Dominican Republic, Kenya, Mexico, and the United Kingdom. The target is 31 signatories by COP31 in Turkey (November 2026) — a coalition representing 80% of the world's coral cover.

The conference closed with $6.4 billion pledged across all ocean conservation commitments.

What Reefs Are Worth — and What Their Loss Costs

Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but support an estimated 25% of all marine species. Their economic value spans fisheries, tourism, pharmaceutical research, and the physical protection of more than 150,000 kilometres of coastline. Globally, reef ecosystem services are valued at up to $9.9 trillion per year (NOAA Ocean Service). More than one billion people depend on these systems for food, livelihood, or protection from coastal flooding.

In the Asia-Pacific region alone — which holds three of the five countries with the highest concentration of climate-resilient reefs — reef fisheries yield $25 billion in annual benefits. Research has established that losing key ecosystem services, including marine fisheries, could cut global GDP by $2 trillion a year. These numbers explain why the "Our Reefs, Our Future" campaign — launched jointly by WCS, WWF, and The Nature Conservancy — frames reef protection not as an environmental choice but as an economic one.

Antha Williams, who leads the Environment Program at Bloomberg Philanthropies, stated that the 50 Reefs+ research "gives governments, scientists, Indigenous Peoples, and local communities a powerful new roadmap for protecting them."

Local Threats Can Undo What Climate Resilience Preserves

Heat resistance alone does not make a reef safe. Overfishing, agricultural runoff, plastic pollution, coastal construction, and poorly managed tourism can degrade reefs faster than any natural recovery process allows. The IUCN has found that 44% of reef-building coral species now face extinction — a figure driven by the combination of climate stress and these local pressures acting together.

Ocean acidification adds a separate layer. Seawater pH off reefs has fallen from approximately 8.11 in 1985 to 8.05 in 2021 — a roughly 30% increase in acidity since pre-industrial times. Acidification weakens coral skeletons and reduces a reef's ability to physically rebuild, even when its coral populations have shown thermal resilience. This interaction between warming and acidification feeds directly into wider biodiversity loss patterns.

For reef-dependent communities — artisanal fishers in the Caribbean, dive tourism workers in the Philippines, coastal populations in Lakshadweep — resilience in the lab or in a model means little if the water running off their land is still choking the coral with sediment and fertiliser.

From 50 Reefs to 50 Reefs+: What Changed

The original 50 Reefs initiative in 2018 identified 50 specific reef systems most likely to survive climate change — the first systematic global effort to direct conservation resources toward reefs with the best chance. The 50 Reefs+ study builds on that foundation with 65 times more data points and a scope that now covers 71 countries rather than a curated set of 50 sites.

Both studies point to the same conclusion: reef fate is not predetermined. The difference between a reef that endures and one that does not is often a matter of where it sits geographically, how much fishing pressure it has absorbed, and whether governments act before conditions pass beyond the point of recovery. For the first time, there is a map precise enough to guide that action at a national level — across 165,922 square kilometres, in 71 countries, over 100 territories.

Sea surface temperatures have continued to register at or near historic highs through 2025 and into 2026. The conditions that drove the fourth bleaching event have not reversed. What the 50 Reefs+ study adds — and what the commitments made in Mombasa are built on — is a precise, data-driven answer to the question of where to focus.

A reef that has survived four bleaching events, managed its local fishing pressure, and now sits inside a Marine Protected Area is a reef with a genuine future. There are 165,922 square kilometres of them. The question is what happens to the 72% that are still waiting to be protected.

What Was Covered

The 50 Reefs+ study, conducted by the Wildlife Conservation Society and Macquarie University with support from the Bloomberg Ocean Initiative, identified 165,922 square kilometres of coral reefs across 71 countries and 100 territories with the strongest potential to survive the climate crisis — three times more than the original 2018 50 Reefs assessment. The findings were presented at the 11th Our Ocean Conference in Mombasa, Kenya, on June 16, 2026, alongside the launch of the "Our Reefs, Our Future" campaign, led by WCS, the World Wildlife Fund, and The Nature Conservancy. Twenty countries have signed the High-Level Climate-Resilient Coral Reef Commitment to date, with a target of 31 signatories by COP31 in Turkey in November 2026. The study drew on over 45,000 field records spanning 1960 to 2025 and 42 environmental and human variables. Seventy-two percent of the identified climate-resilient reefs remain outside any formal Marine Protected or Conserved Area. The Our Ocean Conference closed on June 18, 2026, with $6.4 billion pledged across ocean conservation commitments.
