# Italy Plans to Ban Burqas and Niqabs in Schools and Cap Foreign Students Per Class — What's Proposed and What Isn't Law Yet

**Published:** September 22, 2026  
**Category:** World / Europe / Policy  
**Format:** Breaking News  
**Target length:** 600 words

---

Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni announced on September 20 that her government intends to bring a measure to the Council of Ministers that would ban burqas and niqabs in schools, set a statutory cap on the number of foreign students per class, and require parents of foreign students facing serious integration difficulties to attend Italian-language lessons.

The announcement was made at the Fenix event in Rome. It has not yet become law. Before any of these measures take effect, they must be approved by the cabinet and then pass through parliament within 60 days. Understanding what has been announced versus what is enacted is the most important thing to get right about this story.

## What Was Proposed

Three elements were described in Meloni's remarks:

**1. A ban on burqas and niqabs in schools.** The proposal targets full-face veils — the burqa and niqab — not the hijab, which covers the hair and neck but not the face. Italian media reporting confirmed this distinction. The 1975 Reale law already prohibits concealment of the face in public places in certain contexts; the proposal would extend a specific ban to school environments.

**2. A cap on foreign students per class.** Italy has had a non-statutory guideline since 2010 limiting foreign students to 30 percent of a class, but enforcement has been inconsistent and the guideline has no legal force. The new proposal would create a statutory limit. The exact percentage has not yet been confirmed in the announced text.

**3. Italian-language requirements for parents.** Parents of foreign students who are assessed as facing serious integration difficulties would be required to attend Italian lessons.

## What Is Already in Place

The 2010 circular establishing a 30 percent guideline on foreign students per class is the current baseline. Because it's a ministerial circular rather than a law, schools are not uniformly obligated to enforce it. Meloni's announcement, if passed, would replace this unenforceable guideline with a statutory ceiling.

A constitutional law professor, Salvatore Curreri, cited by Italian media, questioned whether the proposal would withstand constitutional scrutiny — specifically whether differential treatment based on a student's foreign origin could conflict with Italy's constitutional equality provisions.

## How It Becomes Law

The measure must first go to the Council of Ministers (cabinet) for approval. If approved, it enters the parliamentary process — the precise legislative form (whether a decreto-legge or a standard government bill) has not yet been specified by the government. Opposition figures, including Green Europe MEP Angelo Bonelli, have already announced plans to challenge the proposal.

The timeline means Italians will have a clearer picture of whether this becomes law within roughly two months. The political calendar also matters: Italian regional elections and European-level pressure from the further-right FN bloc (led by MEP Roberto Vannacci) are part of the context in which Meloni is making this announcement.

**Q: Has Italy already banned burqas and niqabs in schools?**  
No. Meloni's September 20 announcement concerned a measure she said would be brought to the Council of Ministers — it has not yet been approved by cabinet or passed through parliament. It should not be described as enacted law until both stages are complete. The proposal targets burqas and niqabs specifically, not the hijab.

The political significance of Meloni's announcement extends beyond any individual provision. It signals a direction on immigration integration policy that will define a significant part of the Italian government's legislative agenda over the coming months — and the cabinet's response to the proposal is the next concrete event to watch.
