Magda Szubanski has publicly disclosed that her cancer — mantle cell lymphoma — is currently in remission, while also being clear that her doctors consider the disease incurable and expect it may return. She made the disclosure in an interview with Tracy Grimshaw for 60 Minutes, which aired this week.

The distinction between remission and recovery is not a technicality. For Szubanski and the people close to her, it reframes treatment not as a one-time ordeal that ends in a cure, but as an ongoing condition that requires monitoring, preparation, and a different kind of relationship with the future.

## What Is Mantle Cell Lymphoma?

Mantle cell lymphoma is a rare and often aggressive form of blood cancer that develops in the lymph nodes. The Leukaemia Foundation describes it as a B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, meaning it originates in a type of white blood cell. It accounts for roughly 6 percent of non-Hodgkin lymphoma cases and is more common in people over 60.

The cancer is typically diagnosed at a later stage, which contributes to its reputation as difficult to manage long-term. Treatments can include chemotherapy, immunotherapy, stem cell transplant, and targeted therapies. Remission — a reduction or disappearance of detectable cancer — is achievable, but relapse is common.

Szubanski cited an average survival figure of around 8.5 years from diagnosis, consistent with published medical literature on the condition. Individual outcomes vary significantly depending on age, health, treatment response, and disease subtype.

## What Remission Means in Practice

For anyone living with an incurable but currently remissive cancer, the practical reality shifts from "treatment is over" to a different kind of vigilance. Monitoring appointments continue. The possibility of relapse shapes decisions about work, relationships, and longer-term plans.

Szubanski's own account — from a woman who grew up in public view, played Sharon in *Kath & Kim*, and became one of Australia's best-known comedians — brings that medical reality into a context many Australians can engage with directly. Her willingness to name the prognosis plainly, including the part that most people avoid discussing, is the most significant part of the interview.

She connected her cancer experience to earlier trauma, including disclosures about her mother's past, that she is exploring in her forthcoming memoir. The memoir is due for release on September 29.

## Why the Language Matters

Several early reports described Szubanski as having "recovered" or suggested her prognosis was straightforwardly positive. Those framings are inaccurate. Remission in the context of mantle cell lymphoma means the cancer is currently controlled — not that it cannot return.

Writing about a celebrity's cancer without that distinction does a disservice not just to the subject, but to the many Australians living with similar diagnoses who understand exactly what "incurable but in remission" means from the inside.

**Q: What does it mean when cancer is in remission but considered incurable?**  
Remission means signs of the cancer have decreased or disappeared, but it does not mean the disease can never return. For mantle cell lymphoma specifically, remission is achievable but relapse is common, and the disease is generally not considered curable with current treatments. Ongoing monitoring and further treatment if the cancer returns are typically part of the long-term picture.

Szubanski's September 29 memoir will likely extend the public conversation about her diagnosis and treatment. Her candour in the 60 Minutes interview — naming the incurability directly while also communicating that she is currently well — reflects exactly the kind of honest, precise communication that health stories in this space too rarely get.
