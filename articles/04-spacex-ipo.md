## STEP 1: INITIAL DRAFT

SpaceX went public at $135 per share, raising approximately $75 billion in gross proceeds. But the real story isn't just the headline valuation. It's about how much of that wealth is locked away and what happens when insiders can finally sell.

The company offered 555,555,555 Class A shares on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX. That's a precise number chosen for branding and symmetry, but what matters is the scale: SpaceX's founders, early investors, and employees collectively held far more shares that they couldn't sell immediately.

This is where lock-up periods become important. Elon Musk, SpaceX's founder and CEO, faced a 366-day lock-up. He couldn't sell shares for a full year after the IPO. Other executives, directors, and early institutional investors faced staggered restrictions. These aren't punishment—they're standard practice meant to prevent massive insider selling that could crash the stock price immediately after the public offering.

On August 6, the first lock-up restrictions expired, affecting an estimated $120 billion in eligible shares. That's massive. If insiders decided to sell everything at once, they could theoretically flood the market. Even if they didn't, the mere possibility created uncertainty in trading.

The SEC prospectus warns explicitly that future large share sales by Musk or other shareholders—or even speculation that sales could happen—could push the stock price down. That's not prediction. That's the company telling investors in its official filings that insider selling risk exists.

So what's actually driving the valuation? SpaceX has three main business engines. Starlink, the satellite internet service, is expanding globally and generating recurring revenue from millions of subscribers. The launch business handles commercial and government missions, competing with rivals while maintaining high pricing power. The space infrastructure side involves data routing, communications, and satellite services. Together, these create a company worth far more than traditional aerospace firms.

Morgan Stanley, the underwriter, set a base price target of $300 per share and a bull-case scenario of $600. The analysis hinges on Starlink's subscriber growth, increasing orbital launch frequency, and space-based data infrastructure becoming commercially viable. These are long-term bets on technologies that work but require scale.

Here's the distinction that matters: the stock price doesn't equal the business fundamentals. SpaceX's IPO showed strong demand for the shares. But markets also move on sentiment, lock-up expirations, broader tech trends, and whether analysts think the $300 or $600 case is more likely. A stock decline doesn't automatically mean SpaceX's rockets stopped working or Starlink lost subscribers.

The xAI acquisition matters more than headlines suggest. SpaceX acquired xAI in February 2026, integrating an AI company into its operations. The SEC filing requires SpaceX's financial statements to include xAI's historical results retroactively, adjusted for a five-for-one stock split in May 2026. That split made shares cheaper but didn't change ownership stakes. When you own 100 shares pre-split, you own 500 shares post-split—each worth one-fifth as much.

Why does the xAI connection matter? SpaceX is positioning itself as infrastructure for both space and artificial intelligence. Starlink provides bandwidth. SpaceX infrastructure provides satellite compute. xAI needs both. This isn't just a rocket company anymore. It's becoming an AI infrastructure play.

The IPO proceeds are committed to specific uses: satellite constellation expansion, launch vehicle development, launch infrastructure, and general corporate purposes. That capital supports the long-term business model. But it also means SpaceX has money to execute, regardless of whether the stock trades at $100, $300, or $600.

For investors, the August 6 lock-up expiration created a test. Insiders had the chance to sell. The market had the chance to absorb that selling pressure. The question now is whether large insider sales actually happened or whether confidence in the business kept people holding.

What this IPO actually shows: SpaceX raised enormous capital at a high valuation, but investor expectations are stretched across several scenarios—conservative at $300, bullish at $600, and pessimistic below that. Lock-up expiration adds uncertainty. The business fundamentals remain strong, but the stock price reflects investor sentiment, not just operational reality.
