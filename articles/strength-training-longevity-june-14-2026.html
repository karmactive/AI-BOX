What Counts? (Tap Each to Explore)

The study used a broad definition of strength training — not just the gym. Tap a card to learn more about each type.

💪
Push-Ups
Chest · triceps · shoulders · core
No gym needed. Push-ups in their many forms — standard, incline, knee — all count as resistance training. Aim for 3 sets of 8–15 reps. They can be done anywhere, making them one of the most accessible qualifying activities in the study.
🏋️
Squats
Quads · glutes · hamstrings · core
The largest muscle groups in the body — in the legs and glutes — are engaged here. Building them improves metabolic health, insulin sensitivity, and joint stability. Bodyweight or loaded with dumbbells, squats are foundational to any strength routine.
🦵
Lunges
Legs · balance · stabilisers
Single-leg work trains balance and functional strength that directly reduces fall risk in older adults. Lunges also expose imbalances between left and right sides — important to address with age. No equipment required.
🏗️
Weightlifting
Full body · compound & isolation moves
Traditional resistance training with dumbbells, barbells, or cable machines. Compound lifts — deadlifts, rows, bench presses — deliver the most benefit per minute by engaging multiple major muscle groups simultaneously. Most efficient route to the 90-minute threshold.
⚠️ The Muscle Clock Starts Ticking at 30

Sarcopenia — age-related muscle loss — affects an estimated 8–15% of adults aged 40–64 and up to 50% of those over 80. It begins at 30 and accelerates. 🌍

1 in 3 Adults Still Not Meeting Basic Exercise Guidelines

The World Health Organization recommends that adults perform muscle-strengthening activities at moderate or greater intensity on at least 2 days per week, targeting all major muscle groups. Despite decades of public health guidance, research published in April 2026 confirmed that approximately one in three adults globally still does not meet these minimum recommendations. The Harvard study's findings add measurable weight to why closing that gap matters.

How to Reach 90 Minutes a Week: A Practical Starting Point
1
Start with two sessions of 45 minutes each Two sessions per week gets you to the 90-minute threshold. Choose any two days with at least one rest day between them — Monday and Thursday, for example.
2
Bodyweight is enough to begin Push-ups, squats, lunges, and planks require zero equipment. Start with 3 sets of 8–10 repetitions per exercise. These all counted in the Harvard study.
3
Add resistance when the last 2 reps stop feeling hard If the final 2 reps of your last set are easy, increase resistance — heavier weight, more reps, or a harder variation. Progressive challenge is what maintains and builds muscle.
4
Add a daily walk to unlock the combined benefit A 30-minute brisk walk daily adds 3.5 hours of aerobic activity weekly — approaching the WHO aerobic recommendation. Combined with 90 minutes of strength work, the mortality risk reduction in the data rises substantially.
5
If you are over 50 or managing a health condition, consult a doctor first While the study found benefits across all age groups, individual joint conditions, cardiovascular issues, or long gaps in activity make a medical check-in worthwhile before starting.
