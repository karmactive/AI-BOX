## STEP 1: INITIAL DRAFT

Australia's biggest bank recorded a 15% drop in home loan applications this quarter. That number sounds alarming—but what exactly fell, and what does it tell us about the housing market?

The key point: applications fell by 15% compared to the previous quarter. This doesn't mean house prices crashed 15%, or that people stopped buying homes entirely. It means the number of loan applications NAB received dropped compared to three months earlier.

Owner-occupier applications fell 14%, while investor applications dropped 17%. That's the real story. Investors—people buying property to rent it out—pulled back more sharply than people buying their first or next family home.

Why? Interest rates remain elevated, which makes borrowing more expensive. On top of that, the government announced tax changes that will eventually affect investors. Starting from May 2026, investors buying established properties can't deduct losses against non-property income like wages. From July 2027, negative gearing for residential properties applies only to new builds. These changes matter most to investors who've traditionally relied on loss deductions to reduce their tax bills.

There's confusion about these dates, so it's worth separating fact from timeline. If an investor bought before May 12, 2026, they're not affected. Properties bought after that date face restrictions on deductions—but not immediately eliminating negative gearing entirely. Investors can still claim losses against property income and capital gains; they just can't use them against regular wages.

NAB's cash earnings reached about $1.83 billion this quarter. Despite the fall in applications, the bank's overall loan book grew modestly. Total gross loans and acceptances reached $451.4 billion, up 1% from the previous quarter and 4% year-on-year. The bank's direct lending channel—where customers borrow straight from NAB rather than through brokers—grew to 50.9% of total lending, up from 47.7% earlier in the year.

This matters because direct channels are growing while broker channels decline. NAB is shifting its distribution model.

Credit quality metrics show early warning signs but nothing severe. The bank's "watch loans"—accounts flagged for monitoring—rose from 1.11% of the portfolio in March to 1.17% in June. Loans overdue by more than 90 days stayed at 1.01%, while loans 30 to 90 days overdue edged up to 1.63%. These rises are small but they're moving in the wrong direction.

The Reserve Bank's own data puts NAB's numbers in context. Housing credit growth remains strong across the banking system, not weak. Owner-occupier credit is growing at rates close to its long-term average. Investor credit has actually surged significantly. So one quarter of lower NAB applications doesn't mean the banking system is collapsing.

Housing supply is another part of the picture. NAB's own economic forecasts note that dwelling starts rose 34% since late 2023, with 235,000 homes under construction. Yet net additions to housing stock still lag population growth. Fewer applications might reflect weaker investor demand without fixing the underlying supply shortage.

NAB's housing outlook became more cautious. The bank is watching how interest rates, tax changes, and consumer uncertainty interact. But applications falling doesn't immediately predict house prices falling or the housing market crashing.

What this really shows: investors are reassessing, interest rates are biting, and the tax changes are influencing decisions right now—even before the rules fully take effect.
