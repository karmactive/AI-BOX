2 Dead, 230+ Rescued: Texas Hill Country Floods Again as Guadalupe River Rises 32 Feet in Four Hours

One year after the July 4, 2025 disaster that killed 119 people in Kerr County alone, the same rivers are flooding again. Governor Abbott expects the rainfall totals to exceed last year's catastrophe.

At 3 in the morning on July 16, 2026, the National Weather Service issued a flash flood emergency for Hunt, Kerrville, and Ingram in Kerr County, Texas — telling residents the situation was "a particularly dangerous situation" and urging them to "seek higher ground now." Three to six inches of rain had already fallen across central Kerr County. More was coming at two to four inches per hour. The Guadalupe River at Center Point had just risen 32 feet in four hours.

By Thursday morning, Kerrville — a city still bearing the wounds of the July 4, 2025 flood — ordered its residents to shelter in place. Flash flood emergencies were also declared for Uvalde County, and for the Guadalupe River corridor through Kerr and Kendall counties, and the Pedernales River through Blanco and Gillespie counties. A "large and deadly flood wave" was moving downstream from Kerrville through Center Point toward Comfort, Waring, Sisterdale, and Bergheim.

Two people were killed. More than 230 water rescues were completed. The state deployed 85 or more boats, 20 aircraft, 200 high-profile vehicles, and more than 2,350 emergency responders from over 30 agencies. Governor Greg Abbott declared a disaster for 59 Texas counties.

The area where the flooding hit hardest

The flooding was concentrated along three river systems in south-central Texas: the Guadalupe River through Kerr and Kendall counties, the Nueces River in Uvalde County, and the Pedernales River through Blanco and Gillespie counties. Forecasters described "especially critical" conditions along each of these corridors. In Uvalde, over 20 inches of rain fell in 48 hours — with 8 inches recorded in two hours at some locations. Some spots in Ingram and Kerrville received more than 20 inches between Monday and Thursday, per preliminary NWS data.

The Hunt area in Kerr County was cut off by floodwaters. Highway 39 in Kerr County was closed. Interstate 10 in the region was also affected. Texas Game Wardens alone participated in more than 40 rescues, pulling people from submerged vehicles and flooded homes. Aerial operations ran through the night and into the following day.

One year after 119 deaths: the same rivers, a different scale

The July 4–5, 2025 Guadalupe River flood was the deadliest in modern Texas Hill Country history. A sudden, catastrophic surge struck in the early hours of Independence Day. In Kerr County alone, the death toll reached at least 87 as recovery continued, and the final confirmed toll for the county climbed to 119. Across the broader Hill Country, 52 were dead and 27 missing within the first days, with the toll continuing to grow. The final regional toll reached at least 120 dead with 172 still missing when the state held its memorial. The disaster drew scrutiny over flood warning systems and the operation of summer camps along the Guadalupe.

Camp Mystic is not operating this summer

Camp Mystic — the girls' summer camp along the Guadalupe River in Hunt that became central to coverage of the July 2025 disaster, with 11 campers initially reported missing — filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on June 24, 2026. The camp is not operating this summer. Its property, located directly on the Guadalupe River, was not occupied during the July 2026 flooding.

After the 2025 disaster, Texas lawmakers passed Senate Bill 3 (SB3), which allocated approximately $50 million for flood warning sirens and emergency communication systems along flood-prone waterways. The July 2026 flooding marked the first major real-world test of those systems in the Kerr County area. Details on system performance were not yet publicly reported as of July 17.

Texas Governor Greg Abbott, who in 2025 honored the interstate support effort that mobilized resources from across the country, again coordinated a multi-agency response — this time with 2,350 state responders deployed before the flooding's peak.

Why this part of Texas floods repeatedly

Meteorologists and geologists refer to the Texas Hill Country — particularly the belt running from Uvalde County north and northeast through Kerr, Kendall, Blanco, and Gillespie counties — as "Flash Flood Alley." The geography is the reason. The terrain is built on thin soil over porous limestone bedrock. When intense rain falls, the ground cannot absorb it quickly enough. Water runs off immediately into the tributaries and rivers that drain this part of the Edwards Plateau.

Those rivers — the Guadalupe, Nueces, Pedernales, and their tributaries — can rise tens of feet in hours when enough rainfall hits their upper watersheds. The rivers are also relatively narrow and steep, channeling enormous volumes of water downstream quickly. A community 50 miles downstream can go from a dry riverbank to a life-threatening surge in under two hours, as Center Point demonstrated on the night of July 16.

The flooding is driven by natural weather patterns, not cloud seeding or other interventions — a point that Texas authorities have had to clarify publicly in both 2025 and 2026. The region sits at the intersection of Gulf moisture flows and the geographic funnel of the Hill Country terrain, making summer flash floods a recurring feature of the landscape rather than a random anomaly.

The 2025 flood began with an event that killed 27 in the first hours, with 24 camp girls reported missing. The 2026 event, beginning July 12 with the state already on alert, deployed resources four days before the most severe conditions arrived — a contrast in lead time that may have contributed to the lower immediate death toll, though the storm continues to develop and river levels are still high across much of the region as of July 17.
