# Scientists Investigate Possible Shotgun Attack on Critically Endangered Orca Named Toni

Toñi is one of roughly 49 Iberian orcas known to exist in the world. At approximately 60 years old, she is the oldest and most photographed individual of an extremely fragile population. On August 14-15, 2026, researchers from the WeWhale Association documented something shocking: approximately 10 holes in Toñi's dorsal fin, consistent with shotgun pellets. The discovery has triggered a criminal investigation by Spain's Environment Ministry—and raised urgent questions about what could threaten the last survivors of one of Earth's most endangered marine populations.

## A Rare Individual's Injuries

Toñi's significance extends beyond her individual existence. She is the grandmother of her population, the matriarch of her social group, and the most documented orca in the Iberian population. Researchers have tracked her movements, studied her behavior, and documented her survival through decades of human activity in the Strait of Gibraltar. Her identification markers—including her distinctive dorsal fin shape—make her instantly recognizable to the scientific community studying orca populations in European waters.

The injuries observed on August 14-15 represent a startling deviation from what researchers typically document. Rather than natural scarring or boat-strike injuries, the holes suggested projectile impacts. Ballistic analysis by the Atlantic Orca Working Group examined the wound pattern and concluded the evidence was consistent with 9mm buckshot—the type of ammunition used in bird and small-game hunting. This finding shifted the incident from an environmental hazard to a potential animal cruelty case.

## A Population on Extinction's Edge

The Iberian orca population, scientifically classified as *Orcinus orca*, consists of fewer than 50 individuals. The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) lists the population as Critically Endangered—the designation immediately below extinct in the wild. Historical records suggest the population once numbered in the hundreds; today, fewer than 50 remain. In the past two years alone, researchers documented an 80% decline in encounter rates, indicating the population is becoming increasingly rare.

The population's primary threat is nutritional: these orcas specialize almost exclusively in Atlantic bluefin tuna. As commercial fishing has dramatically depleted bluefin stocks, the orcas face starvation. A single injured individual in such a small population represents a significant loss. If Toñi dies from her injuries, the population loses not just numbers—it loses a matriarch whose knowledge of migration routes, feeding grounds, and survival strategies is critical to population cohesion.

## The Specialization Problem: Bluefin Tuna and Ecological Vulnerability

Iberian orcas' dependence on bluefin tuna is both their evolutionary success and their current crisis. Over generations, these orcas specialized in hunting a single prey species, developing sophisticated techniques for pursuing bluefin in the Strait of Gibraltar. This specialization made them extraordinarily skilled hunters—but it also eliminated their dietary flexibility. When bluefin stocks collapsed due to overfishing, the orcas had no alternative food source.

Climate change compounds the crisis. Warming Mediterranean waters have altered bluefin migration patterns and abundance. The combination of overfishing and climate-driven prey decline creates a squeeze on the population that no individual can escape. Even healthy, uninjured orcas struggle to find sufficient food. An injured matriarch faces significantly reduced survival prospects.

## Human Conflict in Shared Waters

The shooting incident raises questions about motive. Fishing industry representatives have documented incidents of orcas damaging boats, sometimes causing significant economic losses. Some fishermen view orcas as competitors for valuable bluefin stocks and as obstacles to profitable fishing operations. Speculation about motivation abounds, though no evidence has definitively identified who shot Toñi or why. The investigation remains ongoing.

What is clear is that human activity—whether fishing competition, recreational hunting, or deliberate killing—represents an additional threat layered on top of the ecological crisis. An already fragile population cannot absorb human-caused mortality without risking final collapse.

## Why Toñi Matters: The Science of Population Tracking

Toñi's research value extends the human concern beyond sentiment. Spain granted photo-identification research permits in 2026, allowing scientists to track individual orcas using distinctive markings. Toñi, as the oldest documented individual, represents invaluable knowledge about population history, behavioral patterns, and long-term survival strategies. Her loss would eliminate a crucial source of scientific data about how orcas adapt to changing conditions.

The research methodology itself—photo-ID tracking—relies on individuals remaining identifiable and alive. Each orca provides data points about population health, migration, reproduction, and response to environmental change. The death of a matriarch erases years of accumulated observational data and eliminates future data collection opportunities.

## Race to Prevent Extinction

Spain's Environment Ministry has initiated a criminal investigation into the shooting. Marine protected areas in the Strait of Gibraltar have been designated to reduce boat traffic and fishing pressure. Research initiatives continue to document population status and behavior. Yet realistic assessment suggests these measures may be insufficient. Population biologists studying Critically Endangered species often face grim timelines: species at population levels below 50 have historically shown extinction probabilities exceeding 90% within decades, even with intensive conservation effort.

The investigation into Toñi's injuries has sparked renewed calls for stronger marine protections, enforcement against illegal killing, and international coordination to protect the remaining population. Yet the fundamental crisis—the collapse of bluefin tuna stocks and climate-driven changes to marine ecosystems—remains largely unsolved.
