Every AI Prompt Has a Hidden Price. The UN Just Added It Up.

A June 2026 United Nations University report puts hard numbers to what AI is quietly drawing from the planet — water, land, electricity, and a trail of broken climate pledges.

🗓 June 11, 2026 🌍 Technology & Climate ⏱ 5 min read
Server racks fill a modern AI data center — each drawing over 100 kW of power, with water-intensive cooling systems running around the clock. As AI workloads multiply, so does the draw on grids that still run partly on coal. You have probably used AI today — a search, a summary, a drafted email. What you did not see was the water drawn from a cooling tower somewhere, the electricity pulled from a grid still partially powered by fossil fuels, and the land now occupied by the infrastructure that produced your answer. In June 2026, timed to World Environment Day, the United Nations University Institute for Water, Environment and Health (UNU-INWEH) published a report that converted this invisible cost into measurable figures — covering carbon, water, land, and waste together for the first time at scale.

Global data centers consumed 485 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2025, a 17% increase in a single year, according to the International Energy Agency. By 2030, that figure is projected to reach 945–950 TWh annually — nearly triple the combined electricity use of Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Nigeria, three countries home to more than 650 million people. Carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is already at record levels; AI infrastructure is now a significant and growing contributor to the demand that makes that harder to reverse.

Water is where the UNU report draws the sharpest lines. By 2030, AI-related water consumption is projected to equal the basic annual domestic water needs of all 1.3 billion people in Sub-Saharan Africa — 9.3 trillion liters. This includes water used directly in cooling towers, where heat is removed by evaporation, and the water used indirectly in the power plants that generate the electricity those cooling systems require. The two figures are often reported separately, and that gap in accounting is where most public misunderstandings take root.

At India's AI Impact Summit in February 2026, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman called water concerns around AI "fake," describing claims about per-query water use as "completely untrue, totally insane." What Altman has consistently emphasized is direct operational water use — a figure his team puts at around 0.3 milliliters per query. UC Riverside researchers calculated that generating a 100-word ChatGPT response consumes approximately 519 milliliters of water once electricity-generation water use is factored in. Both figures are technically accurate; they measure different parts of the same chain. The UNU report specifically argues that accounting for the full chain is what policy decisions require.

India faces this question with particular urgency. The country holds 18% of the world's population but only 4% of its freshwater. A study prepared for NITI Aayog, the Indian government's policy research body, found that AI data centers in the country could consume up to 37.5 billion liters of water annually. India's Union Minister for Electronics and IT, Ashwini Vaishnaw, acknowledged at the same summit that "requirement of power and water is a major concern" for the country's AI infrastructure. Separately, research published by the Asia Society Policy Institute warned of a pattern forming across South Asia: data center capacity being built in already water-stressed areas.

This is the rebound effect, and it dismantles one of the most common defences offered for AI's growing resource use. As models become cheaper, faster, and more accessible, usage increases — often faster than efficiency gains. The net demand on water, energy, and land grows even when each individual query becomes slightly leaner. As global ecosystems approach tipping points, this pattern has consequences that extend well beyond any single technology.

The emissions data from the world's largest tech companies makes the trend concrete. Google's total greenhouse gas emissions have jumped nearly 50% since it made its clean energy pledge, according to Fortune's March 2026 analysis of company sustainability reports. Amazon's rose 33%, Microsoft's more than 23%, and Meta's more than 60%. Google now describes its 2030 zero-emissions target as a "moonshot." Microsoft calls the effort "a marathon, not a sprint." The scale of data center energy demand by 2030 has made even well-funded commitments difficult to maintain on the original timelines.

One of the least-discussed dimensions of the UNU report is what it calls the bioenergy paradox — a trade-off that exposes the limits of treating carbon as the only measure of environmental cost. Switching a data center's electricity supply from coal to bioenergy cuts the carbon footprint by 70%. But it increases the water footprint more than 30-fold and the land footprint by a factor of 100. "Low-carbon" does not automatically mean "low-water" or "low-land." The report argues that governments and companies need to account for all three when evaluating infrastructure decisions — a standard that current voluntary reporting frameworks rarely require. Carbon accounting, even when done well, captures only part of the environmental picture.

AI infrastructure also generates a physical residue that scales with deployment. Server chips, circuit boards, cooling hardware, and battery backup systems have limited lifespans and are rarely recycled at the rates required to offset what's being produced. The UNU report projects AI-related electronic waste at up to 2.5 million tonnes annually by 2030. Indonesia, which recorded 170 operational data centers as of April 2026 and is on a rapid expansion path, is among the countries watching this problem accumulate fastest. Two-thirds of the 809 planned US AI data center projects are located in areas already classified as experiencing water stress, according to analysis cited in Tom's Hardware — meaning the resource draw and the scarcity are converging in the same geographies.

What Responsible AI Infrastructure Looks Like
🌊
Immersion cooling — servers submerged in non-conductive dielectric fluid rather than cooled by evaporating water. Reduces water consumption by up to 70%. Being deployed in select hyperscale facilities.
♻️
Data-water symbiosis — pairing data centers with municipal wastewater treatment plants for cooling. Modelled annual savings: 84 million tonnes of CO₂ and 1,300 million cubic meters of freshwater, with net cost savings of approximately US$95 billion.
🌡️
Waste heat recovery — a March 2026 European Commission analysis found data center waste heat can power water purification and carbon capture systems. Google already channels data center heat to warm homes in Finland.
☀️
Solar and wind-powered operations — carry zero operational water footprint. The US Department of Energy has outlined pathways for meeting data center demand entirely through clean sources.
📋
Mandatory disclosure — the UNU report recommends governments require AI companies to report water, land, and carbon footprints as a condition of operating. Most companies currently report carbon voluntarily and water rarely.

For most of the past decade, the environmental conversation about technology centered on devices — the carbon in a phone's manufacture, the energy to run a laptop. What the UNU-INWEH June 2026 report added to that picture is the infrastructure layer: the data centers, the cooling systems, the land they occupy, and the water they draw, often from the same regions where that water is already scarce. The report covered AI's carbon, water, land, and waste footprints together, attached projections to each, and proposed a governance framework — transparency, efficiency by design, equity, lifecycle responsibility, global cooperation, and sustainable use — specific enough to be written into regulation. The capabilities of AI continue to grow alongside its footprint. The numbers in the report put a figure to the cost that was, until recently, largely invisible to the people generating it one query at a time.
