**Operation Cremate Monsanto: How India's 1998 Farmer Uprising Exposed the True Cost of Patented Seeds**

In November 1998, farmers in Karnataka did not wait for a government hearing or a legal ruling. They found Monsanto's unauthorized field trials of genetically modified Bt cotton, pulled the plants from the ground, and set them on fire. What followed — a legal fight over corporate seed patents that continues to shape Indian agriculture — began in those burning fields.

Operation Cremate Monsanto was launched by the Karnataka Rajya Raitha Sangha (KRRS) under Prof. M.D. Nanjundaswamy. The campaign targeted Bt cotton field trials that India's Department of Biotechnology had approved without public announcement. State governments and host farmers had largely been kept in the dark, and activists argued the approvals were legally invalid. A minister named the trial locations publicly. Within days, every trial plant had been uprooted and burned. Despite the protests, India approved commercial Bt cotton cultivation years later. Genetically modified cotton now accounts for over 90 percent of India's cotton production. The Karnataka government subsequently filed suit against Monsanto over proprietary trait fees and won, securing price caps under the Essential Commodities Act.

For agricultural producers worldwide, India's experience shows that winning price caps on patented seed technology does not restore biological independence. Once multinational seed consolidations replace indigenous seed-saving networks with proprietary hybrids, growers lose the genetic diversity needed to adapt to changing climatic conditions. To avoid permanent reliance on corporate agrochemical inputs, agricultural communities are actively re-establishing localized seed banks to protect traditional heritage varieties before they face irreversible extinction.

**What the Legal Victory Did Not Restore**

Seed sovereignty advocates who tracked the direct actions and their aftermath described both the immediacy of the farmer response and the deeper historical break it represented: "Some people think the fight is already over, but the rest are mounting the resistance. In India, farmers didn't wait for a hearing. They found Monsanto's secret GMO cotton fields and burned them to the ground immediately. This was after the government initially kept them a secret. A minister named them, and it only took days for every single plant to be pulled up and set on fire."

The historical context behind the intensity of that response: "Seed had never been property before. For all of history, farmers saved seed from this year's harvest to plant next year and swapped it with neighbors to breed their own local varieties. But the 90s changed this with companies like Monsanto, who first bought into Mahyco, India's biggest seed company, while buying up many other seed firms worldwide."

On the legal outcome: "Even after the farmers burned the fields in protest, genetically modified cotton was approved just years later and now covers over 90% of the production. The silver lining was the state government sued Monsanto over charging a fee alongside this and won. This gave them cheaper seeds and stronger rights on paper, but they still lose control in the end because there's no way to get back the lost diversity."

That loss is the core lesson that extends beyond India. Capping the price of a patented input does not restore the open-pollinated alternatives that disappeared from commercial supply chains when proprietary hybrids took over. Indian farmers hold price regulation on Bt cotton seed but remain entirely dependent on annual hybrid purchases — the biological alternative was not regulated out, it was phased out. For anyone following [global seed sovereignty movements] and [India's agricultural biodiversity policy], the Karnataka case is why activists now treat decentralized seed banking as more strategically durable than litigation alone.
