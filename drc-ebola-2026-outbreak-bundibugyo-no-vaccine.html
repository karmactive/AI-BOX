Over 837 Infected, 196 Dead — DRC's Ebola 2026 Outbreak Has No Vaccine. The World Is Being Warned.

The rare Bundibugyo strain is spreading across DRC and Uganda. WHO declared a global health emergency. Africa's top health official warns this could surpass the 2014 West Africa epidemic. Here is everything that matters.

📅 June 19, 2026 🌍 Ituri Province, DRC ⚠️ WHO PHEIC Active ⏱ 6 min read

An Ebola outbreak triggered by the rare Bundibugyo ebolavirus has been spreading across the Democratic Republic of Congo and Uganda since May 2026, and by June 15, it had resulted in 837 confirmed cases and 196 deaths — figures the World Health Organization and Africa's Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describe as a rapidly escalating emergency. On May 17, 2026, WHO Director-General Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus declared the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, the same classification reserved for COVID-19 in 2020.

What separates this crisis from prior Ebola outbreaks is significant: unlike the Zaire ebolavirus strain — which now has approved vaccines and treatments — Bundibugyo has no licensed vaccine and no specific antiviral. The WHO has indicated that a vaccine candidate is at minimum nine months away. The outbreak began in Mongbwalu Health Zone in Ituri Province, northeastern DRC, a region already burdened by active armed conflict, mass displacement, and severe food insecurity. This is the 17th declared Ebola outbreak in DRC's recorded history, but the first caused by Bundibugyo at this scale. The Funding Crisis Underneath the Outbreak

The scale of this outbreak did not arrive without warning — it arrived amid a collapse of the global health architecture built to prevent exactly this. In 2024, the United States provided $906 million in humanitarian funding to DRC. By 2025, that dropped to $178.8 million. In 2026, approximately $232 million has been disbursed — roughly 25% of what was given two years earlier. The dissolution of USAID, deep staffing cuts at the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the US withdrawal from the World Health Organization directly weakened the early-warning infrastructure in the region. USAID had previously supported laboratory sample transport, health worker training, disease surveillance networks, and outbreak response logistics. When those programs were cut, detection was delayed.

On the ground, response organisations are operating in a dangerous gap. The International Council of Nurses issued a formal warning on June 3, 2026: DRC nurses are "scared for their safety" because of PPE and testing kit shortages. Médecins Sans Frontières (Doctors Without Borders) has deployed hundreds of staff across DRC and Uganda, opened treatment centres in Goma and in the South Kivu region, and worked alongside Alima's transparent 'Cube' isolation units — technology allowing clinicians to monitor and treat patients without direct physical contact. Health systems under strain rarely recover quickly once an outbreak gains momentum.

"If we don't stop the outbreak very soon, it will be worse than what we had in West Africa and eastern DRC." — Dr. Jean Kaseya, Director-General, Africa CDC · June 16, 2026

The US CDC's modelled scenario projections, published in the MMWR, estimate that without urgent intervention, the outbreak could reach up to 20,000 cases and 4,000 deaths within three months. For reference, the 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic — still the largest Ebola outbreak in history — resulted in 28,616 confirmed cases and 11,310 deaths across Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone. That outbreak was brought under control through an extraordinary combination of international coordination, community engagement, and a vaccine that did not yet exist at its start.

That vaccine option does not exist for Bundibugyo today. WHO confirmed in May 2026 that a viable candidate is at least nine months from deployment. Until then, response relies entirely on contact tracing, isolation, supportive care, and community cooperation — tools that require trust, which in eastern DRC remains in critically short supply amid years of conflict and health system failures. The CDC's MMWR projections document outlines what the next three months could look like across multiple response scenarios. Understanding the Bundibugyo Virus
What is the Bundibugyo virus — and why is it rare? +
Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) is one of six known species within the Ebolavirus genus. It is named after Bundibugyo District in western Uganda, where it was first identified in 2007. That outbreak recorded 149 suspected cases and 37 deaths — a case fatality rate of approximately 40%. A second outbreak followed in 2012 in DRC's Province Orientale, resulting in 59 cases and 34 deaths. Between 2012 and 2026, no further outbreaks were recorded, leading to limited research investment in vaccines and therapeutics specific to this strain. The 2026 outbreak is, by a large margin, the largest known Bundibugyo outbreak in history.
Why is there no vaccine or treatment? +
The approved Ebola vaccines — rVSV-ZEBOV (Ervebo) and Ad26.ZEBOV/MVA-BN-Filo (Zabdeno/Mvabea) — were developed specifically for the Zaire ebolavirus strain, which caused the devastating 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic and the 2018–2020 Kivu epidemic in DRC. The scientific and funding priority went to Zaire, given its higher historical death toll. Bundibugyo, being smaller in scale across prior outbreaks, received far less research attention. WHO has confirmed it will take at minimum nine months before a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine candidate could be ready. In the interim, treatment relies entirely on supportive care: fluid replacement, oxygen support, blood and cardiac monitoring, and nutritional support.
How does Bundibugyo spread? +
Bundibugyo virus spreads through direct contact with the blood, body fluids, secretions, organs, or other bodily materials of a person who is infected or has died from the disease. It is not airborne. Funeral and burial practices that involve touching the deceased have historically been major transmission events in Ebola outbreaks. The average incubation period in confirmed cases was 6.3 days, though symptoms can appear anywhere from 2 to 21 days after exposure. A person is not infectious during the incubation period — only once symptoms develop. Healthcare workers are at elevated risk due to close patient contact, particularly when PPE is inadequate.
What is the case fatality rate — and why does it vary? +
Based on the two prior Bundibugyo outbreaks, case fatality rates ranged from 25% to 50%. In the current 2026 outbreak, the confirmed fatality rate stands at approximately 23.4% as of June 15 — lower than historical peaks, but this may reflect reporting lags and the large number of active hospitalisations. Early supportive care significantly improves survival chances. The wide historical range reflects differences in healthcare access, outbreak detection speed, supportive care availability, and the proportion of cases in healthcare settings vs. community settings. According to ECDC's threat assessment, the current response capacity is significantly below what would be required to contain the outbreak at its present trajectory.
