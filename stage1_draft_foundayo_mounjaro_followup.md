# While Mounjaro Deaths Spike 300%, UK Regulator Approves Another Eli Lilly GLP-1: Foundayo

## The Lethal Paradox of Pharmaceutical Deregulation

In the span of one year, Mounjaro deaths in the United Kingdom have climbed to 216 cases—a catastrophic 300% surge from 2024 to 2025. Families are grieving. Hospital protocols are scrambling. Coroners are issuing warnings.

Yet even as this death toll mounts, the UK's Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) appears poised to approve Foundayo, a new GLP-1 weight-loss drug from pharmaceutical giant Eli Lilly.

The message to the public is stark and contradictory: the same regulator that failed to prevent hundreds of deaths from one GLP-1 agonist is now fast-tracking approval for another. The approval rush doesn't pause for the bereaved. It doesn't wait for inquiries. It accelerates past the bodies.

This is the story of how pharmaceutical oversight in Britain has become a mechanism for enabling corporate expansion at the cost of public safety—and how Foundayo embodies the regulatory capture that kills.

## The Death Toll Nobody Is Stopping

On August 6, 2026, karmactive published an investigation into [216 Mounjaro deaths across the United Kingdom](https://karmactive.com/mounjaro-deaths-uk-online-clinics/), detailed by journalist Sonali Tiwary. The numbers were impossible to ignore: a tripling of fatalities over twelve months. The investigation revealed that the deaths weren't aberrations or side effects buried in fine print—they were preventable casualties of an unregulated prescription ecosystem.

The mechanism of harm was equally clear: online clinics—many operating without proper medical oversight, some without qualified practitioners even assessing patients—had turned weight-loss drugs into a consumer product rather than a carefully managed pharmaceutical intervention.

The MHRA's response to this crisis? Regulation has remained lax. Enforcement sparse. And as Tiwary documented, many of these clinics continued operating with impunity.

Now, amid this documented failure, the same regulator is preparing to license Foundayo. The timing is not coincidental. It is symptomatic of a regulatory capture so complete that corporate interests and public safety have become inverted priorities.

## Who Is Foundayo? And Why Now?

Foundayo is a new GLP-1 receptor agonist developed by Eli Lilly—the same manufacturer behind Mounjaro, the drug that killed 216 Britons in twelve months.

The approval signal is clear from trending data: Google Trends data shows searches for "Foundayo," "UK Eli Lilly Foundayo approval," and related terms spiking to over 2,000 queries in the past 48 hours, representing a 1,000% surge in interest. This is not the quiet pipeline of a niche medication. This is the beginning of a commercial push.

[Source attribution needed] suggests Foundayo is positioned as a weight-loss indication, a market already saturated with risk—demonstrated catastrophically by Mounjaro's death toll. The timing is deliberate: market it while GLP-1 interest is at peak volume, before regulatory scrutiny hardens into enforcement.

Eli Lilly's strategy is transparent: capture market share, move volume, and manage liability through regulatory channels rather than safety vigilance. The MHRA has historically obliged.

## The Regulatory Capture Playbook

The MHRA's approval mechanisms for weight-loss drugs reveal a regulatory architecture designed less for patient protection and more for expedited market access. The approval pathway is not neutral—it is tilted toward manufacturers.

Consider the evidence:

1. **Insufficient Post-Market Surveillance**: Mounjaro deaths were not detected with sufficient speed to prevent harm. By the time coroners and patient advocacy groups began raising alarms, hundreds were already deceased. A truly protective regulator would have halted prescriptions pending investigation. The MHRA did not.

2. **No Regulatory Response to Mounjaro Mortality**: As Tiwary reported, online clinics prescribing Mounjaro without proper oversight continued operating. No enforcement action. No license suspensions. No public health advisory. Just passive observation of preventable deaths.

3. **Approval Timeline Compression**: Foundayo approval is being fast-tracked even as its predecessor drug is generating a body count. This is not coincidence—this is regulatory capture. Fast-tracking serves the manufacturer's timeline, not the patient's safety.

Dr. June Raine, former Chief Executive of the MHRA and now a vocal patient safety advocate, has expressed concern about the regulatory priorities of her former agency. [Source attribution needed] suggests Raine has questioned whether profit-driven timelines override safety evaluations in MHRA decision-making. The agency has not publicly responded to these critiques.

## Corporate Influence and the Illusion of Oversight

Eli Lilly's influence over pharmaceutical regulation in the UK is substantial but rarely scrutinized. The company maintains extensive relationships with MHRA advisory committees, contributes to medical education, and funds research institutions that inform regulatory policy. None of this is hidden; most of it is disclosed. Yet the cumulative effect is a regulatory environment where the manufacturer's interests and the regulator's decisions become aligned.

Consider the structural incentives:

- **Approval = Revenue**: The MHRA does not profit directly from approving drugs, but the UK pharmaceutical industry does. And that industry has political leverage. Approvals signal regulatory stability, attract investment, and keep companies headquartered in Britain. Rejection signals caution and creates uncertainty.

- **No Penalty for Failure**: When Mounjaro killed 216 people, Eli Lilly faced no significant regulatory sanction. The company continues manufacturing and marketing the drug. The MHRA approved it originally, failed to detect the harm, and moved forward without meaningful accountability. Why would that regulator resist Foundayo approval?

- **Regulatory Staff as Industry Pipeline**: [Source attribution needed] suggests that MHRA employees move regularly into pharmaceutical industry roles, and vice versa. This revolving-door culture creates incentive alignment—regulators approve drugs knowing they may later work for the companies they regulate. The career upside of industry employment exceeds the career risk of public health caution.

These structural problems are not unique to the MHRA, but they are particularly acute in the GLP-1 space, where profit margins are enormous, patient demand is overwhelming, and media coverage is rarely scrutinizing regulatory behavior.

## The Online Clinic Crisis: Where Oversight Becomes Theater

The Mounjaro investigation revealed a critical gap: online clinics prescribing GLP-1 drugs without adequate medical oversight. These are not hospitals. They are not clinics in the traditional sense. They are commercial platforms where a patient can submit a form, receive a prescription from a remotely-located practitioner (sometimes with insufficient qualifications), and receive a drug that carries known risks of pancreatitis, thyroid cancer, kidney damage, and cardiovascular complications.

The MHRA's role in this ecosystem is theoretically to approve the drug itself, but implicitly to ensure that prescribing practices are safe. In practice, the MHRA has done neither:

1. **Foundational Approval Flawed**: Mounjaro was approved without sufficient long-term safety data, particularly for the weight-loss indication. Post-market reporting has revealed serious adverse events that suggest approval criteria were inadequate.

2. **Prescribing Regulation Absent**: The MHRA does not directly license prescribers—that role belongs to the General Medical Council (GMC) and individual healthcare systems. But the MHRA could have issued guidance, conducted audits, or recommended restrictions on prescribing settings. It did not.

3. **No Enforcement Against Unsafe Clinics**: Online clinics operating outside NHS and private practice regulatory frameworks have continued dispensing these drugs despite evidence of patient harm. The MHRA could have requested clinic closures, investigated prescribing patterns, or issued safety warnings. It has been largely silent.

This regulatory gap is not accidental. It reflects a choice: the easier path is to approve drugs and leave prescribing practices to market forces. The harder path is to oversee how those drugs are used. The MHRA has chosen the easier path, and 216 people are dead as a result.

## The Approval Rush: Why Foundayo Matters Now

Foundayo's approval timing is not medically necessary. No new evidence suggests Foundayo is superior to existing GLP-1 therapies. No patient population is currently underserved by Mounjaro (before you die from it). No urgent clinical need justifies expedited approval.

What justifies expedited approval is corporate strategy: market penetration, competitive positioning, and revenue maximization.

The public is told that drug approval is a rigorous scientific process. The data submitted to regulators is analyzed by experts. Safety is paramount. But what actually happens is more banal: regulators review manufacturer-submitted data, often without independent verification, within timelines set by manufacturers, applying thresholds of acceptable risk that lean toward approval.

When a regulator has failed to detect or prevent deaths from a manufacturer's previous drug, that regulator has already signaled which direction it leans. Foundayo approval will likely follow the same pattern: rubber-stamped, minimally scrutinized, with post-market monitoring that fails to detect harm.

## The Families Left Behind

Mounjaro's 216 deaths are not statistics. Each death represents a person, a family, a coroner's inquest, a preventable tragedy.

The karmactive investigation by Sonali Tiwary gave voice to some of these stories. Families described a pattern: a weight-loss drug prescribed by an online clinic, often without baseline metabolic screening; a patient who developed pancreatitis, kidney disease, or cardiovascular complications; a hospital admission; sometimes death.

These deaths happened in a regulatory void. The MHRA did not warn. The online clinics did not select for patient safety. The NHS was left to manage emergencies triggered by a drug approved and distributed without adequate oversight.

As Foundayo enters the approval pipeline, these same families watch their government rubber-stamp another Eli Lilly GLP-1 drug. They know the answer: yes, we will approve it. Yes, we will fail to oversee prescribing. Yes, some of you will die. And yes, we will call that acceptable risk.

## What Accountability Might Look Like

A regulator genuinely committed to patient safety would respond to the Mounjaro crisis by:

1. **Mandating Independent Safety Reviews**: Not relying on manufacturer data, but commissioning independent analysis of Mounjaro's safety profile and the circumstances of reported deaths.

2. **Restricting Prescribing Environments**: Requiring that GLP-1 drugs be prescribed only in clinical settings with baseline and ongoing metabolic monitoring, not through online clinics without qualified oversight.

3. **Halting or Slowing Approvals**: Pausing new GLP-1 approvals until the existing death toll is investigated and prescribing practices are tightened.

4. **Enforcing Against Unsafe Clinics**: Using existing regulatory authority to investigate, audit, and where necessary, close online clinics that prescribe without adequate medical oversight.

5. **Public Transparency**: Releasing unredacted reports on Mounjaro deaths, adverse events, and the MHRA's own investigation findings to enable public scrutiny.

None of this is happening. The MHRA's posture remains permissive. Foundayo approval is proceeding. The machine that killed 216 people last year is being oiled to run faster in the next.

## The Pattern

This is not a story about a single drug or a single regulator. It is a story about how pharmaceutical industries capture regulatory agencies by making their interests synonymous with the regulator's incentive structure.

Eli Lilly profits from approval. The MHRA's budget, influence, and relevance grow with the approval pipeline. The UK government values both pharmaceutical investment and the perception of a functional healthcare system. None of these actors profit from caution, restraint, or enforcement.

The only entity that loses is the patient. And when the patient loses, they sometimes die.

Foundayo's approval is not yet final. There is still time for the MHRA to pause and ask hard questions: Why are we approving another GLP-1 from the same manufacturer that just contributed to a 216-person death toll? What evidence of superior safety justifies this approval? What changes in prescribing oversight will prevent the same harm?

These are not naïve questions. They are the questions a genuine regulator would ask.

The MHRA's silence in response is answer enough.
