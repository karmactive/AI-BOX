Before June has arrived, 2026 has already become the worst wildfire year in recorded history. Between January and April, more than 150 million hectares of land burned across the globe — an area roughly the size of Iran — according to data released by the World Meteorological Organization and the World Weather Attribution network on May 11, 2026. That figure is 50% above the 2012–2025 average for the same months, double what burned in 2024, and 20% above the previous record set in 2020.

And it may get worse. A developing El Niño — expected to emerge from May 2026 — raises what scientists are calling a "serious risk" of unprecedented fire conditions later this year. The fires are not confined to one country or one forest type. Africa is burning at its highest rate since satellite records began. Asian peatlands are on fire during what should be rainy season. Smoke from these fires is crossing continents. Last year, WMO documented how wildfire pollution was already affecting 900 million people in South Asia. The 2026 numbers put that in an entirely new context.

Africa: Highest Fire Area Since Satellite Records Began
Africa accounts for the single largest burned area of any continent in 2026 — 85 million hectares, the highest since satellite monitoring began in 2012, and 23% above the previous record of 69 million hectares. The fires spread across 16 countries in the Sahel and sub-Saharan region. Many of the affected countries have limited fire-fighting infrastructure, and the health burden falls disproportionately on rural and farming communities with no means of evacuation.

Asia: Peatland Fires During Rainy Season Signal a New Threshold
Asia burned 44 million hectares through April 2026 — 40% more than the previous regional record of 32 million hectares set in 2014. India, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and China were among the worst-hit countries. In Indonesia, more than 5,000 fire hotspots were detected across peatlands in January 2026, during what is normally peak rainy season. Peatlands store carbon accumulated over thousands of years. When they burn, that carbon is released in days — making these fires qualitatively different from forest fires in their climate impact. The FAO has launched a dedicated peatland fire measurement project in Southeast Asia, where approximately 50 million people are directly exposed to toxic smoke.

Americas: Southern Hemisphere Opens 2026 With Record Carbon Emissions
In the Americas, January 2026 brought record fire conditions to the Southern Hemisphere. Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service data showed Chile's wildfire carbon emissions in January were the highest since 2017 according to the Global Fire Assimilation System (GFAS) dataset. In Argentina, fires in the Chubut province reached their highest intensity since 2003, when CAMS satellite monitoring began. Bolivia had recorded its highest CO₂ emissions total of the century from fires in the prior year. The economic cost of large-scale fire seasons in this region is significant — Indonesia's 2015 fire event alone caused US$28 billion in losses; six major fire events between 2004 and 2015 totalled US$93.9 billion globally.

What separates 2026 from previous fire years is not just volume — it is where fires are burning. Tropical peatlands, which store carbon accumulated across millennia, are now burning at levels not seen in at least 2,000 years according to research published in ScienceDaily in March 2026. When peat burns, that ancient carbon enters the atmosphere in days. There is no mechanism to replace it on any human timescale.

A 2026 paper in Nature Communications calculated that permafrost thaw and wildfire emissions combined are reducing the remaining carbon budget for limiting warming to 1.5°C by 25%. That is not a projection — it is the arithmetic of what has already been lost.

There is also a feedback loop that rarely gets attention: wildfire smoke reduces the atmosphere's capacity to break down methane, a greenhouse gas more than 80 times more potent than CO₂ over 20 years. Research published in Nature Geoscience in 2026 confirmed this atmospheric chemistry effect, meaning fires warm the climate, the warmer climate produces more fires, and the smoke from those fires further amplifies warming through methane accumulation.

The extreme heat already gripping India in 2026 — where all 50 of the world's hottest cities were located on a single April day — is part of the same climate pattern feeding the fire season across Asia.

What El Niño Does to Fire Season

The World Meteorological Organization expects El Niño conditions to emerge from May 2026, bringing increased likelihood of drought, heatwaves, and further wildfire outbreaks globally.
Global sea surface temperatures set a new daily record in mid-April 2026, with March 2026 measured as the second-warmest March on ocean record at 21.04°C average — feeding drier conditions on land.
Friederike Otto, co-founder of World Weather Attribution: "El Niño is a natural phenomenon that comes and goes. What makes it so dramatic is not the event itself but that it is happening in a dramatically changing climate."
A strong El Niño can push peatlands in tropical locations — which can burn for months on end — into unprecedented fire conditions that expose tens of millions of people to prolonged smoke pollution.

What Wildfire Smoke Does to the Body

The health data — and who is most at risk when fire season reaches this scale

💔
99,000+
Deaths per year linked to wildfire smoke
Up from 69,000/year in the 1990s. Cardiovascular deaths from smoke almost doubled — from 17,000 to 30,000 annually over the same period, per research published in Nature npj Clean Air and the NIH.
🫁
PM2.5
The particle that enters your bloodstream
Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) from wildfire smoke penetrates deep into the lungs and enters the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation. The American Lung Association links it to asthma, COPD, and cardiovascular events.
⚠️
6 groups
At highest risk from smoke exposure
Per the US EPA: children, older adults, pregnant women, people with asthma, COPD, heart disease, and those with diabetes face disproportionate risk even at moderate smoke levels.

The economic dimension of this fire season is rarely discussed but substantial. Indonesia's 2015 fire event — comparable to what parts of Asia are already experiencing in 2026 — caused US$28 billion in economic losses. The six largest fire events in Indonesia between 2004 and 2015 totalled US$93.9 billion, according to research in Nature Communications. No equivalent estimates have been published yet for the 2026 global totals.

Smoke does not stay where fires start. Fires in the Sahel and in Southeast Asia have historically pushed smoke plumes across oceans, reaching populations thousands of miles away. The transboundary dimension means that a fire burning in Central Africa or in Indonesian peatlands contributes to air quality emergencies in cities where residents have no connection to the fire's source. Ecosystems already under pressure from multiple threats face compounding stress when fires sweep through habitats that are already degraded.

Real-time satellite data tracking active fires globally is publicly available through NASA FIRMS and Copernicus CAMS Global Fire Monitoring. Both platforms update within three hours of satellite detection.

What You Can Do When Smoke Reaches Your Area

Practical steps — especially for those in high-risk groups or near fire zones

1
Check air quality before going outsideTrack the Air Quality Index (AQI) daily. Limit outdoor activity when AQI exceeds 100, and avoid it entirely above 150.
2
Use an N95 mask, not a cloth maskCloth masks do not filter fine PM2.5 particles. N95 or KN95 respirators offer meaningful protection during smoke events.
3
Seal your indoor airClose windows and doors during smoky periods. Run an air purifier with a HEPA filter. Avoid burning candles or using gas stoves, which add to indoor pollution.
4
Children, elderly, and those with conditions: stay indoorsIf you or someone you care for has asthma, COPD, diabetes, or heart disease, treat any AQI above 50 as a reason to stay inside.
5
Monitor NASA FIRMS for real-time fire locationsThe NASA FIRMS fire map updates within 3 hours of satellite detection. Useful for anyone in a fire-adjacent region.
6
Have a go-bag ready in fire-risk areasDocuments, medications (especially inhalers), water, and a charged phone. Wildfires can spread faster than evacuation orders can be issued.
