**Elaboration of the suggestions points**

1. Two storms already this season. NOAA's August update states the 2026 Atlantic season has produced two named tropical storms so far — Arthur and Bertha — and no hurricanes. Tropical Storm Arthur was the first named storm of the season. This counters the impression the basin is dormant. Source: NOAA "maintains prediction for below-normal Atlantic hurricane season" (noaa.gov news release). Reader relevance: scanning "three areas of interest" alone can suggest "nothing has happened" — the accurate frame is "two storms already, more possible, season overall below normal."

2. NOAA below-normal numbers. The May 2026 outlook called for 8–14 named storms (winds 39 mph+), 3–6 hurricanes (74 mph+), 1–3 major (Category 3–5, 111 mph+); an average season has 14 named, 7 hurricanes, 3 major. The August update held the below-normal call. Colorado State University's August outlook called for 9 named storms, 4 hurricanes, 1 major. Source: NOAA news releases; CSU via FOX Weather. These are the figures to anchor "below normal."

3. El Nino and wind shear, simply. El Nino is a warming of the central/eastern Pacific that shifts global wind patterns. Over the Atlantic it strengthens high-altitude winds (wind shear) that tilt and shred developing storms. The NHC TWO explicitly cites "upper-level winds expected to become less conducive" for the central Atlantic wave near the Lesser Antilles — that is the El Nino effect in practice.

4. Saharan Air Layer. The Cabo Verde low sits downstream of the Sahara. NOAA AOML describes the SAL as a mass of very dry, dusty air that forms over the Sahara in late spring through early fall and moves over the tropical Atlantic every three to five days. Three traits suppress storms: (a) extremely dry air — about 50% less moisture than the typical tropical atmosphere — which weakens a system via downdrafts; (b) the African Easterly Jet, strong winds of 25–55 mph (10–25 m/s) that increase vertical wind shear and tilt the storm's column; (c) warm, stable air that suppresses cloud formation. SAL activity ramps up mid-June, peaks late June to mid-August, and begins to subside after mid-August. So the Cabo Verde low is trying to organize just as dust normally starts easing — but the NHC notes it "will have to navigate bouts of Saharan Air." Source: NOAA AOML; NASA Earthdata.

5. Climatological peak. The peak of the Atlantic hurricane season is September 10, with most activity between mid-August and mid-October (NOAA Tropical Cyclone Climatology). This explains why "three areas of interest" in early August is normal seasonal behavior, not an anomaly, and why the window ahead is the busiest of the year.

6. Exact NHC percentages (Aug 10 TWO): Central Tropical Atlantic wave — 48h 10%, 7-day 10%. Eastern/Central Atlantic low south of Cabo Verde — 48h 20%, 7-day 60%. Western subtropical low NW of Bermuda — 48h 10%, 7-day 10%. No coastal watches or warnings; no land threat per NHC and all local sources.

7. Name order. WMO 2026 list: Arthur, Bertha, Cristobal, Dolly, Edouard, Fay… Arthur and Bertha already used; Cristobal and Dolly are next. Confirms local reporting that those are the upcoming names.

8. Preparedness. With no immediate threat, the calm window is the time to confirm a household hurricane plan: know your evacuation zone, check supplies, sign up for local alerts. This is helpful and trustworthy without forecasting future storms.

9. Central Pacific note (optional). A separate system (CP93) about 1,100 miles ESE of Hawaii carries 50% (48h) / 90% (7-day) formation odds (NHC Central Pacific). Out of Atlantic scope; include only if broadening the basin-wide view.
