# STAGE 2 — DRAFT (TRIAL)

## Three Atlantic disturbances are being watched, but the season is already ahead of them

The National Hurricane Center is tracking three separate areas of interest across the Atlantic basin, yet none poses a threat to land right now. The broader 2026 season has already been busier than the headlines suggest.

Two named storms have already formed this year. Tropical Storm Arthur was the first named storm of the Atlantic season, followed by Tropical Storm Bertha. No hurricanes have developed so far. The center's 200 AM EDT outlook on Monday, August 10, listed three active disturbances: a tropical wave in the central Atlantic, a small low-pressure system south of the Cabo Verde Islands, and a trough northwest of Bermuda.

The system worth watching sits a few hundred miles south of the Cabo Verde Islands. A tropical wave over West Africa is expected to merge with it within a couple of days. Environmental conditions are forecast to turn more favorable by midweek. The center puts its chance of becoming a tropical depression at 20 percent through 48 hours and 60 percent through seven days. If it organizes, the next name on the 2026 list is Cristobal, then Dolly.

"The Cabo Verde system is the more likely to develop," said Bryan Norcross, hurricane specialist at FOX Weather, on August 9. "There's no indication that it will be a threat to land."

The other two are long shots. The central Atlantic wave, positioned midway between West Africa and the Lesser Antilles, has a 10 percent formation chance at both 48 hours and seven days. It is moving quickly westward but is expected to run into hostile upper-level winds as it nears the islands. The Bermuda-area trough also carries a 10 percent chance and is forecast to reach cooler waters later this week, ending its formation window after Wednesday.

What the regional weather desks left out is the season's larger shape. NOAA maintains a below-normal forecast for 2026: 8 to 14 named storms, 3 to 6 hurricanes, and 1 to 3 major hurricanes. An average season brings 14 named storms, 7 hurricanes, and 3 major hurricanes. Colorado State University's August outlook called for 9 named storms, 4 hurricanes, and 1 major.

The reason for the quieter call is El Nino. The warming of the central and eastern Pacific strengthens high-altitude winds over the Atlantic. These winds, known as shear, tilt and tear apart storms that try to form. That is the same mechanism the center cites for the central Atlantic wave falling apart near the Lesser Antilles.

Dry Saharan dust adds another hurdle. The Saharan Air Layer is a mass of very dry, dusty air that moves off Africa every three to five days. NOAA research shows it carries about 50 percent less moisture than the typical tropical atmosphere, plus strong winds of 25 to 55 mph that increase shear. The Cabo Verde low must cross these dust outbreaks as it tries to organize. The dust season normally peaks in late June to mid-August and begins to ease after that.

The Atlantic's busiest stretch is still ahead. The climatological peak of the season falls on September 10, with most activity between mid-August and mid-October.

No tropical cyclones are in the Atlantic, Caribbean, or Gulf of America at this time, and no coastal watches or warnings are in effect. Forecasters note that long-range odds for systems that have not yet developed remain uncertain.

The calm window is the time to confirm a household plan. Know your evacuation zone, check supplies, and sign up for local alerts. Preparation done now matters more than worry later.
