# ARTICLE 1: USDA RECALLS NEARLY 30,000 POUNDS OF ARGENTINE BEEF IN TEXAS AND FLORIDA

Nearly 30,000 pounds of raw beef imported from Argentina has been recalled by the U.S. Department of Agriculture after the products entered the country without the required FSIS import reinspection. The meat, distributed to retailers and distributors in Texas and Florida, came from Corte Argentino USA LLC, a Florida-based importer.

The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service issued the recall on August 7, 2026, after discovering the shipment from Frigorífico Gorina SAIC in Argentina was imported without required reinspection procedures. The recall covers 29,628 pounds of raw boneless beef cuts produced between May 15 and May 20, 2026.

**What consumers need to check:**

The affected packages carry establishment code EST. N° OF. 2025 and shipping mark 26644-AA. Look for these identifiers on the packaging if you purchased Argentine beef recently. The meat has use-or-freeze-by dates between September 15 and September 20, 2026. Five specific cuts are included: Top Sirloin Butt (Cuadril Sin Tapa), Eye Round (Peceto), Topside Cap Off (Nalga AD S/Tapa), Flat (Carnaza Cuadrada), and Knuckle (Bola de Lomo).

Corte Argentino supplies both retail grocery stores and commercial food service operations like restaurants, hotels, and catering companies. This means the meat may have reached more than just home kitchens—it could be in restaurant kitchens and commercial freezers.

**What import reinspection means:**

When meat enters the United States from another country, federal law requires it to undergo verification of import requirements at approved FSIS facilities. This verification covers documentation, product labeling, and confirms the product meets U.S. import specifications. The beef in this recall was imported without that verification step, which is why the USDA classified it as a Class I recall.

**Understanding the Class I classification:**

A Class I recall is the highest-priority recall category. However, this classification is not automatically assigned to all procedural violations. FSIS determines the classification based on whether there is a reasonable probability that consumption of the product could cause serious adverse health consequences.

**What to do if you have the recalled beef:**

Discard it or return it to the store where purchased. There are no confirmed illnesses linked to this recall. The USDA did not identify specific contaminants in the shipment. The recall notice identifies the problem as the failure to undergo required import reinspection.

This recall underscores how imported meat moves through U.S. supply chains and why verification requirements exist. Most consumers don't see the regulatory steps that protect their food. This case shows what happens when those steps are missed.

# ARTICLE 2: FEDERAL JUDGE BLOCKS OHIO PROOF-OF-CITIZENSHIP VOTING REQUIREMENT

A federal judge has blocked Ohio's requirement that voters present documentary proof of U.S. citizenship when registering at motor vehicle offices, ruling the state's rule conflicts with federal voting law. The decision comes just over two months before the 2026 midterm elections.

U.S. District Judge Solomon Oliver Jr. of the Northern District of Ohio issued a preliminary injunction on August 25, 2026, stopping enforcement of Ohio's documentary citizenship proof requirement at Bureau of Motor Vehicles facilities. The order addresses two distinct legal problems with Ohio's registration process.

Ohio's law required people registering to vote through motor vehicle offices to provide documentary proof they are U.S. citizens. The federal National Voter Registration Act of 1993, known as the Motor Voter law, allows states to require only that voters sign a statement under penalty of perjury attesting to their citizenship. Federal law says states cannot demand more documentation at the point of registration.

**The two legal grounds for the ruling:**

Judge Oliver found Ohio violated federal law on two separate grounds. First, the state's documentary-proof requirement exceeded what federal law permits. Second, Ohio improperly conditioned the voter registration opportunity on approval of the driver's license application itself. The Motor Voter law requires that registration be offered at the initial application stage, not after BMV approval.

The judge noted that Ohio's own online voter registration system did not require document uploads, creating an inconsistent standard across registration methods. This arbitrary difference in process persuaded the court to issue the injunction blocking the BMV requirement.

**What changed for Ohio voters:**

The injunction blocks enforcement of Ohio's documentary-proof requirement at BMV locations. Ohio has announced that it will immediately appeal the ruling. Secretary of State Frank LaRose issued a statement: "We strongly disagree with that decision, and I've asked our legal team to file an immediate appeal."

**Understanding the legal issue:**

This ruling does not mean noncitizens can vote in Ohio. Federal law and Ohio law both require voters to be U.S. citizens. The court's order concerns only the method of verification at specific registration locations. The injunction addresses documentary proof requirements, not citizenship eligibility itself.

Think of it this way: the requirement that voters be citizens stays in place. The question the judge answered was whether Ohio can force people to show specific documents to prove that eligibility at a motor vehicles office, given that federal law says signed statements under penalty of perjury are sufficient.

**What happens next:**

The preliminary injunction remains in effect unless it is stayed, modified or reversed. Ohio has stated it will appeal the decision. The case adds to ongoing litigation nationwide about voter registration procedures, with similar challenges underway in other states.

The ruling affects only BMV registration. Ohio voters can still register through other methods and must still meet citizenship requirements to vote. The dispute centers on what documentation states can require at a specific registration point and when registration opportunities must be offered.

# ARTICLE 3: 2,400-YEAR-OLD SHIPWRECKS REVEAL ANCIENT CARGO IN MEDITERRANEAN DEPTHS

Two ancient shipwrecks discovered in the Mediterranean Sea are giving archaeologists rare glimpses into how people traded goods thousands of years ago. One wreck off Greece held cargo from around 400 BCE, while another off Sicily dates to the Roman era.

The Greek wreck lies 130 to 145 feet deep in waters off Andros Island in the Cyclades. Archaeologists from Greece's Ministry of Culture found approximately 600 to 650 ceramic vessels still sitting on the seabed. These containers, called amphorae, were designed specifically to carry liquids like wine or oil on long sea voyages. The pointed bottoms allowed them to nestle securely in ship holds without rolling.

Three distinct types of amphorae rested in this cargo, each made in different parts of the ancient world. Mende amphorae came from the Chalcidice peninsula. Peparethos vessels were produced on Skopelos Island. Solokha 2 amphorae are associated with the wider Black Sea archaeological sphere, providing clues to the broader trading networks represented by the cargo.

The cargo composition suggests this single merchant ship was consolidating goods from multiple sources for delivery to distant markets. Archaeologists also recovered fragments of a lead anchor stock. Ballast stones that anchored the vessel in the water were identified as well. These details help date the wreck. The style of the amphorae places the cargo in the Late Classical period, roughly 2,400 years ago.

Off Sicily's southern coast, the Italian Regional Superintendency of the Sea documented a Roman-era wreck at about 150 feet deep. This ship carried hundreds of Dressel 1A and Lamboglia 2 wine amphorae, along with North African and Punic oil jars. Lead anchor stocks rested beside the cargo.

Modern underwater archaeology uses technologies like 3D photogrammetry and high-resolution acoustic mapping to study wrecks. Researchers photograph every detail from multiple angles, creating three-dimensional digital models. Sonar mapping creates detailed maps of cargo arrangement and vessel structure.

This non-destructive approach differs sharply from salvage operations that rip artifacts from the seabed. Mediterranean nations, including Greece and Italy, maintain strict ownership of underwater antiquities. The discoveries were reported to the relevant cultural authorities for documentation and protection.

The preserved cargo reveals what mattered to ancient merchants—wine, oil, and the containers that protected them during weeks at sea. Understanding how ancient people conducted commerce helps historians grasp how civilizations developed, what they valued, and how connected they were to distant regions. These underwater snapshots preserve that history in ways that few other sources can.

The court determined questions about whether the ride's design caused her injury could only be resolved through full litigation.

The case established that injuries and design concerns with X2 have been subject to litigation before.
