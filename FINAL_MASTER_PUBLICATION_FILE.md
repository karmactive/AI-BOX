This isn't a guarantee of disaster. The Met Office emphasizes that El Niño changes the odds—it shifts probabilities—but no two El Niños are identical. Some autumns stay relatively dry despite the warning signs. However, households and businesses should treat this as a planning signal. Home and business owners in flood-prone areas should verify drainage systems, ensure gutters are clear, check non-return valves on drainage pipes, and review insurance coverage for water damage.

The agricultural sector faces its own considerations. Waterlogged soil conditions in autumn can delay planting schedules and complicate field operations. Supply chains for winter crops may be affected if autumn weather patterns align with the forecasts.

If this El Niño pattern strengthens as forecast, reduced monsoon rainfall could stress agricultural regions across South Asia, affecting commodity markets for global grain and pulse prices heading toward 2027.

Ocean warming doesn't work like a light switch. It accumulates over months. The jet stream disruption takes time to fully establish. Regional weather effects build gradually. Forecast confidence and the expected regional impacts will be reassessed as the event develops.

The Met Office will issue regular updates through their forecasting portal. The World Meteorological Organization publishes quarterly climate outlooks. For UK residents and businesses, the practical message is straightforward: treat autumn 2026 as a period with elevated rainfall risk, verify your property's drainage and insurance, and watch official weather forecasts as they develop.

If you wake up before dawn on Friday, August 28, you can watch the <a href="https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/">Moon pass through Earth's shadow</a>. This <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/tag/astronomy-events/">partial lunar eclipse</a> will plunge over 93 percent of the lunar disk into Earth's umbra—the deep central shadow—making it an unusually deep partial lunar eclipse.

Greatest eclipse occurs at about 04:14 UTC (coordinated universal time), which translates to approximately 05:12 AM British Summer Time for UK observers. The Moon will appear low in the western sky at that moment, just before it sets below the horizon. US Eastern Time observers will see the event at approximately 00:14 AM on August 28 (just after midnight), while US Pacific viewers catch it around 21:14 on Thursday evening—their best viewing opportunity.

Here's what happens during a lunar eclipse. The Sun, Earth, and Moon align with Earth in the middle. <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/universe/what-is-an-eclipse/">Earth's shadow has two parts</a>: the penumbra (the outer, faint shadow) and the umbra (the deep shadow where sunlight is almost completely blocked). During this eclipse, the Moon slides through the penumbra starting around 02:23 UTC, then enters the umbra starting around 03:33 UTC and reaches maximum eclipse at 04:14 UTC. By maximum eclipse, the Moon sits deep in Earth's umbra, taking on a reddish color from sunlight that filters through Earth's atmosphere.

That reddish tint happens because sunlight passing through Earth's atmosphere is filtered so that more red and orange light reaches the Moon. Shorter blue wavelengths scatter away, while longer red wavelengths pass straight through. The exact shade depends on volcanic dust and pollution in Earth's atmosphere—sometimes the Moon appears bright orange, sometimes deep red, sometimes almost black.

No protective glasses are necessary. Unlike solar eclipses, you can safely watch lunar eclipses with your naked eye, through binoculars, or with a telescope. The Moon isn't bright enough to cause eye damage.

The full eclipse sequence for observers in London runs from the penumbral phase start at approximately 02:23 BST through the partial phase ending around 06:13–06:15 BST, with peak immersion at 05:12 BST. Total duration is about three and a half hours. Most observers focus on the 90 minutes around maximum eclipse, when the reddish coloration appears most vivid.

Cloud cover could be a problem depending on your location and August 28 weather patterns. Check local forecasts the evening before. If clouds block your view, <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/">NASA</a> and other space agencies usually stream <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/category/space/">eclipse footage online</a>.

The umbral magnitude shows 0.930—meaning over 93 percent of the Moon enters Earth's deep shadow.

Set an alarm, brew coffee, find a dark spot with clear western skies, and spend 90 minutes watching the Moon redden. No equipment required.

The <a href="https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements">US Food and Drug Administration</a> approved a new cancer treatment on August 26 that showed survival improvement in clinical testing, offering hope to patients facing one of medicine's most difficult diagnoses. The drug, called Rasonque (generic name: daraxonrasib), is a RAS inhibitor designed to target RAS-driven cancer signaling in <a href="https://www.cancer.gov/">pancreatic cancer</a>.

Here's what makes this significant. Pancreatic cancer kills quickly. In the clinical trial, patients taking Rasonque achieved a median overall survival of 13.2 months compared to 6.7 months in the standard-chemotherapy group. The median survival was nearly twice as long.

The trial enrolled 500 patients with advanced pancreatic cancer who had already received at least one prior treatment or couldn't tolerate intensive chemotherapy. Half received Rasonque as a daily tablet. Half received investigator's choice standard chemotherapy. After following these patients over time, researchers found that 53.3 percent of Rasonque patients were alive at 12 months, compared to 18.7 percent of those on standard chemotherapy.

The FDA approved the application about 6.5 months ahead of its goal date under its expedited review processes, including the Commissioner's National Priority Review Voucher pilot program.

Daraxonrasib works by inhibiting RAS signaling molecules that drive cancer cell division. <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/tag/cancer-research/">RAS mutations</a> appear in roughly one-third of all human cancers, but oncologists have struggled for decades to develop drugs that effectively target RAS. The technical barriers are substantial. RAS proteins sit inside cells and are notoriously difficult for drugs to reach and inhibit. The approval represents a new clinical application of RAS-targeted therapy in pancreatic cancer.

The drug isn't a cure. That needs to be stated clearly. It extends survival in <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/tag/medical-innovation/">advanced metastatic pancreatic cancer</a> for the defined patient population studied in the trial.

Side effects accompany the benefit. The FDA's safety data showed that 43.6 percent of patients on Rasonque experienced grade 3 or higher adverse reactions—serious side effects. Common adverse effects included rash, diarrhea, mouth sores, nausea, fatigue, vomiting, abdominal pain, swelling, decreased appetite, and bleeding. Grade 3 or higher adverse reactions were reported less frequently in the daraxonrasib group than in the standard-treatment group.

The approval carries important limitations. Rasonque is approved specifically for adults with advanced metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who have progressed after at least one prior treatment. It's not approved for early-stage pancreatic cancer. It's not approved as a first-line treatment. The FDA approves drugs for defined patient populations based on trial data, not for all patients with a disease.

<a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/">Clinical trials are ongoing</a> in other pancreatic cancer settings. Studies registered on ClinicalTrials.gov show tests of daraxonrasib in first-line metastatic disease (treatment-naive patients) and resected pancreatic cancer (patients after surgery). Those trials will determine whether Rasonque has a role in earlier disease stages or as initial therapy.

For patients currently facing advanced pancreatic cancer diagnosis after prior treatment, Rasonque represents a <a href="https://www.karmactive.com/category/health/">treatment option</a> where few existed before.
