Ariana Grande filed a lawsuit at Los Angeles County Superior Court on Monday, July 27, 2026, accusing a group of unidentified defendants of hacking into collaborators' accounts and devices to steal her unreleased music, photos and video footage. The complaint names "John Doe 1" and "John Does 2" through 100, since none of the alleged hackers has been identified by name.

According to the complaint, the defendants gained access to online accounts and devices belonging to photographers, producers and other people who worked with Grande, rather than breaking into her own devices directly. The filing alleges the material — unreleased masters and demos, recording-session footage, music-video outtakes and behind-the-scenes photos — was later resold for large sums of money. That distribution pattern matches what the Recording Industry Association of America has documented in its own submissions to federal trade officials, which describe organized groups using hacking and social engineering to obtain pre-release recordings and sell them on.

Grande's suit raises three claims: invasion of privacy, violation of California's Comprehensive Computer Data Access and Fraud Act (Penal Code Section 502), and conversion, a claim built around unauthorized control of someone else's property. Her attorneys wrote that the "malicious invasion of Ms. Grande's privacy and disruption of her career have caused her substantial and irreparable harm due to this breach of privacy," adding that "neither Ms. Grande nor any other public or private figure should have to suffer such violations."

A comparable scheme already produced a federal criminal case. Christian Erazo pleaded guilty at Manhattan federal court after prosecutors said he broke into a producer's social media account, impersonated that producer to request unreleased tracks from other artists, and separately hacked two music management companies, according to the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York. How a Doe lawsuit tries to name an anonymous hacker

Suits like Grande's rarely name a real person on day one. Expand each step to see the general process a Doe complaint follows on its way toward an identity.

Step 1 — File against unknown defendants
A plaintiff who cannot yet identify who harmed them can still open a case by naming "Doe" defendants, which preserves legal claims while the search for a real identity continues.
Step 2 — Request records through discovery
Once a case is open, attorneys can ask a court for permission to send subpoenas to companies that may hold records tied to an account, device or connection linked to the alleged conduct.
Step 3 — Match records to a person
Any records returned get compared against other evidence gathered during the case, with the goal of connecting an account or device to one specific individual.
Step 4 — Amend the complaint
If a name is confirmed, attorneys can amend the complaint to replace a "Doe" placeholder with the identified defendant, letting the case proceed against a named party who can then be served.

Grande is not the first musician whose circle has faced this kind of scheme. A 2014 hacking campaign against more than 200 Apple iCloud accounts, many belonging to entertainment figures, led to four separate federal guilty pleas — including a Connecticut man sentenced to eight months in prison for a phishing scheme that captured usernames and passwords, according to the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Connecticut. The FBI's cyber division continues to publish guidance for reporting phishing attempts and account takeovers of the kind described across both cases.
