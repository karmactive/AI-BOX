### What "Circumscribed" Tells Clinicians

A lung nodule is a small abnormal area on lung tissue, typically under 30 millimeters in diameter. Causes range from past respiratory infections and scar tissue to non-cancerous cysts. Some nodules are malignant. "Circumscribed" tells clinicians the lesion has defined edges and has not extended into surrounding tissue — a finding that substantially reduces the statistical likelihood of invasive spread.

For patients over 70, surgical removal followed by biopsy is standard protocol even when a nodule appears benign on imaging. Pathology on the removed tissue is the only definitive confirmation of whether cells are cancerous. Imaging alone cannot make that determination. The American Cancer Society's lung nodule explainer at cancer.org outlines this process clearly for patients navigating similar findings.

VATS — the minimally invasive approach — uses small incisions and a camera rather than opening the chest wall. Patients typically regain mobility within days and are discharged within a week, which matches the Vatican's stated recovery timeline. That is a major clinical difference from traditional open thoracotomy, which carries higher complication and recovery rates in older patients.

**Is a circumscribed lung nodule always cancerous?**
No. Circumscribed lung nodules are frequently benign growths caused by past respiratory infections, scar tissue, or non-cancerous cysts. In adults over 70, surgical removal is routinely performed to confirm the growth's nature through pathology. A circumscribed finding does not indicate invasive spread.

**Does surgery for a lung nodule mean cancer was confirmed?**
Not at the time of the procedure. Removal provides the tissue sample that allows pathology to determine whether cells are malignant. The decision to operate is based on clinical risk assessment, not a confirmed cancer diagnosis in advance.

# Why Is Columbus Day Controversial? Colonialism, Italian-American Heritage, and Indigenous Peoples' Day Explained

Columbus Day falls on the second Monday in October and remains a federal holiday. Dozens of states, cities, and universities have either renamed it Indigenous Peoples' Day or added that observance alongside it. The disagreement is real, documented, and older than the social media cycles that amplify it each year. Here is where the holiday came from, what each position is actually arguing, and how observances vary where you live.

Columbus Day became a federal holiday under President Benjamin Harrison in 1892, the 400th anniversary of Columbus's 1492 voyage. The proclamation came one year after the 1891 lynching of eleven Italian immigrants in New Orleans — the largest mass lynching in American history — and was partly a diplomatic gesture toward Italy and partly a civic signal that Italian Americans belonged in US public life. The holiday was formally codified as a federal observance in 1934. Indigenous Peoples' Day emerged as a counter-observance in 1992, when Berkeley, California, became one of the first cities to officially rename the day on the 500th anniversary of Columbus's arrival. As of 2026, more than a dozen states and hundreds of cities have adopted Indigenous Peoples' Day. It is not a federal holiday; Congress has not voted to rename the federal observance.

Whether your school, employer, or local government observes Columbus Day, Indigenous Peoples' Day, or both depends on the level of government that governs it. Federal employees and institutions follow the federal calendar. State employees follow state law. Public school closures follow local district calendars. The result is a direct patchwork: workers in the same building may follow different holiday schedules depending on who employs them.

## What Columbus's 1492 Voyage Set in Motion

Christopher Columbus's arrival in the Caribbean in October 1492 initiated sustained European presence in the Western Hemisphere. What followed over the next three centuries included the systematic dispossession of Indigenous peoples from their land, the introduction of diseases to which Native populations had no prior immunity — causing the deaths of tens of millions — and the transatlantic slave trade, which forcibly moved an estimated 12 to 15 million Africans to the Americas.

Historians do not dispute these outcomes. The debate is about whether a federal holiday should honor the person most associated with initiating them. The [National Congress of American Indians](https://www.karmactive.com/category/culture/indigenous/) and many tribal nations argue it should not. Italian-American civic organizations argue the holiday was not created to celebrate colonialism but to protect a community facing lethal violence in the US — and those two origin stories are both accurate.

## Why Italian Americans Have a Specific Stake in Columbus Day

Columbus's Italian origin was the direct reason he became a symbol for Italian immigrants in the US. The 1891 New Orleans lynching — in which eleven men were killed by a mob following acquittals in a police chief's murder trial — came after decades of anti-Italian prejudice that classified recent immigrants as racially inferior and socially dangerous. Harrison's 1892 proclamation named Columbus as a figure representing Italian achievement specifically to confer civic recognition on the community.

The Columbus Day parade tradition — still running in New York City with tens of thousands of marchers — carries that origin directly. For many older Italian Americans, the holiday is less about the explorer and more about the moment their community was formally recognized by the federal government after years of exclusion and violence. That [historical context](https://www.karmactive.com/category/culture/history/) does not invalidate the Indigenous critique. But it explains why dismissing the parade tradition as simply "celebrating colonialism" misses what the tradition was built on.

## Why Indigenous Peoples' Day Emerged as a Specific Counter-Observance

The 1992 Berkeley counter-observance was not a campaign to erase Italian-American culture. It was a demand that Indigenous histories, perspectives, and sovereignty be part of the national October conversation — rather than invisible in a calendar that treats 1492 as a moment of discovery for everyone equally.

The shift to Indigenous Peoples' Day in states and cities has generally come through legislative votes. South Dakota renamed its observance Native American Day in 1990, before the Berkeley proclamation. Alaska, Vermont, Maine, New Mexico, and others have followed through their own legislative processes. The pattern reflects local political consensus rather than a national directive. Some governments retain Columbus Day, some rename it entirely, and some officially recognize both on the second Monday of October.

**Is Columbus Day a federal holiday?**
Yes. Columbus Day is observed on the second Monday in October and is one of 11 federal public holidays. Federal offices, banks, and many postal services are closed. The holiday has not been renamed at the federal level.

**Is Indigenous Peoples' Day a federal holiday?**
No. Indigenous Peoples' Day is observed by many states, cities, universities, and institutions, but it does not have federal holiday status. Some governments observe both on the same calendar date.

**Why do some places observe both?**
Some states and cities keep Columbus Day on their official calendars while adding Indigenous Peoples' Day as a concurrent or separately named observance. This reflects political compromise between communities with different relationships to the day.

**Why do Indigenous advocates criticize Columbus Day?**
Columbus's 1492 voyage initiated European colonization of the Western Hemisphere. The colonization process involved dispossession, violence, and the deaths of tens of millions of Indigenous people through disease and direct conflict. Critics argue that a federal holiday honoring Columbus implicitly treats those outcomes as acceptable or irrelevant.

The strongest version of this debate is not about one Italian navigator's character. It is about what a national calendar signals about whose history is centered and whose is treated as context. The practical answer for most people is local: what your state, school district, and employer observe on the second Monday of October is determined by your specific level of government, and those decisions vary widely.
