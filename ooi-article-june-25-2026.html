Senate Saves the $368 Million Deep-Sea Monitoring Network That Watches Earth's Climate Engine

On June 18–19, 2026, the U.S. Senate passed the "Saving the OOI Act" by unanimous consent, halting the dismantling of the Ocean Observatories Initiative — a network of over 900 deep-sea instruments spanning five ocean arrays.

What Was Going Silent — And Why It Matters

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is a planet-scale system of ocean currents that moves warm surface water from the tropics toward the North Atlantic, where it cools, sinks, and returns south as cold deep water. This system regulates temperatures across Europe, the eastern United States, and large parts of the Arctic. The Irminger Sea Array is one of the only direct, continuous, real-time monitoring stations for this system. Research using OOI data has shown that AMOC could slow by 51% by 2100, with a tipping point potentially arriving as early as the 2040s. Without the Irminger Sea data, scientists lose one of their primary tools for detecting these changes before they affect global weather patterns, rainfall, and food systems. The OOI provides the only sustained deep-ocean measurement platform in this location.
OOI data does not stay in university labs. It flows directly into fisheries management models used by NOAA Fisheries and state agencies to set catch limits, predict migration patterns, and issue early warnings for harmful algal blooms and low-oxygen zones. Station Papa in the Gulf of Alaska feeds data that underpins the management of one of the most valuable commercial fishing regions on Earth — a $8 billion annual sector built on salmon, pollock, crab, halibut, and sablefish. The Endurance Array, now partially removed, tracked ocean acidification levels in Pacific coastal waters — a critical factor for shellfish larvae survival. Oyster hatcheries in Oregon and Washington have already experienced crashes linked to acidification events that were first detected and tracked by OOI instruments.
The Regional Cabled Array sits directly on the Juan de Fuca tectonic plate — one of the most seismically active zones off the U.S. West Coast. This array is the only real-time seafloor observation system monitoring the Cascadia Subduction Zone, a fault capable of producing a magnitude 9.0 or greater earthquake. The data it streams continuously feeds into early warning systems and geological research that informs tsunami preparedness protocols for Oregon, Washington, and Northern California. The array also monitors submarine volcanic activity and hydrothermal vent systems that provide unique windows into the conditions that support life in extreme environments on Earth — and potentially on other ocean worlds in our solar system.
Ocean science depends on long, unbroken time series — data recorded at the same location, with the same calibrated instruments, year after year. A continuous 10-year record is not the same as two 5-year records with a gap in between. Removing and reinstalling instruments resets the calibration baseline. The data gap created when instruments go dark cannot be reconstructed after the fact. Every month of missed data represents a permanent hole in the scientific record. Because OOI provides open, free access to all its data through the NSF data portal — accessible to any researcher anywhere in the world — the cost of these gaps extends far beyond U.S. institutions. Research groups across Europe, Japan, Canada, and Australia who depend on OOI datasets would lose access to data they have no alternative source for.

From Budget Cut to Senate Vote — What Happened

2016

OOI Goes Online After Years of Planning

After more than a decade of design, construction, and deployment, the Ocean Observatories Initiative begins full operations. The $368 million NSF-funded network spans five arrays across the Atlantic and Pacific, with more than 900 instruments monitoring chemistry, biology, geology, and physics of the ocean from the surface to the seafloor.

Early 2026

Administration Proposes Cuts to NSF Science Programs

The Trump administration's budget proposals include significant reductions to NSF funding. The Ocean Observatories Initiative is targeted, with plans to remove the Endurance Array — the network's Pacific coastal monitoring component — and reduce operations across other arrays. Scientists and coastal communities begin raising concerns through public comment and congressional outreach.

May 2026

Endurance Array Instrument Removal Begins

Removal of Endurance Array instruments begins off the Oregon and Washington coast. Ocean scientists, fishing industry representatives, and coastal state governments issue statements documenting the data gap this creates, particularly for monitoring Pacific Ocean acidification, upwelling patterns, and coastal ecosystem conditions that directly affect the commercial shellfish industry.

June 18, 2026

Senate Introduces the "Saving the OOI Act"

Senators Jeff Merkley (D-OR) and Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) introduce a two-page bipartisan bill titled the "Saving the OOI Act," directing that the remaining Ocean Observatories Initiative arrays not be dismantled. The bill reflects a rare alignment between Democratic and Republican senators on a science funding issue, with both citing the economic and scientific costs of losing the network.

June 19, 2026

Senate Passes the Act by Unanimous Consent

The U.S. Senate passes the "Saving the OOI Act" by unanimous consent — meaning no senator objected to or voted against the measure. The vote halts further dismantling of the OOI network beyond the already-removed Endurance Array components. The bill now moves to the House of Representatives for consideration.

NSF — Official Program Description

The Ocean Observatories Initiative (OOI) is a science-driven ocean observing network that delivers real-time data on physical, chemical, geological, and biological variables from the seafloor to the sea surface. Funded by the National Science Foundation, OOI makes all data freely available to the global scientific community through an open-access portal at oceanobservatories.org. The network was designed to provide a minimum of 25 years of continuous observations to enable understanding of climate variability, ocean ecosystems, and seafloor processes.
