Something is happening in the world's rivers that most people can't see, smell, or taste — at least not yet. Beneath the surface of rivers from the Ganges to the Mississippi, from the Amazon to the Yellow River, dissolved oxygen levels have been quietly falling for decades.

A study published in Science Advances in May 2026 — the most comprehensive global analysis of river oxygen levels ever conducted — found that 78.8% of the world's river systems, covering more than 21,000 rivers, have experienced sustained deoxygenation over the past 40 years. The researchers, led by Prof. Kun Shi and Dr. Qi Guan at the Nanjing Institute of Geography and Limnology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, analyzed dissolved oxygen data from 1981 to 2020.

On average, dissolved oxygen in the world's rivers dropped by 0.045 milligrams per liter every decade. That may sound small. But in river ecosystems — where fish, invertebrates, microorganisms, and plant life depend on precise oxygen thresholds — even small shifts carry outsized consequences.

WORDPRESS PARAGRAPH BLOCK 2 — WHAT IS DISSOLVED OXYGEN

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is exactly what it sounds like: oxygen molecules dissolved in water. Fish absorb it through their gills. Aquatic invertebrates — the beetles, worms, and larvae that form the base of freshwater food chains — depend on it to survive. When oxygen levels fall below roughly 3 milligrams per liter, rivers enter what scientists call hypoxia. Below 2 mg/L, they are considered "dead zones" — environments where most complex life cannot survive.

Healthy rivers carry between 8 and 12 mg/L of dissolved oxygen. The decline recorded in the Science Advances study is not evenly distributed: rivers in South and Southeast Asia, where population density and agricultural intensity are highest, are declining the fastest. According to the USGS, rivers and streams are the primary drinking water source for roughly one in three people worldwide — which means a river oxygen crisis is also, in many places, a drinking water crisis.

WORDPRESS PARAGRAPH BLOCK 3 — TWO DRIVERS

Two forces are driving the global decline, and they compound each other.

The first is climate warming. Warmer water holds less oxygen — this is basic physics. As global temperatures rise, river water absorbs less oxygen from the atmosphere and releases what it already holds more quickly. The Science Advances study found that 62.7% of the documented deoxygenation is driven by temperature increases. With the world currently on course for 2.7°C of warming, the trajectory points toward accelerating losses in the decades ahead.

The second driver is nutrient pollution — primarily nitrogen and phosphorus from agricultural runoff, sewage, and urban stormwater. Excess nutrients fuel explosive algal blooms. When the algae die, decomposing bacteria consume enormous amounts of oxygen, stripping what little remains from the water. This cycle — called eutrophication — is compounding a looming 2050 water quality emergency driven by nitrogen pollution. Warmer water supports larger algal blooms. Larger blooms create deeper dead zones. Dead zones warm faster. The feedback is self-reinforcing.

There is a third, less-discussed consequence: in low-oxygen conditions, anaerobic bacteria produce methane, hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia as byproducts. These compounds affect the taste, safety, and treatment cost of drinking water — and they link directly to the broader methane acceleration already recorded in Earth's atmosphere.

WORDPRESS PARAGRAPH BLOCK 4 — WILDLIFE AND BIODIVERSITY

The effect on freshwater wildlife has been severe. Data from the IUCN Red List and the WWF Living Planet Report show that migratory freshwater fish populations have fallen by 76% since 1970. Overall, freshwater species — including fish, amphibians, reptiles, and invertebrates — have declined by 83% over the same period, a rate of loss far outpacing terrestrial or marine species.

Rivers with depleted oxygen can't support fish populations. The species that disappear first are often the most ecologically important: large migratory fish like salmon, sturgeon, and hilsa that connect river headwaters to estuaries and oceans, moving nutrients and energy across entire watersheds. Their loss doesn't stay in rivers — it reaches forests, wetlands, coastal fisheries, and the communities that depend on all of them. This chain reaction across interconnected ecosystems is now well-documented in conservation science.

WORDPRESS PARAGRAPH BLOCK 5 — WATER AND FOOD SECURITY

Freshwater fish provide the primary protein source for more than 200 million people worldwide. Many of the world's most productive inland fisheries — in Bangladesh, the Mekong Basin, the Congo Basin, and along the Ganges — are directly dependent on river oxygen levels. As rivers lose oxygen, fish stocks decline, and with them the livelihoods of the people who depend on those fisheries.

The risks don't stop at fishing communities. Sewage and waste already entering rivers create conditions where harmful bacteria proliferate — deoxygenation compounds these risks by favoring exactly the microbes that produce harmful byproducts. The result is drinking water that requires more treatment, at higher cost, to reach safe standards. In lower-income regions where treatment infrastructure is limited, those costs are often simply not paid — and the burden falls on the people drinking the water.

WORDPRESS PARAGRAPH BLOCK 6 — HISTORICAL CONTEXT

In August 2022, the Odra River along the Poland-Germany border became a visible, close-up example of what happens when a river's oxygen balance collapses. A combination of extreme heat, reduced water flow, elevated salinity, and an algal toxin stripped oxygen from the water, killing an estimated 100 or more tonnes of fish and triggering a full ecological emergency. Government investigations found that years of industrial and agricultural discharge had built up a reservoir of vulnerability — and one hot summer was enough to tip the system over.

The Odra was not an isolated event. The Ganges River in India is losing dissolved oxygen at a rate 21 times faster than the global average, according to the Science Advances study. Rivers in densely populated agricultural basins across South and East Asia face pressures the Odra River — for all its problems — still doesn't match in scale.

Yet rivers are not inevitably lost. The Thames, which was effectively biologically dead through much of the 1950s and 1960s, recovered after sustained investment in wastewater treatment and water quality regulation. Today it supports over 115 fish species. The same warming pressures now reshaping ocean ecosystems were thought impossible to reverse in rivers too — until they were.

WORDPRESS PARAGRAPH BLOCK 7 — WHAT WORKS

A study from China, published in June 2026, found that in river segments where wastewater management had been substantially upgraded, dissolved oxygen levels had reversed course and begun to rise. The improvement linked directly to reductions in nutrient discharge from treated wastewater — showing that the human-driven portion of river deoxygenation is not permanent.

The Science Advances study identified several practical levers. Improved flow management — controlling how much water moves through a river at key times of year — reduced oxygen losses by between 7% and 18.6% in studied basins. Fertilizer reduction and precision agriculture lower the nitrogen and phosphorus entering rivers from farmland, addressing eutrophication at its source. AI-powered satellite monitoring now allows researchers and regulators to track river oxygen levels in near-real-time, identifying problem areas before they become crises — and directing limited resources to where they matter most. According to Wetlands International, protecting and restoring river-adjacent wetlands also buffers against nutrient overloading by acting as natural filtration systems.

WORDPRESS PARAGRAPH BLOCK 8 — WHAT YOU CAN DO

The connection between everyday choices and river oxygen levels is more direct than most people realize. Excess fertilizer applied to lawns and gardens washes into stormwater systems and eventually reaches rivers — reducing or eliminating synthetic fertilizer use, or switching to slow-release formulas, cuts nutrient loading at the household level. Food choices with a lower agricultural footprint reduce the demand for the intensive nitrogen-heavy farming that drives eutrophication in major river basins.

Proper disposal of medications, household chemicals, and cleaning products keeps them out of wastewater systems that rivers ultimately receive. Support for water quality legislation, investment in municipal wastewater infrastructure, and political engagement on climate policy all shape the broader conditions that river health depends on. The same water systems already carrying microplastics into drinking supplies are the ones being asked to absorb ever-higher nutrient and heat loads. The compounding is real — and so is the opportunity to reduce it, one lever at a time.

A peer-reviewed analysis of 40 years of dissolved oxygen data across more than 21,000 of the world's river systems has confirmed a sustained, widespread decline in river oxygen levels — one driven primarily by climate warming and compounded by agricultural nutrient pollution. The findings document changes affecting 78.8% of global river systems, with the most severe rates of decline recorded in South and Southeast Asia. Historical examples of river recovery — including the Thames revival and improvements documented in Chinese river basins through better wastewater management — have been cited in the scientific literature as evidence that human-driven deoxygenation can, under the right conditions, be reversed. Wastewater management, flow regulation, and agricultural nutrient reduction have been identified as the primary tools available. The research has been made available through Science Advances, a peer-reviewed open-access journal of the American Association for the Advancement of Science.

What People Are Asking About River Oxygen
Five questions — answered directly

Fish absorb dissolved oxygen through their gills — they cannot breathe atmospheric air the way land animals do. When dissolved oxygen drops below 3 mg/L (the hypoxia threshold), most fish species experience physiological stress: reduced growth, impaired reproduction, and immune suppression. Below 2 mg/L, mass die-offs become likely.

The species hit hardest are large, migratory fish — salmon, sturgeon, hilsa, and catfish — that require sustained high-oxygen conditions throughout their life cycle. These are also often the species that support major inland fisheries. When they go, the food web restructures around smaller, more tolerant species — which are less nutritious and support fewer dependent predators, including humans.

According to the UN Convention on Migratory Species, migratory freshwater fish populations have declined 76% since 1970 — a collapse playing out across every major river basin on Earth.

Anaerobic bacteria produce methane, hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia as metabolic byproducts. These compounds affect the taste and odor of drinking water and increase treatment costs.

Standard water treatment systems are designed primarily for bacterial pathogens, not for high concentrations of these anaerobic byproducts. In regions where treatment infrastructure is limited — which includes most of the areas where river deoxygenation is worst — the impact can be direct: water that smells, tastes, or is unsafe to drink without expensive additional treatment.

USGS water resources data estimates that roughly one in three people worldwide relies on rivers and streams as their primary drinking water source. For those 2+ billion people, river oxygen levels are not an abstract ecological statistic.

Freshwater fish provide the primary protein source for an estimated 200 million people worldwide. Inland fisheries — rivers, lakes, and floodplains — contribute roughly 12% of global fish catches, with the true nutritional impact far higher in countries where freshwater fish dominates local diets (Bangladesh, Cambodia, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and large parts of India).

River deoxygenation reduces fish populations directly by making habitats uninhabitable, and indirectly by disrupting the invertebrate and plant communities that fish feed on. When a major river loses its fish, the people who depend on that fishery for daily protein typically don't have affordable alternatives — they eat less, or eat worse.

The problem compounds outward. Nitrogen pollution from the same agricultural systems that strain river oxygen also contaminates groundwater and coastal fisheries, closing off the escape routes that overstressed river fish populations might otherwise take.

Several approaches have demonstrated measurable results:

  • Wastewater treatment upgrades: The Thames recovery is the gold standard — from biologically dead in the 1960s to 115 fish species today, driven by investment in sewage treatment. A 2026 Chinese study found that improved wastewater management reversed oxygen decline in specific river segments.
  • Flow management: The Science Advances study found that better regulation of river flow rates reduced deoxygenation by 7% to 18.6% in studied basins.
  • Agricultural nutrient reduction: Precision fertilizer application and buffer zones between farmland and rivers reduce the nitrogen and phosphorus that feed algal blooms. Wetlands International documents how river-adjacent wetlands act as natural nutrient filters when preserved.
  • AI satellite monitoring: Real-time river oxygen monitoring via satellite and AI now enables targeted, early intervention before hypoxia becomes established.

The evidence is clear that recovery is physically possible. The Thames was a genuine dead zone. It recovered. The question is political will and investment speed.

The connection between individual choices and river oxygen levels is more direct than most people realize:

  • Reduce lawn and garden fertilizer use. Excess nitrogen from synthetic fertilizers washes into stormwater systems and reaches rivers. Slow-release or organic alternatives drastically cut runoff.
  • Eat a lower-footprint diet. Meat and dairy production drives intensive nitrogen fertilizer use. Reducing consumption of animal products — even partially — lowers the agricultural pressure on river basins.
  • Dispose of medications and chemicals properly. Pharmaceutical compounds and household chemicals that enter wastewater create treatment challenges that rivers absorb downstream.
  • Support river-protection policy. Wastewater infrastructure investment, agricultural runoff regulation, and climate action all shape the conditions river health depends on. Political engagement scales impact beyond individual household choices.

The Thames Revival

In the 1950s, the Thames was one of the most polluted rivers in Europe — ecologically dead, with no fish in central London for decades. After sustained investment in sewage treatment and water quality regulation from the 1970s onward, it recovered dramatically. Today it supports over 115 fish species, including salmon. The Thames remains the most frequently cited real-world proof that river recovery is achievable.

Rivers Are Not a Background Detail

Every river that loses oxygen is a river losing its capacity to sustain life — for the fish that live in it, the communities that drink from it, and the ecosystems that extend from it in every direction. The science is clear. The solutions are proven. What determines the outcome now is whether the response moves faster than the decline.
