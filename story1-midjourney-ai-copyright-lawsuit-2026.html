Midjourney is trying to keep a discovery fight alive in its copyright case against Disney, Universal, and Warner Bros. Discovery, asking a federal judge in Los Angeles to force the three studios to hand over records of their own artificial intelligence use. The motion, filed in late June 2026 before U.S. District Judge John A. Kronstadt in the Central District of California, seeks to overturn a narrower order from Magistrate Judge A. Joel Richlin that limited the studios' disclosure obligations to consumer-facing AI products only. The underlying case traces back to June 11, 2025, when Disney Enterprises and NBCUniversal filed a joint lawsuit accusing the AI image generator of letting paying subscribers produce unlicensed images of characters including Darth Vader, Shrek, and Homer Simpson. Warner Bros. Discovery joined months later with its own suit naming Superman, Batman, and Bugs Bunny, and the two cases were later consolidated under Judge Kronstadt. Full docket details for the lead case, Disney Enterprises, Inc. v. Midjourney, Inc., are public on CourtListener. Hollywood vs. AI: How the Midjourney Case Reached a Federal Courtroom

A copyright fight over cartoon and movie characters is now a fight over who has to open their own AI records first. Here is how it built up, step by step.

June 11, 2025
Disney Enterprises and NBCUniversal sue Midjourney in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California (Case No. 2:25-cv-05275), alleging its image generator produces unlicensed copies of characters such as Darth Vader, Shrek, and the Minions.
August 6, 2025
Midjourney files its answer, asserting fair use and "unclean hands" as defenses, and notes that many of its subscribers use corporate emails tied to Disney and Universal domains.
September 4, 2025
Warner Bros. Discovery files a parallel suit (Case No. 2:25-cv-08376), naming Superman, Batman, Wonder Woman, Tom and Jerry, Scooby-Doo, and Bugs Bunny among the characters allegedly generated without permission.
November 5, 2025
Judge Kronstadt consolidates the Disney and Warner Bros. Discovery cases "for all purposes, including trial," with the Disney case as lead docket.
June 2026
Magistrate Judge A. Joel Richlin rules that the studios only have to disclose consumer-facing AI products in discovery, not internal tools, training data, or model weights.
June 29, 2026
Midjourney files a motion asking Judge Kronstadt to overturn that limit under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 72(a).
August 17, 2026
A hearing on Midjourney's motion is scheduled at 8:30 a.m. before Judge Kronstadt.

Midjourney's defense rests on two legal ideas. The first, fair use, argues that training an AI model on large volumes of publicly available images is a transformative activity, comparable to how a human artist studies existing work, and that a user typing a prompt for "Batman" doesn't by itself prove infringement. The second, unclean hands, is an older equitable principle: a party that has engaged in the same conduct it is suing over can be barred from winning that suit. Midjourney's attorneys argue that if the studios are training their own internal AI tools on unlicensed material, that practice would count as the same conduct at issue in the case, which is why the company is demanding access to the studios' internal AI business plans, training data, and model records. The U.S. Copyright Office's own public guidance on AI and copyright lays out how the fair-use analysis is applied to generative tools more broadly. The legal standard Judge Kronstadt must apply to overturn Magistrate Judge Richlin's order is a deferential one — the order must be shown to be "clearly erroneous or contrary to law," rather than simply re-argued from scratch. In the studios' original complaint, Midjourney's conduct is described in the filing itself as that of "the quintessential copyright free-rider and a bottomless pit of plagiarism." In Midjourney's own June 2026 motion, attorney Bobby Ghajar of Cooley LLP wrote that "if Plaintiffs are doing the very thing they seek to punish, that evidence goes to the heart of Midjourney's fair use and unclean hands defenses."

Midjourney has said in public interviews that it has never taken venture capital funding and became profitable within months of its 2022 launch, a detail that matters here because a self-funded company absorbs a damages judgment or a content-filtering injunction differently than a venture-backed AI lab can. The Midjourney case is one of several AI-copyright disputes moving through U.S. courts at the same time. A separate class action from visual artists, Andersen v. Stability AI, in which Midjourney is also a defendant, is scheduled for trial in September 2026. Music publishers UMG, Concord, and ABKCO are seeking $3 billion from Anthropic in a related case over song lyrics. The New York Times' suit against OpenAI and Microsoft is also ongoing. In a separate matter concerning AI-generated authorship rather than training data, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear Thaler v. Perlmutter in March 2026, leaving in place a ruling that AI output without meaningful human creative input cannot be copyrighted. In Summary

The dispute covered here traces to a June 2025 complaint filed by Disney Enterprises and NBCUniversal, later joined by Warner Bros. Discovery, over the generation of characters including Darth Vader, Shrek, Superman, and Bugs Bunny on Midjourney's image tool. Midjourney's answer raised fair use and unclean hands defenses. A magistrate judge's June 2026 order limited studio disclosure obligations to consumer-facing AI products, and Midjourney's subsequent motion asked the district judge to expand that scope ahead of an August 17, 2026 hearing. The Copyright Act's statutory damages framework, cited in the complaint, allows up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement, applied across an exhibit list of more than 150 works. Background on the fair-use framework applied to AI training is available through the U.S. Copyright Office and through Georgetown Law's Institute for Technology Law & Policy, and further case filings are on the public court docket.
