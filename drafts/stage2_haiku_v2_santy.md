## An 11-year-old activist deleted from YouTube: Death threats, platform silence, and the price of dissent

Santy Sharma, an 11-year-old YouTube content creator based in Andheri West, Mumbai, created videos on the August Kranti movement and related social themes. Sometime before August 11, 2026, YouTube deleted Santy's channel or specific videos without explanation. Santy had received death threats online in connection with content related to reservation policy criticism ("Reservation Hatao" - Abolish Reservations activism).

The deletion raises acute questions: Was Santy's content removed because it violated YouTube's community standards, or because the threats created liability risk? Was Santy offered protection and support for being targeted, or simply removed from the platform? And for an 11-year-old engaging in social commentary, what safeguards exist when they become targets?

## Child creator and political content: The vulnerability

An 11-year-old creating political content on YouTube is unusual. Questions arise:

- **Parental involvement**: Did parents/guardians supervise content creation and vet posted videos?
- **Platform age restrictions**: YouTube's terms of service require age 13+ for independent accounts. If Santy is 11, how was the account established? (Family account, parental consent, or account misrepresentation?)
- **Political maturity**: Does an 11-year-old have capacity to grasp nuance in reservation debates, or is content scripted/coached by adults?
- **Threat awareness**: Did Santy understand the risk of public political speech, or were threats a shocking surprise?


An 11-year-old is a minor under Indian law (Juvenile Justice Act 2015). Speech protection for minors exists but is not absolute; parental responsibility is concurrent.

## The death threat content: Severity and source


Death threats against a child fall into categories:

- **Frivolous**: Anonymous comments like "kill yourself" (common, not operationalized, rarely prosecutable alone).
- **Specific**: "I know where you live; I'm coming to [address] on [date]" (actionable, warrant investigation).
- **Organized campaign**: Coordinated harassment from multiple accounts, escalating intensity (psychological abuse, potential real harm).

The distinction determines whether threats are noise or danger.

## YouTube's deletion: Policy reason or liability avoidance?

YouTube's automated and manual review systems flag content for:

- **Hate speech**: Content that attacks identity groups based on protected characteristics.
- **Harassment**: Videos that target individuals for abuse.
- **Dangerous organizations**: Content from or promoting banned groups.
- **Suicide/self-harm**: Content that encourages harm.


Alternatively, YouTube may have deleted to mitigate liability:

- If threats are hosted in Santy's comment section, YouTube is liable for failing to remove them quickly.
- Deleting the channel eliminates the target and thus the threat vector—a shortcut to liability management.
- But this punishes the victim (Santy) rather than addressing the harassers.

This pattern is common and problematic: women who report harassment get their accounts suspended; minority creators facing threats see their channels deleted; the platform protects itself by eliminating visibility of the problem.

## The "Reservation Hatao" controversy: Is this speech under attack?

The specific content theme is crucial. "Reservation Hatao" (Abolish Reservations) is a politically contentious position in India:

- **Supporters**: Some argue caste-based reservations are outdated; merit-based selection is fairer.
- **Opponents**: Reservations are constitutional protection (Article 15, 16) against historical discrimination; abolishing them reverses centuries of progress.
- **Ground reality**: Reservation debates are legitimate political discourse in India. Advocacy for policy change (even controversial change) is protected speech.


If Santy's videos were civil policy criticism, deletion for content violates free speech. If videos incited violence or doxing, deletion for safety is justified.

The political context matters: Is Santy being targeted *because* of taking a particular political stance, or would any 11-year-old making social videos face equivalent threats?

## Police FIR and investigation status


Death threats are prosecutable under:

- **IPC Section 503**: Criminal intimidation.
- **IPC Section 504**: Intentional insult with knowledge it provokes breach of peace.
- **IPC Section 506**: Criminal intimidation (enhanced penalties for threats of death/hurt).

If Mumbai police (likely under Andheri West jurisdiction) filed an FIR, investigation should have commenced. Delays in investigation signal low priority or resource constraints.


## Platform moderation and pattern: Is this systemic?

Is Santy's deletion a one-off, or part of a pattern where:

- YouTube/Instagram/Twitter disproportionately delete accounts of creators who face harassment?
- Platforms prioritize liability avoidance (removing targets) over perpetrator accountability?
- Creators who take controversial political stances see higher deletion rates?


If the pattern exists, it's a chilling effect: victims are punished (silenced) rather than protected, and harassers face no platform consequences.

## Puneet Vasishtha's role: Investigation or pressure?

Puneet Vasishtha, a former Bigg Boss contestant, publicly supported Sharma and condemned the threats. The controversy also follows remarks made earlier this week by the Chief Justice of India (CJP) on reservation policy, which sparked national debate.

If Vasishtha is law enforcement, the inquiry is whether investigation is progressing. If Vasishtha is activist/politician, the question is whether they're using Santy's case for political positioning.

## Child protection protocols: What YouTube should do

Child safety frameworks require:

1. **Account security**: Verify parental consent; restrict visibility of child's location/school.
2. **Harassment response**: Proactive removal of threats; not deletion of child's channel.
3. **Parental tools**: Give parents control over who can comment, message, or follow.
4. **Transparency**: Tell parents *why* content is removed; offer appeals process.
5. **Law enforcement liaison**: Connect parents to police if threats are serious.


YouTube's community standards include child safety, but implementation is sparse. Deletions happen with minimal explanation; appeals processes are slow.

## The silencing effect: Broader implications

Santy's case shows a dilemma:

- If Santy's channel is restored, threats resume; she needs protection.
- If the channel stays deleted, Santy is silenced and harassers succeeded in censoring her.
- If police investigate but move slowly, Santy remains unsafe indefinitely.

For an 11-year-old, the calculus is simple: engaging in political speech brought harassment, platform deletion, and no obvious remedy. The message learned: dissent is risky.

Multiply this across thousands of young creators, and a broader chilling effect emerges: people (especially minorities, dissenters, minorities engaging in dissent) self-censor to avoid harassment. Platforms enable this by making silence easier than protection.

## Comparative child creator safety

Other platforms have child-specific protections:

- **TikTok**: Restricts commenting on child creators' videos unless parents approve.
- **Instagram**: "Child Account" mode limits DMs and comments from strangers.
- **Twitter/X**: Allows account protection; limits replies.


## What restoration would require

To restore Santy's channel safely:

1. **Police action**: Identify and arrest/charge threat perpetrators (deterrence).
2. **Platform accountability**: YouTube reverses deletion; implements child protections; publicly commits to defending harassed creators.
3. **Parental support**: Parents given legal aid, counseling resources, ongoing platform support.
4. **Precedent**: Make Santy's case public so other platforms strengthen child protections proactively.

Without precedent, the next 11-year-old activist will face the same choice: silence or harassment.
