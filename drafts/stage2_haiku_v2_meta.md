## Meta under scrutiny: CSAM compliance, automated systems, and Section 79 liability

Meta faced government pressure from India's Ministry of Electronics and IT (MeitY) in early August 2026. On August 2, a Facebook post by Prime Minister Narendra Modi was removed for 5-6 hours due to an automated moderation error. Between August 5-6, Joel Kaplan (Meta's Global Affairs and Communications Vice President) engaged directly with MeitY leadership. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Communications and Information Technology, chaired by BJP MP Nishikant Dubey, issued a three-day ultimatum demanding an unqualified apology from Mark Zuckerberg, threatening to withdraw Section 79 safe harbour protection if he failed to comply.

The core issue: India's parliamentary panel flagged Meta's inadequacy in detecting and removing child sexual abuse material (CSAM) and related harms. Section 79 of India's Information Technology Act 2000 shields intermediaries (platforms) from liability for user-generated content—but only if they act "expeditiously" to remove illegal content and comply with government directions.

The question now is whether Meta's current automated systems meet "expeditious" removal standards, and what remedies MeitY is demanding.

## The August 2 service interruption: What happened?

Meta's 5-6 hour outage/interruption on August 2 requires clarity:

- Was it a planned compliance action (e.g., taking down content in bulk to meet government request)?
- An unplanned technical failure in content moderation systems?
- A targeted block by MeitY?


The timing—just days before Kaplan's MeitY engagement—suggests the outage and government pressure are connected.

## Section 79 and expeditious removal: The legal standard

Section 79 IT Act requires intermediaries to:

1. Implement procedures to block content "upon notification by government, law enforcement, or court order."
2. Respond "expeditiously"—the term is legally vague. Is it 24 hours, 48 hours, or 6 hours?
3. Not proactively monitor user content (safe harbor principle), but remove flagged content quickly.
4. Preserve evidence (logs, user data) for law enforcement.

Meta's automated systems are designed for this, but:

- Are they tuned for India's legal/cultural context (e.g., recognizing deepfakes that mock Indian political figures)?
- Do they catch CSAM variants (e.g., AI-generated, grooming material, non-graphic abuse)?
- What is Meta's actual response time for government removal notices?


## Parliamentary scrutiny of CSAM and deepfakes

A parliamentary panel  issued findings on Meta's CSAM handling. Specific gaps likely include:

- **Detection rate**: What % of CSAM does Meta's AI catch vs. user reports?
- **Deepfakes**: How many synthetic sexual abuse images (AI-generated) are being circulated? How is Meta classifying and removing them?
- **Grooming detection**: Material that facilitates child abuse (not always explicit) is harder to catch than CSAM itself.


The panel likely recommended:

- Faster removal timelines.
- Enhanced AI training for Indian content context.
- Regular audit reports to MeitY.
- Possible legal liability for Meta if standards are not met.

## Joel Kaplan's August 5-6 engagement with MeitY

Joel Kaplan's direct involvement (he is Meta's top public policy executive globally) indicates high-level negotiation, not routine compliance chat.

Likely topics:

- **Compliance roadmap**: Meta's plan to accelerate CSAM detection and removal.
- **Data localization**: MeitY has pushed platforms to store user data in India. Meta has partly complied but not fully. Is this now a condition?
- **Intermediary status**: MeitY's leverage is Section 79 protection. Loss of safe harbor liability protection would expose Meta to user lawsuits for every piece of harmful content. Kaplan's goal is to keep that shield.
- **Legal precedent**: If MeitY finds Meta in breach of Section 79, it sets precedent for other platforms (Google, X, TikTok).


## India's deepfake and synthetic abuse material problem

India is a notable generator and consumer of synthetic sexual abuse imagery:

- AI-generated deepfakes of actresses, influencers, and political figures (primarily women) are created in India and distributed globally.
- Grooming networks operate on encrypted channels with public platforms as recruitment funnels.
- Child exploitation material is frequently re-uploaded after takedown under new accounts.

Detection is hard because:

- No explicit image = harder to flag with image hashing algorithms.
- Actors use proxies, VPNs, and account cycling to evade bans.
- Complaint workflows in India are slow (victim reports to police, police files case, CBI/local cyber cell investigates, platform receives order weeks later).

## Comparative: Google, X, and other platforms' India compliance

How do other platforms fare?

- **Google**: Removed 63,000+ items in India in 2024 per transparency reports. But voluntary removal vs. forced removal rates are unclear.
- **X (Twitter)**: Faces similar pressure on political content; compliance timeline unclear.
- **TikTok**: Banned in India (2020); not relevant.
- **Telegram**: Encrypted; minimal compliance; strong in India.


Meta's August 2026 pressure may signal that it lags competitors, or that MeitY has hardened standards for all platforms.

## Data localization and user privacy

Separately, MeitY has long demanded that platforms:

- Store copies of Indian users' data on servers in India.
- Submit to Indian law enforcement searches without U.S. legal process.
- Comply with Indian content policies (more expansive than global policies).

Meta has resisted full localization, citing privacy and technical cost concerns. The parliamentary panel's pressure adds a new variable to that long-standing dispute.

If MeitY links compliance on CSAM to progress on data localization, Kaplan's visit suggests a potential trade-off: faster CSAM removal in exchange for limited data localization, or vice versa.

## Liability exposure under Section 79

Section 79 is Meta's shield in India. Lose it, and:

- Any user harmed by content (deepfake, CSAM, harassment) can sue Meta directly.
- Liability accumulates across millions of incidents.
- Damages could be substantial (examples: defamation suits in India average 5-50 lakh rupees per case).

MeitY's implicit threat: comply on CSAM, or we strip your safe harbor. Kaplan's mission: keep the shield while making minimum compliance concessions.

## What compliance could cost Meta

Expeditious CSAM removal at Meta's scale means:

- **Staffing**: More human reviewers in India or outsourced contractors to review flagged content in Indian languages (Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Kannada, Bengali, etc.).
- **AI investment**: Retraining models to catch India-specific deepfakes and abuse material.
- **Latency**: Faster response to government notices (e.g., 12-hour removal SLA vs. 72-hour).
- **Audit**: Regular third-party audits of compliance, publishing data.

Cost: likely millions of dollars annually. Revenue impact: none immediate, but regulatory risk decreases.

## Precedent: Meta's India operation at crossroads

India is Meta's largest user base by count (~500M). A setback here affects product roadmap, profit margins, and regulatory precedent globally.

If India's government successfully forces Meta to:
- Sharply accelerate CSAM removal, other democracies follow (EU, Brazil, Australia demand the same).
- Localize data, other countries follow (Russia, China already do).
- Accept government audit, platform independence erodes.

Kaplan's August visit is high-stakes: concede enough to keep operating, concede too much and surrender control.
