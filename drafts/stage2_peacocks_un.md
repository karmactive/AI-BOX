# India Donates 5 Peacocks to UN Office in Geneva: A Gesture of Soft Power Through Wildlife

When nations want to make a point without words, they sometimes send animals. India just proved that logic works at the United Nations.

On August 7, last week, India's Permanent Representative Arindam Bagchi handed over five young peacocks to the UN Office at Geneva—a gift that signals something deeper than mere diplomacy. These birds represent a calculated blend of cultural pride, environmental messaging, and strategic positioning on the world stage. But they also raise harder questions: Does symbolic wildlife diplomacy work when a nation's own conservation record remains contested?

The five birds include four blue peafowl and one white-feathered male. Tatiana Valovaya, Director-General of the UN Office in Geneva, received them personally. This wasn't a spontaneous gesture. India revived a tradition that stretches back 45 years, to 1981, when then-Prime Minister Indira Gandhi sent a breeding pair of peacocks from Delhi Zoo to the same UN office. That pair thrived for decades, becoming fixtures in the UN's Ariana Park, a sprawling 46-hectare green space on the Geneva grounds.

But why peacocks? Why now?

Peacocks carry weight in Indian culture. They're national birds. They symbolize grace, beauty, and resilience—qualities India wants to project internationally. They're also inherently photogenic, which matters in modern diplomacy. A photograph of colorful birds arriving at a UN facility travels farther than a policy statement ever could.

The logistics alone reveal how serious India's effort was. The five birds weren't simply shipped in a crate. They've been placed in an enclosed habitat within Ariana Park for the first three months, a quarantine and adaptation period. Dr. Tobias Blaha, a veterinarian from Bioparc Genève, is overseeing their care—ensuring the transition doesn't kill them, which has been a real risk with past wildlife transfers. After three months, if all goes well, they'll roam freely across the park's grounds.

The UN is capitalizing on the gift. Officials announced plans to launch a naming competition, inviting staff and visitors to suggest names for the birds. It's a smart move. Naming competitions create engagement. They make the birds part of the institutional identity. Within weeks, these peacocks will have human connections at the UN. People will check on them. They'll become symbols that employees and visiting diplomats see and remember.

On the surface, this is clever soft power. India gets positive coverage. The birds are living reminders of Indian culture and biodiversity. Every photograph of a peacock in Geneva is, in essence, a small advertisement for India's commitment to nature and international cooperation.

But scratch deeper, and the symbolism becomes more complicated.

India has aggressive environmental goals on paper. The country has committed to expanding forest cover and protecting endangered species. Peacocks themselves aren't endangered—they're relatively stable in wild populations. But the donation comes at a moment when India's domestic conservation challenges are severe. Forest loss continues in regions like the Western Ghats and the Northeast. Wildlife corridors are fragmented. Urban expansion is relentless.

Sending peacocks to Geneva while struggling with conservation at home raises an obvious question: Who is this really for?

The answer, likely, is both audiences. The international gesture serves India's image abroad. It positions India as a responsible steward of nature on the global stage. But it also sends a message domestically—that wildlife matters, that peacocks are precious, that nature is worth celebrating. Whether that message translates into actual conservation funding or policy shifts remains unclear.

The cost of the donation is also worth considering. Acquiring five birds, obtaining export permits, arranging international transport, and setting up a proper enclosure doesn't come cheap. Neither does ongoing veterinary care in Geneva. These are real expenses. The money could have funded habitat protection in India itself. But that's not how soft power works. Soft power requires visible, impressive gestures. It requires sacrifice. It requires doing something that captures attention.

India's Permanent Representative framed the gift as a symbol of India's commitment to biodiversity and its relationship with the UN. He emphasized the continuity—that this echoed the 1981 donation, reaffirming a 45-year connection between India and the UN's Geneva office. He positioned the birds as ambassadors of Indian culture and environmental values.

Whether the peacocks deliver on that promise depends on what happens next. If they thrive and become beloved fixtures at the UN, the symbolism holds. If they sicken or die—as has happened with past wildlife gifts—the gesture falls flat. If the naming competition generates genuine public engagement, the birds become real ambassadors. If it's forgotten within weeks, it becomes just another donation.

For now, five young peacocks are adjusting to life in a Geneva enclosure. They're eating well. The weather is favorable. Dr. Blaha reports no serious health issues.

In three months, they'll emerge into Ariana Park. They'll walk across UN grounds, visible to diplomats, staff, and visitors. They'll be living symbols of India's reach and vision. Whether that vision translates into meaningful action on conservation—in Geneva or in India—is a question only time will answer.

For the moment, India has made its point. The peacocks have arrived.
