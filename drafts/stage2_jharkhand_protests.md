# Jharkhand's Recruitment Exam Crisis: When Students Fight for Fair Access

The hunger strike lasted nine days. By the end, Devendra Nath Mahto had lost 10.5 kilograms. His blood glucose dropped to 53—dangerously low. Police beat him with lathis during a water cannon assault. He ended up in the hospital. This is what it took to get Jharkhand's government to the negotiating table.

Mahto, a leader of the Jharkhand Loktantrik Karmichari Morcha (JLKM), was not staging a symbolic fast. He was fighting over something that touches the lives of hundreds of thousands of young people every year: recruitment exams.

## The Exam That Broke Trust

For months, rumors had circulated about the 14th JPSC Civil Services Examination. Stories of leaks. Stories of paper copying. Stories of the test being unfair before the answer sheets were even graded. By late July, the rumors had become a crisis.

On July 25, students began protesting. They demanded one thing clearly: cancel the exam. They demanded something else just as important: a CBI investigation into how the system had failed.

The JPSC-JSSC Reforms Manch, the student coalition leading the agitation, says the exam was compromised at a fundamental level. Nineteen people have been arrested in connection with the irregularities. Three members of the JPSC itself—the commission that should be guaranteeing fairness—resigned.

When a system's own people resign, it shows something is seriously wrong.

## The Adivasi Student Crisis

Jharkhand is not like other states. More than 28 percent of its population is tribal—Adivasi communities with distinct histories and distinct barriers in India's competitive exam system.

For Adivasi students, the recruitment exam is supposed to be a pathway. A way to escape low-wage jobs and land government positions that offer stability, respect, and the ability to support a family. But when the exam itself becomes rigged, the pathway collapses.

The students protesting now are mostly Adivasi. They come from villages and small towns. Many are the first in their families to compete for government jobs. They studied for this exam. They saved money to take coaching classes. They believed the system would be fair. Then it wasn't.

This is not a small matter in Jharkhand. Government employment is one of the few routes to economic security for young people from marginalized communities. When that route is blocked or corrupted, there is no backup plan.

## Six Talks, No Answers

The government held six rounds of talks with protesting students. Six rounds. Nothing broke through. No announcement of a new exam date. No commitment to a probe. No acknowledgment of systemic failure.

For students, the silence was an answer in itself: we are not listening.

By early August, the protest had moved beyond demands. It had become about survival. Devendra Nath Mahto decided to put his body on the line. A hunger strike. Nine days. He would not eat until the government acted.

## When Police Meet Protesters

On August 9, police deployed water cannons and tear gas. They used lathis. Mahto was beaten. He collapsed. He was rushed to the hospital, where doctors found his blood glucose at 53 and his body depleted.

Nineteen people have been arrested in connection with the exam irregularities. The police have arrested no government officials. No JPSC members in their private capacity. Just students and organizers.

The message was not subtle: we will use force against you, not against those who broke the system.

## The Political Backdrop

As August 11 approached, a protest march to the State Assembly was planned for August 10-11. It would have been a major show of strength.

On August 11, the BJP announced a state-wide bandh—a complete shutdown—in support of the protesting students. The move was politically calculated. It allied the ruling party with the student movement, at least symbolically. But it also raised a question: if the government is sympathetic, why haven't the demands been met?

The bandh underlined a political reality in Jharkhand. Student movements have power. They can move parties and politicians. But that power only translates to change when the state actually acts. So far, it has not.

## What Happens Now

The protest is ongoing. Devendra Nath Mahto is in the hospital. The government has not cancelled the exam. The government has not announced a CBI probe. The government has held six rounds of talks and produced nothing.

Thousands of Adivasi students are suspended in uncertainty. They cannot plan their futures. They cannot know if the job they studied for is still available, or if they will have to take the same compromised exam again, or if they will move on to something else entirely.

The recruitment exam in Jharkhand was supposed to be a ladder. For a moment, it became a barrier. Whether it becomes a ladder again depends on what happens in the weeks ahead—and whether the government decides that the demands of Adivasi students, voiced loudly enough to make national news, are worth taking seriously.

For now, the answers are still no. The exam is still scheduled. The investigation has not been announced. And Devendra Nath Mahto is still in the hospital, his body a symbol of how far students have been willing to go to demand fairness from a system that has let them down.
