## UPI's free model in question: Opposition walkout over fee proposal

Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman made a statement or regulatory proposal regarding UPI (Unified Payments Interface) in early August 2026, sparking parliamentary outrage. Both CPI(M) (Communist Party of India—Marxist) and AAP (Aam Aadmi Party) walked out of parliament in protest, a rare show of unity against the central government on a financial regulation matter.

The core issue: whether UPI should remain free or transition to a fee-based model. The regulatory instrument appears to involve Section 10A (a fiscal or statutory section) and/or the Payment Systems Regulation 2007 (PSS 2007).

## UPI's current architecture: Why "free" matters

UPI processes financial transactions at near-zero cost to users:

- A merchant receives payment from a customer; no fees on either side (in most cases).
- Banks facilitate the transfer without charging users.
- The RBI subsidizes the infrastructure cost through regulatory framework.

This free model has driven UPI's extraordinary adoption:

- 500M+ registered users in India (as of mid-2026).
- 8-10 billion monthly transactions (estimated).
- Cost to Indian economy: roughly ₹0 out-of-pocket per transaction for users; substantial backend cost borne by banks and RBI.

The question: Is this model sustainable, or do banks need revenue to operate UPI at scale?

## RBI's role and regulatory framework

The RBI (Reserve Bank of India) governs payment systems under the Payment System Regulation 2007. The central bank can:

- Set transaction limits per user per day.
- Require security standards.
- Permit or restrict payment service providers.
- Approve or reject fee structures proposed by banks/operators.

## Section 10A and fiscal implications

Section 10A likely refers to tax law or subsidy provision. Possible interpretations:

- **Direct tax code**: If Section 10A exempts banks from certain taxes on UPI transaction revenue, removing this exemption would force banks to pay tax on UPI operations, making the business less attractive without a corresponding fee.
- **Subsidy regulation**: If Section 10A mandates government subsidy for free UPI, amending it could withdraw that subsidy, forcing banks to charge users to sustain operations.

## Opposition walkout: Political meaning

Both CPI(M) and AAP walking out is notable. Normally:

- CPI(M) opposes policies favoring corporate interests.
- AAP, led by Delhi Chief Minister Arvind Kejriwal, opposes policies hurting common citizens.

Both saw UPI fees as anti-poor: small merchants, informal workers, and low-income Indians rely on UPI for payments precisely because it's free. A fee shifts burden onto them.

The walkout sends a message: "This crosses a line." Parliament did not pass a resolution or debate; opposition simply left the chamber—a protest tactic used sparingly.

## Transaction impact modeling: Who pays the real cost?

If UPI fees are introduced, impact cascades:

**Small merchants (street vendors, small shops)**:
- Current: Accept ₹100 payment, keep ₹100.
- Post-fee (at ₹0.50 per transaction): Accept ₹100 payment, keep ₹99.50 (1% effective fee on small transactions is proportionally huge).
- Response: Likely push back to cash or demand customers pay fee.

**Large businesses**:
- Process millions of transactions. Fee is operational cost, passed to consumers or absorbed.
- Impact is dispersed across pricing; less visible.

**Informal workers**:
- Gig workers, daily wage workers, domestic help rely on UPI for wages.
- Fee reduces take-home pay incrementally.

**Common citizens**:
- UPI is used for bill payments (utilities, insurance), peer-to-peer transfers, shopping.
- Fees add up if applied across all categories.

- A household help receives daily wages via UPI (no bank charges).

## RBI's subsidy model and sustainability argument

The RBI's implicit argument for free UPI was:

- Accelerate digital payment adoption (achieved: 500M users).
- Reduce cash handling cost for banks and RBI.
- Formalize informal economy (bring unbanked into digital system).

Net benefit to RBI and banks exceeded the cost of subsidy.

But at scale (8B monthly transactions), banks' operational costs rise:

- Server infrastructure for 500M users.
- Fraud prevention and dispute resolution teams.
- Regulatory compliance overhead.

At some point, subsidy becomes unsustainable. The question: Has that point been reached?

## Comparative international models

Other countries' digital payment systems:

- **China (Alipay, WeChat Pay)**: Initially free; now charge small fees to merchants. Consumers remain uncharched.
- **Singapore (PayNow)**: Free for users; banks absorb cost; subsidized by financial services ecosystem breadth.
- **Brazil (PIX)**: Free for individuals and small businesses; modest fees for large/commercial transactions.

## What comes next

The parliamentary walkout delays but does not kill the proposal. Likely scenarios:

1. **Withdrawal**: Government backtracks under political pressure; UPI remains free indefinitely.
2. **Compromise**: Fee introduced only for high-value transactions or merchants, exempting common use cases.
3. **Quiet implementation**: Fee slipped into regulation without public debate; users see it post-hoc.
4. **Structural reform**: Instead of user fees, RBI adjusts bank revenue models (e.g., transaction volume-based compensation) to sustain free UPI.

The opposition walkout bought time but not solution. Without a clear government statement on backing down, the fee proposal remains live policy.
