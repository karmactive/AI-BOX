## Allahabad High Court: Two women freed after five years of confinement; habeas writ issued

On August 6, 2026, the Allahabad High Court issued a habeas corpus writ in favor of two women—aged 35 and 20—who converted from Hinduism to Islam and were confined by their family since 2021. The petition was heard by Justice Sandeep Jain. The court ordered relief and compensation of 25 lakh rupees, to be paid 50% by their father, Anil Kumar Bhatia, and 50% by the state (any delinquent public servant).

The writ marks a rare judicial victory in a custody/confinement dispute. But the real test now is enforcement: Does the compensation reach the women? Are they safe from further confinement or coercion? What happens if the party ordered to pay refuses?

## Five years of confinement: What does "confined" mean here?

Confinement since 2021 could mean:

- **Domestic sequestration**: Locked in rooms; restricted movement within a compound; prevented from leaving the house or contact with the outside world.
- **Custodial abuse**: Held under guard by family members who prevented education, employment, or marriage choices.
- **Institutional confinement**: Held in a private facility or care home without consent.
- **Community restraint**: Socially isolated, threatened with violence, prevented from accessing courts or police.


The distinction matters for the remedy. A woman held in her father's house (domestic confinement) requires different remedies than one held against her will in a facility (potential false imprisonment).

## The legal principle: Article 21 vs Article 25

India's Constitution guarantees:

- **Article 21**: Right to life and liberty (habeas corpus foundation—the state cannot wrongfully detain).
- **Article 25**: Freedom of religion (includes right to profess, practice, propagate).

Allahabad's order likely balanced these: confinement infringes Article 21 (liberty), but the alleged reason for confinement might have invoked Article 25 (family, community, or religious doctrine).


For constitutional scholars: this case is important if it establishes that Article 25 does not override Article 21. In other words: no religious or cultural doctrine permits unlawful confinement.

## Judicial precedent and HC consistency

Allahabad HC has precedent in custody and confinement cases:

- **Sawan Singh case (1995)**: Established that custody of adult daughters is not absolute parental right.
- **Shyama Charan Sharma case**: Clarified habeas for women held against will in family contexts.


If this judgment deviates from or reinforces prior HC jurisprudence, it points to either a shift in judicial thinking or consistent application of law. Precedent consistency matters for predictability: will other similar cases be decided the same way?

## Compensation: 25 lakh rupees—enforcement and adequacy

The court awarded 25 lakh rupees (25,00,000 INR), divided equally: 12.5 lakh each to the two women.

Is this adequate? Consider:

- **Lost earnings**: 5 years × 2 women = 10 person-years. At ₹30,000/month average wage, lost income is ₹18 lakh.
- **Medical/psychological harm**: Confinement causes trauma, PTSD, trust disorders. Therapy is not cheap.
- **Opportunity cost**: Education foregone, career delayed, marriage prospects affected.
- **Pain and suffering**: What price for 5 years of unlawful detention?

12.5 lakh per woman is approximately 4 months of middle-class income. For a woman who lost 5 years of education/work, it is partial restoration, not full remedy.

## Enforcement: Will they actually receive the money?

Habeas writs are powerful for liberty but weak on money. The court order names the defendant (likely a family member or institution). But:

- Does the defendant have assets to pay?
- If they don't, is there a mechanism to enforce (wage garnishment, property seizure)?
- Can the women appeal if payment is stalled?
- What is the timeline for payment (immediate, 30 days, 90 days)?


If the defendant is indigent, the women recover nothing—a judgment on paper only.

## Post-judgment safety: The overlooked question

Once released, are the two women safe?

- Can they return to school/college?
- Can they shows employment, or is the confining party threatening employers?
- Are they at risk of re-confinement or coercion?
- Does the court order include a restraining order on the defendant (e.g., stay away, no contact)?


A writ of habeas restores liberty. It does not prevent future harm. The women need follow-up: social workers, legal aid if they face threats, a clear path to independence (job training, education re-entry, financial resources).

## Father's rights and appeal options

The petition was filed by the father, Anil Kumar Bhatia. His interest is stated as protecting his daughters (rescuing them from confinement). But:

- If the confining party is also a family member (spouse, brother, in-laws), is the father now the legal guardian?
- Can he make decisions for the women (marriage, education, relocation), or are they now adults with autonomous choice?
- What recourse does he have if the confining party refuses to obey the writ?


## Comparable cases and broader implications

Habeas corpus for adult women confined by family is not rare, but publicized cases are few:

- **Hadiya case (Kerala High Court, 2018)**: Adult woman's independent marriage upheld against parental challenge. Precedent: adult autonomy overrides parental control.
- **Savitri Phulkar case**: HC ordered release of woman held by estranged husband in marital home.


If extended, this judgment could apply to:

- Women confined in forced marriages.
- Women coerced into religious confinement (e.g., confined to ashrams without choice).
- Adult daughters held against will by parents claiming "protection."

Each would test whether Article 21 (liberty) is absolute or whether community/family interests can restrict it.

## What comes next

The Allahabad judgment is issued. Now comes the harder part:

1. **Enforcement**: Do the two women receive 25 lakh rupees? On what timeline?
2. **Compliance**: Does the confining party obey the writ, or do the women need police protection to leave?
3. **Re-integration**: Do they access education, employment, independent housing?
4. **Appeal**: Can the confining party appeal to the Supreme Court? If so, the case becomes national precedent.

Habeas corpus is liberty's most powerful remedy. But it is only as strong as enforcement.
