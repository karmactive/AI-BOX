# Allahabad High Court Orders Release of Two Women Confined Since 2021

The Allahabad High Court issued a habeas corpus order on August 6, 2026, directing the release of two women, ages 35 and 20, who have been confined since 2021. The women, both Hindu by birth religion, had converted to Islam. The court found that their confinement violated their constitutional right to personal liberty and freedom of movement.

The judgment is spare and unambiguous. The court declared that the women are "free to reside at any place and with any person of their own choice." The language is intentionally strong. It creates an affirmative right—not merely the absence of formal detention, but the freedom to live where and with whom they choose.

The case involved three actors: the two women, the father of one woman, and the Government of Uttar Pradesh. The court ordered the father and the state government to jointly pay 25 lakh rupees (approximately $30,000 USD) to the women. This financial award is not compensation in a tort sense; it is acknowledgment of the deprivation of liberty and freedom of movement over a five-year period.

## The Factual Basis: Confinement Since 2021

The women's confinement began in 2021. .

Both women were held in a residential setting without legal authority to do so. Their movement was restricted. They were not detained in a prison or police custody under lawful order. They were confined by family members, and the state did not intervene to secure their release.

The habeas corpus petition, filed in 2026, sought their immediate release and freedom of movement. The Allahabad High Court found that the confinement was unlawful and that the women's constitutional rights had been violated.

## Constitutional Violation: Articles 21 and 25

The court's reasoning centers on two constitutional provisions:

**Article 21** guarantees the right to life and personal liberty. The Supreme Court has interpreted this broadly to include freedom of movement, freedom of residence, and bodily autonomy. Unlawful confinement is a paradigmatic violation of Article 21.

**Article 25** guarantees the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate religion. The court found that the confinement was directly motivated by the women's religious conversion—a exercise of the Article 25 right to profess a different faith. Preventing them from leaving or making their own life choices was an attempt to suppress their Article 25 rights through physical confinement.

The relationship between Articles 21 and 25 is central to the judgment. The court recognized that confinement in the name of "family honor" or religious disapproval is a means of suppressing both personal liberty and religious freedom simultaneously.

.

## Judicial Precedent and Jurisprudence

The Allahabad High Court judgment is not novel. Indian courts have consistently held that confinement based on religious conversion is unconstitutional. However, enforcement remains uneven.

.

Constitutional scholars at Delhi University and Jamia Millia Islamia have tracked a pattern: courts recognize the violation, but state governments move slowly to enforce the judgment, and families sometimes relocate or evade compliance.

.

## The Father's Role and Joint Liability

The judgment names the father as a liable party. This leaves open questions about familial authority and state responsibility.

In Indian law, a parent has guardianship authority over minor children but not over adult children. One of the confined women was 35 years old at the time of the order. An adult has the right to choose their religion and residence independent of parental approval.

The judgment against the father reflects the court's position that familial authority does not supersede constitutional rights. The financial award to the women is ordered jointly against the father and the state, implying that both bear responsibility—the father for the confinement, the state for failing to intervene.

.

## State Government Responsibility

The Uttar Pradesh government's joint liability in the judgment suggests the court found state complicity or negligent failure to intervene. .

Uttar Pradesh has been the subject of ongoing debate regarding anti-conversion legislation. The state's "Prohibition of Unlawful Conversion Act" criminalizes religious conversion if deemed to involve force, allurement, or coercion. The act is controversial because "allurement" is vaguely defined and difficult to distinguish from the persuasion inherent in religious practice.

.

## Post-Judgment Safety and Retaliation Risks

A critical gap between judgment and lived reality is post-judgment safety. The court order declares that the women are free to reside where they choose, but execution depends on implementation.

.

Women who convert religions in conservative family contexts face social ostracism, economic dependence, and risk of violence. The court's judgment provides legal protection but does not automatically ensure physical safety or economic security.

.

## Comparative Jurisprudence: India vs. Other Jurisdictions

Other democracies have addressed similar issues. .

The Allahabad High Court's judgment aligns with international human rights standards recognizing freedom of religion as a fundamental right that supersedes familial or cultural authority.

## Conclusion

The Allahabad High Court's August 6 order reflects a clear affirmation that confinement based on religious conversion violates fundamental constitutional rights. The judgment names state and family as jointly responsible and imposes both a release order and financial liability.

The practical outcome—whether the women are secure, safe from retaliation, and able to exercise their freedom to reside and associate as they choose—remains to be observed. The court has spoken. Implementation depends on state machinery, family cooperation, and community acceptance.
