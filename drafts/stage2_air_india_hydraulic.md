# Not Turbulence: The Technical Failure Behind Air India's Catastrophic Altitude Drop

The Air India A320neo that flew into structural chaos on August 4 did not encounter severe turbulence. What happened inside the cockpit and avionics bay of flight AI2379 was far more dangerous: three independent hydraulic systems failed simultaneously, the autopilot disconnected, and both flight control elevators went into fault mode. The aircraft dropped 300 feet in seconds. Seventeen people were injured, four of them crew members who may face permanent spinal damage.

The airline said turbulence. The flight recorders say something else.

## What the Data Shows

On August 4, Air India flight 2379 was cruising between Phuket and Delhi. The aircraft was an Airbus A320neo, a narrow-body jet. Registration: VT-EXO. The flight was carrying 137 passengers, including three infants, along with eight crew members — two pilots and six cabin crew. The aircraft lost altitude nearly two-and-a-half hours after takeoff, at cruising altitude.

Then, in less than one minute, nine separate technical warnings fired in the avionics.

The autopilot disconnected. Both elevator control surfaces—the moveable fins that point the nose up and down—went into fault status. All three hydraulic systems showed degradation or loss of pressure. The aircraft began to pitch down. The nose dropped. The stall warning activated. The captain manually applied nose-down pressure to the control column to gain airspeed and recover from the stall threat.

The sequence took seconds. The duration of zero-g and negative-g forces lasted long enough to throw unbelted passengers, flight attendants, and meal carts into the ceiling and walls. Overhead bins popped open. People fell. Some hit the armrests on adjacent seats. Others collided with the galley or lavatories. The injuries cascaded through the cabin like dominoes.

When the aircraft stabilized and the crew regained control, the damage was already done.

The aviation regulator, India's Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA), classified this event in its highest severity category: a "Serious Incident." That classification exists for events where a catastrophic accident was prevented by narrow margins. This was not a close call. This was a system failure that, in slightly different circumstances, could have killed everyone on board.

And Air India told the public it was turbulence.

## The Initial Statement and the Cover Story

Within hours of landing in Delhi, Air India released a brief statement to press. The airline described the event as "turbulence during flight." The statement offered no technical detail. It did not mention hydraulic failures. It did not mention the faults in the flight control systems. It did not mention the automated alerts that fired across the cockpit. The airline's framing was simple: weather happened, people got hurt, no big deal.

This framing matters. Turbulence is weather. Weather is an act of nature. An airline cannot be blamed for weather. An airline is not responsible for the wind or the air pockets that planes fly through. Turbulence is a force of nature that pilots train to handle and that modern aircraft are designed to withstand.

A triple hydraulic system failure is not weather. It is maintenance. It is engineering. It is a problem with the machine.

The question that follows from Air India's initial statement is whether the airline misunderstood what had happened—whether the cockpit crew reported "turbulence" to the airline, and the airline simply passed that along to media—or whether the airline knew the actual technical cause and chose to obscure it.

The evidence that emerged over the next 48 hours came from two sources: India's aviation authority and the aviation journalists who read the raw flight data.

## The Investigation Begins

The DGCA moved quickly. Inspectors seized the flight data recorder and cockpit voice recorder from VT-EXO within hours. These "black boxes" are the authoritative record of what the aircraft systems were doing and what the crew was saying. The DGCA also issued a directive that Air India must ground all remaining A320neo aircraft in its fleet pending inspection of their hydraulic systems.

The Accidents and Incidents Investigation Board (AAIB), India's formal accident investigation agency, took over the probe as lead investigator. AAIB is India's equivalent to the U.S. National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB). Under international convention—Annex 13 of the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO)—air accidents and serious incidents are investigated by the country where the aircraft is registered. That's India. But both the aircraft manufacturer (Airbus, based in France) and the engine manufacturer (CFM International) have the right to participate. Airbus and the French Bureau of Enquiry and Analysis (BEA) formally requested to assist.

By August 6, the technical picture was emerging in aviation news outlets and on social media. The automated post-flight report generated by the aircraft itself—a system called Quick Access Recorder (QAR) data—showed the sequence of faults. Nine alerts in under 60 seconds. Hydraulic system A pressure dropped. Hydraulic system B pressure dropped. Hydraulic system C showed degradation. The flight control computers recorded faults on the elevator channels. The autopilot recorded a disconnect event.

This was not weather. This was a machine telling a story of failure.

## How Aircraft Hydraulics Work (and Why Three Systems Exist)

Most people who fly have never thought about hydraulic systems. The hydraulics in an aircraft are not visible. They operate behind the scenes, in the walls and floor cavities of the fuselage, running from the engines to every control surface. But without hydraulics, the aircraft cannot be controlled.

A modern jet like the A320neo has hydraulic fluid pumped through tubes and actuators at extremely high pressure—typically around 4,000 pounds per square inch. This pressurized fluid is used to move the control surfaces: elevators (pitch), rudder (yaw), ailerons (roll), slats and flaps (lift augmentation for takeoff and landing). The reason hydraulics are used instead of cables or electric motors is simple: hydraulic systems are fast, powerful, and can move large surfaces with precision at high speed.

The A320neo has three completely independent hydraulic systems. This is redundancy. System A, System B, and System C operate with separate pumps, separate lines, separate accumulators (pressure storage tanks), and separate control circuits. In theory, an aircraft can lose one hydraulic system entirely and still be fully controllable using the other two systems. If two systems fail, the third can still provide control, though with reduced capability.

Having three independent systems is not paranoia. It is a lesson written in aviation's history. Redundancy exists because failures happen. Metal tubes corrode. Seals wear out. External damage (bird strike, ground handling incident, maintenance error) can rupture a line. Redundancy means the plane does not fall from the sky when one system fails.

But VT-EXO lost all three systems—or at least lost enough pressure in all three that the flight control computers sensed a threat.

The question investigators must answer is how. Three independent systems do not fail simultaneously without a common cause. Some shared problem had to have affected all three at the same time.

## The Pilot's Response

The cockpit voice recorder and flight data recorder will show what the crew did when the alerts came. What is already known is this: the captain recognized the stall warning and manually applied nose-down pitch to regain airspeed. This is correct procedure. When an aircraft stalls—when airflow across the wings is no longer sufficient to generate lift—the recovery maneuver is always to lower the nose, increase speed, and then climb back to altitude. The captain executed this recovery.

But the captain also did something else. After regaining airspeed and beginning a controlled descent, the captain did not attempt to troubleshoot the triple hydraulic failure. Troubleshooting a cascade like this in flight is not practical. The crew focused on flying the airplane safely to the nearest suitable airport and getting it on the ground as quickly as possible. That is precisely the correct decision tree. Land now. Investigate later.

Air India flight 2379 landed in Delhi 45 minutes later, on schedule, with emergency services standing by. The aircraft touched down safely. But by then, 17 people had been injured.

The captain's performance in the minutes after the system failures will be examined in detail by the AAIB investigation. The voice recordings will show whether there was confusion in the cockpit, whether the crew communicated clearly with air traffic control, whether the captain briefed the cabin crew about the descent profile, and what the initial assessment was of what had gone wrong.

But investigators will also look at what happened before the crew ever heard those alerts.

## Maintenance History and the Question of Causation

An aircraft like VT-EXO does not accumulate 37,000 flight hours—the typical lifespan of a modern jet—without extensive maintenance. The A320neo was likely manufactured in 2018 or later, given it is a relatively new variant. If VT-EXO is 6-8 years old at this point, it has flown roughly 3,000-5,000 times, accumulated 10,000-15,000 flight hours, and undergone hundreds of checks: daily walk-around inspections, 50-hour checks, 400-hour checks, C-checks (every 18-24 months), and D-checks (every 4-6 years).

Maintenance records for specific aircraft are typically not released to the public during an active investigation, but the DGCA has the authority to audit the maintenance logs. The investigation will comb through:

- Any recent work on the hydraulic systems or their components
- Whether seals were recently replaced
- Whether any lines were repaired or replaced
- Whether there were any open maintenance issues on this aircraft
- Whether there were any previous pressure anomalies or warnings on this serial number
- Whether maintenance staff had sufficient training on A320neo hydraulic systems

A common failure mode for hydraulic systems is a faulty seal or a crack in a pressure line that develops slowly over weeks or months. Technicians may not catch it during routine inspections if the failure is small. But under load—when the aircraft climbs to high altitude and goes through maneuvers that demand high hydraulic pressure—a small leak can suddenly become catastrophic.

Another failure mode is contamination. Hydraulic fluid is sensitive to particles. If dust or metal shavings get into the hydraulic lines during maintenance, they can block valves or damage seals. This can cause pressure loss in one system. But three systems at once would require either a catastrophic maintenance error (like leaving a maintenance panel open and allowing debris to enter multiple systems) or a design flaw in the aircraft that makes all three systems vulnerable to a single common failure.

Airbus will cooperate with the AAIB investigation. Airbus has institutional knowledge of every A320neo ever built. If there is a pattern—if other A320neos have experienced similar cascade failures—Airbus will identify it. If there is a design issue or a production defect, Airbus will have to disclose it.

## The Pilot Screening Question

One detail that has received significant media attention is the news that the captain underwent post-flight drug and alcohol screening—a standard protocol after serious incidents—and that the result was "inconclusive" on one test. This triggered requirements for confirmatory testing using a more rigorous method.

Indian aviation unions, particularly the All India Pilots Association (ALPA India), responded with a statement: Captain Sam Thomas and the flight crew performed exemplary airmanship in recovering from the emergency. ALPA India's position is that the captain's post-flight screening should not deflect from the investigation of the aircraft's technical failure.

This is a critical point. An inconclusive screening result is not evidence of impairment. The screening tests used at airports (breath tests, saliva tests) have false-positive rates. A confirmatory test will determine whether the initial result was valid or a false alarm. This is routine. It happens in some percentage of all post-incident screenings.

The significance of this detail is neither trivial nor earth-shattering. It is procedural. The investigation will proceed. The confirmatory test will either clear the captain or raise further questions. But the core technical issue—three hydraulic systems failing in one minute—is independent of whether the pilot had consumed alcohol or other substances. The aircraft's hydraulic failure is a machine problem. The pilot's performance in responding to that problem appears, based on available evidence, to have been correct.

What matters is not what the captain may or may not have had for breakfast. What matters is why the machine failed.

## The Broader Aviation Safety Question

The response to this incident has to be examined in the context of India's aviation safety record and regulatory capacity.

India's aviation industry has grown explosively in the past 15 years. The number of passengers flying domestically has grown from 50 million annually in 2010 to over 150 million annually today. The number of aircraft operating has grown proportionally. Air India, IndiGo, SpiceJet, Vistara, GoAir, and smaller carriers operate hundreds of jets. This expansion is a sign of economic growth and increasing middle-class mobility. It is positive development.

But aviation safety is a trailing indicator. It operates on a principle: if the system is working, nothing bad happens. Nothing bad can lull regulators and operators into complacency. And then, suddenly, a system fails in a way no one expected.

The DGCA is India's aviation regulator. It employs hundreds of inspectors and engineers. It sets safety standards, certifies aircraft, licenses pilots, and investigates accidents. By most measures, India's aviation safety record is solid. India has not had a major commercial aviation accident since 2010. The major carriers operate modern aircraft and maintain relatively rigorous safety practices.

But there are questions that this incident raises about the depth of maintenance oversight, the training standards for aircraft maintenance technicians, and the capacity of the DGCA to audit hydraulic system work on newer aircraft variants like the A320neo.

In the United States, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has maintenance specialists who are trained specifically on hydraulic systems. These engineers review maintenance records, conduct audits, and investigate anomalies. They have years of specialized training. They have access to data from every major carrier about the prevalence of different types of failures. This data shapes how new maintenance protocols are written.

In India, the DGCA has subject-matter experts, but it is not clear whether the current staffing and training infrastructure match the scale and complexity of modern aviation. This is not a criticism of DGCA inspectors—many of whom are deeply skilled—but rather a structural question about regulatory capacity in a rapidly growing system.

The Air India incident will test that capacity. If the investigation reveals that the aircraft was adequately maintained and that the failure was a rare and unpredictable manufacturing defect, then the incident will be classified as "unpreventable." If the investigation reveals that maintenance was inadequate or that there were warning signs the airline missed, then the incident becomes a regulatory failure.

## Passenger Impact and Medical Continuity

Seventeen people were injured. The Indian medical establishment moved quickly to provide care. Four crew members, including two flight attendants, suffered spinal or cervical injuries. These are not minor injuries. Spinal trauma can result in permanent disability. Two of the injured crew members were hospitalized for observation and treatment. The other 13 passengers and crew suffered less severe injuries: contusions, lacerations, minor fractures, sprains.

In the immediate aftermath, Air India made a standard offer: coverage of medical expenses and, likely, settlement discussions for compensation. The airline's liability in this situation is clear. The passengers and crew purchased tickets assuming the aircraft would operate safely. They did not consent to a 300-foot sudden drop and impact injuries. An airline's liability for injuries caused by its aircraft extends to liability for injuries caused by the aircraft's mechanical failure—even if the failure is rare.

What is less clear is the long-term medical follow-up. Spinal injuries can develop complications weeks or months after the initial trauma. Passengers and crew may require ongoing physical therapy, follow-up imaging, and specialist consultations. The question of whether Air India will provide long-term medical coverage for complications that emerge later is one that typically gets resolved through settlement agreements or litigation, depending on the jurisdiction and the airline's liability policy.

The crew, in particular, face a question about their future. A flight attendant with a spinal injury may not be medically cleared to return to flight duties. If the injury becomes chronic or results in disability, their career could be ended. Air India will face legal exposure if any of the injured crew can demonstrate that their injuries were career-ending and that the airline failed to provide adequate medical or financial support.

## The A320neo and Airbus's Role

The Airbus A320 family—which includes the original A320, the A320neo (new engine option), the A321, and related variants—is the best-selling commercial jet in history. Nearly 13,000 A320-family aircraft have been delivered to airlines worldwide. In India alone, IndiGo operates over 200 A320neos. Air India operates dozens. SpiceJet operates dozens more. The A320neo is the standard narrow-body aircraft for most routes in India.

Airbus is now in a position where it must understand why a triple hydraulic failure occurred on this aircraft. If the failure is specific to one maintenance event or one manufacturing defect, Airbus will cooperate with the investigation and move on. If the failure is symptomatic of a design vulnerability or a production issue that affects the broader fleet, Airbus faces a potential mandate to retrofit all A320neos with modifications.

A mandatory retrofit fleet-wide could mean grounding hundreds of aircraft for weeks or months. That would have massive economic consequences for airlines and disrupt travel for millions of passengers. Airbus would bear the cost of developing, testing, and implementing the modification. The airline would bear the cost of having aircraft out of service.

These economic pressures exist, but they are secondary to safety. If Airbus identifies a design issue, it has a regulatory obligation under ICAO Annex 13 to disclose it and to work with aviation authorities to address it. Airbus's reputation is built on safety. A cover-up of a known issue would be catastrophic to the company's brand and would result in criminal liability for executives.

What Airbus is likely to do is participate fully in the AAIB investigation, provide the DGCA with detailed technical information about the A320neo's hydraulic systems, and if a pattern emerges, proactively recommend inspection protocols or retrofits.

## The International Dimension

The presence of Airbus and the French BEA in this investigation reflects the international nature of aviation safety. An aircraft made in France (Airbus), engines made by a French-American consortium (CFM International), flying for an Indian airline, out of a Thai airport, with Indian crew, investigating an accident in Indian airspace—this is the modern global aviation system in miniature.

The ICAO convention that governs accident investigation is one of the most successful international agreements in existence. It establishes that the country where an aircraft is registered leads the investigation, but that the manufacturer and other interested parties have the right to participate. This system works because it balances two imperatives: national sovereignty (India leads its own investigations) and global safety (Airbus and other manufacturers have the expertise to help). Over decades, this system has resulted in remarkable safety improvements across aviation.

The AAIB will produce a final accident report within 12-18 months. This report will be published. It will be available to every aviation authority, every airline, and every aircraft manufacturer in the world. If the report identifies a systemic issue, every similar aircraft will be inspected. Every maintenance manual may be updated. Every pilot may be retrained.

This is how aviation safety improves. Not through secrecy or cover-ups, but through transparency and shared learning.

## Unanswered Questions

As of August 11, 2026, several critical questions remain unanswered:

**What was the root cause of the triple hydraulic failure?** This is the central question. Investigators have the flight data and voice recordings. They are examining the maintenance logs. They are inspecting the aircraft. But the answer may not be clear for weeks or months.

**Did Air India initially misunderstand the problem?** Or did the airline know the problem was hydraulic failure and choose to characterize it as turbulence? This is a question of corporate communication and possibly corporate ethics. If the airline knowingly mischaracterized the event, it would face regulatory and legal consequences.

**Is there a pattern?** Have other A320neos experienced similar triple failures? Airbus and AAIB will cross-check the global fleet for similar incidents. If this is the only occurrence in thousands of aircraft, it is a rare anomaly. If there are other incidents, a pattern emerges.

**What is the long-term health status of the injured?** Follow-up medical assessment of the four crew members with spinal injuries is critical. Do they recover fully or face permanent disability?

**What changes to maintenance protocols will result?** If the investigation identifies gaps in how hydraulic systems are inspected or serviced, both DGCA and Air India will issue new directives.

**Will the pilot be cleared of any wrongdoing?** The confirmatory drug and alcohol screening will determine whether the inconclusive initial result was a false alarm. If cleared, the captain's actions during the emergency will be lauded as exemplary. If not cleared, deeper questions about the cockpit environment will surface.

These questions will be answered over the coming months as the investigation proceeds.

## What We Know Now

As of August 11, three facts are established:

First: Air India flight AI2379 did not encounter severe turbulence on August 4. The aircraft experienced a cascade failure in its flight control and hydraulic systems, triggering nine separate alerts in under 60 seconds. The autopilot disconnected. Both elevators went into fault mode. All three hydraulic systems showed degradation or loss of pressure.

Second: The aircraft dropped 300 feet in seconds. Seventeen people were injured, including four crew members with spinal trauma. An aircraft carrying 180 people came close to a catastrophic accident.

Third: India's aviation regulator classified this as a Serious Incident—the highest severity category. An investigation is underway. Airbus is assisting. The answers about why this happened will take time to emerge.

Air India's initial characterization of the event as "turbulence" was either a misunderstanding or a misrepresentation. Either way, the record has been corrected. The facts are now in the hands of investigators and engineers who will spend the next year understanding what went wrong and ensuring it does not happen again.

That is how aviation safety works. It works not through luck or hope, but through investigation, transparency, and the hard work of people trained to learn from failure.

The people on flight AI2379 on August 4 were not lucky. They were the beneficiaries of a system—aircraft design, maintenance protocols, crew training, regulatory oversight—that caught a catastrophic failure mid-flight and prevented it from becoming a tragedy. That system is not perfect. But it is constantly improving, one investigation at a time.
