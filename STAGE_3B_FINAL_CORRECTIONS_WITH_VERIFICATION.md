## ARTICLE 4: NOAA GEOMAGNETIC STORM & AURORA FORECAST — CORRECTED FINAL VERSION

**Headline:** The sky has two different spectacles on the calendar: a geomagnetic storm may expand the Northern Lights viewing zone, while a partial lunar eclipse is also taking place—but they are not the same event.

#### What Is a Geomagnetic Storm?

A geomagnetic storm is a temporary disturbance in Earth's magnetic field caused by activity from the Sun. When the Sun releases energy through solar flares and throws charged particles into space through coronal mass ejections, those particles can reach Earth and interact with our planet's magnetic field.

On a technical scale, geomagnetic storms are rated from G1 (Minor) to G5 (Extreme). Higher numbers mean more intense disturbances with broader geographic impact.

#### NOAA's August 2026 Forecast

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) issued geomagnetic storm watches for August 27 through August 29, 2026.

- **August 27:** G1 (Minor) geomagnetic storm watch
- **August 28–29:** G2 (Moderate) geomagnetic storm watch

#### What Caused This Geomagnetic Storm?

On August 25, 2026, the Sun experienced an eruption from one of its active regions. This eruption produced an M6.9-class solar flare and an associated Coronal Mass Ejection (CME). The charged particles from this CME traveled toward Earth, and Coronal Hole High-Speed Streams (CH HSS) also interacted with Earth's magnetosphere.

**[VERIFICATION: NOAA's forecast discussion confirms M6.9 flare and independent solar-weather monitoring corroborates the event]**

#### How Do Scientists Monitor This?

NOAA operates sophisticated space-weather monitoring instruments aboard satellites positioned at strategic points. Real-time coronagraph tracking of the August 25 solar events was captured by the Compact Coronagraph 2 (CCOR-2) instrument aboard the SOLAR-1 spacecraft.

The SOLAR-1 spacecraft operates near the Sun-Earth L1 point, approximately 1.5 million kilometers from Earth. This location allows upstream observations of solar-wind and coronal activity.

**[CORRECTION 16: CRITICAL FIX - Original stated "about 1 million miles from Earth where the gravitational pull of Earth and Sun balance, creating a stable observation point" which is technically inaccurate. SOLAR-1 operates in a Lissajous orbit around L1, not simply at a point where gravitational forces balance. Corrected to accurate 1.5 million km distance and proper description of its observational position]**

Data from SOHO (Solar and Heliospheric Observatory) and LASCO (Large Angle and Spectrometric Coronagraph) provided additional confirmation.

#### Where Can You See the Aurora?

Under G1 (Minor) conditions, aurora is typically visible at high northern latitudes. Under G2 (Moderate) conditions, the auroral oval expands southward.

**Potential visibility areas in the United States include:**

Northern Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, North Dakota, Montana, Idaho, portions of upstate New York, and Northern New England.

The key factor is geomagnetic latitude. Areas at 55° and above geomagnetic latitude have viewing chances during G2 conditions.

For viewers in India: During this event, India is outside the expected aurora-viewing zone.

**[CORRECTION 17: Changed from "India is at too low a geomagnetic latitude to see auroras during a G2 event" to "India is outside the expected aurora-viewing zone" - avoids making an absolute generalization about all G2 events]**

Optimal aurora visibility depends on darkness, local geomagnetic activity, cloud cover, latitude, and the timing of the disturbance's arrival. Viewing conditions vary significantly by location and changing space weather conditions.

**[CORRECTION 18: CRITICAL FIX - Removed "Optimal viewing window: 10 PM to 2 AM local standard time" presented as absolute. This is too specific and unsourced. If you're photographing aurora:

#### What About the Lunar Eclipse?

A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the Sun and Moon, casting Earth's shadow on the Moon.

NASA classifies the August 28, 2026 event as a **partial lunar eclipse**—the Moon enters Earth's shadow but Earth's shadow does not completely cover the Moon.

**Visibility from India:**

The partial phase of the lunar eclipse begins around 2:33 AM IST (Indian Standard Time) on August 28. Maximum eclipse occurs around 4:12 AM IST. The Moon will set before the global eclipse sequence completes, so Indian observers will see only the early partial phases.

**[VERIFICATION: NASA confirms August 27–28 partial lunar eclipse classification and timing]**

#### Are the Geomagnetic Storm and Lunar Eclipse Connected?

No. They occur at approximately the same time but are independent phenomena. A geomagnetic storm results from solar activity reaching Earth's magnetosphere. A lunar eclipse results from Earth's orbital position relative to the Sun and Moon.

#### What Could Geomagnetic Storms Affect Beyond the Sky?

Stronger geomagnetic storms can affect:

- **Power grids:** Induced electrical currents can cause fluctuations
- **Satellite operations:** Increased atmospheric drag and radiation can affect satellite function
- **Radio propagation:** High-frequency radio communications can be disrupted
- **Navigation systems:** GPS and other positioning systems may experience temporary inaccuracy

**[CORRECTION 20: Changed from "generally minor and temporary" to simply listing the effects. "Generally minor" is too broad; G1/G2 impacts vary by infrastructure, location, and design]**

#### Summary

NOAA SWPC projected G1 to G2 geomagnetic storm conditions for August 27–29, 2026, resulting from an M6.9 solar flare and coronal mass ejection that erupted on August 25. These conditions enable potential aurora visibility across northern US latitudes. Separately, a partial lunar eclipse occurs on August 28, 2026, but this is an independent phenomenon from the geomagnetic storm.
