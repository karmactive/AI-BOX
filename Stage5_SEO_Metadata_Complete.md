## FULL ARTICLE WITH LINKS

When a power line sparks a wildfire that destroys your home, someone has to pay for it. California is in the middle of a major fight over who that someone should be. The state's governor, lawmakers, electric companies, and insurance firms are divided over how wildfire costs should be allocated.

Right now, California law says utilities like [PG&E](https://www.pge.com/), [Southern California Edison](https://www.sce.com/), and [San Diego Gas & Electric](https://www.sdge.com/) can be held strictly responsible for damages if their equipment causes a wildfire, even if the utility wasn't careless. This rule is called [inverse condemnation](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=PUC&division=2.5.&title=&part=1.&chapter=11.&article=). When a wildfire hits, homeowners get insurance payouts from their insurance companies. Then those insurers can turn around and sue the utility to get their money back. That recovery process is called [subrogation](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/), and it's central to how wildfire costs currently get sorted out.

Governor Newsom wants to change this. His proposal would cap or restrict how much insurance companies can recover from utilities. The administration argues that reducing utilities' liability exposure would help limit financial pressure on utilities and reduce the risk of higher electricity costs.

But here's the catch. If insurers can't recover money from utilities, they face financial losses. [Insurers and consumer advocates have warned that limiting their ability to recover wildfire losses could increase pressure on an already strained California insurance market](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/consumers/applications/residential-property-insurance).

The [Eaton Fire that started in January 2025](https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/) sparked this latest political fight. The fire killed 19 people and destroyed 9,419 structures with another 1,076 damaged. Nine firefighters were injured. Those numbers show why this debate matters—these aren't theoretical fires. These are real events destroying real homes and taking real lives.

California created the [$21 billion Wildfire Fund in 2019 under AB 1054](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=201920SB1167) as part of a broader response to the financial risks exposed by the state's catastrophic wildfires and PG&E's bankruptcy. The fund has specific limits and processes. It's not unlimited money. [The real question now is whether the fund should cover more costs](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/), or whether utilities should carry more of the burden.

In Sacramento, lawmakers are divided. Legislators have also debated restrictions on the sale of wildfire claims to third parties, including financial firms. Cities and counties want to recover the full replacement cost when municipal buildings burn. The administration initially proposed limiting them to depreciated value, which would be much less. After pushback from fire departments and local government associations, the governor's office signaled it might budge on that point.

California's legislative session ends August 31. This needs to be decided soon. As of late August, no final bill text has been released. Negotiations are still happening. Lawmakers will be voting on proposals, with final language still evolving.

This matters beyond California. [Other states watch what California does](https://www.nga.org/). That could shift wildfire costs across the country depending on California's approach.

What remains unknown is whether reducing utility liability would actually lower electricity bills. The administration says it would prevent rate hikes. This is the central unresolved question: does cost savings for one customer base end up as cost increases for another?

A federal judge in [New York](https://www.nycourts.gov/) has rejected Donald Trump's latest attempt to escape his Manhattan criminal conviction by moving the case to federal court. The ruling, handed down August 28, closes one legal door but leaves the rest of his legal troubles wide open.

Trump was convicted in May 2024 on [34 counts of falsifying business records](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_19m2b.pdf). The charges stemmed from a $130,000 payment made by Michael Cohen to Stormy Daniels, which prosecutors said was directed by Trump before the 2016 election.

After the [Supreme Court ruled in Trump's favor on presidential immunity last summer](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_19m2b.pdf), his lawyers saw an opening. They argued that because some evidence and testimony used in the state case involved conduct related to the presidential period, the whole case should move to federal court where presidential immunity might provide better protection. The underlying hush-money payment and the business-record entries at issue date to 2016, before Trump became president.

But U.S. District Judge [Alvin Hellerstein](https://www.nycourts.gov/courts/1jd/supctmanh/index.shtml) wasn't convinced. Under [federal law, removal to federal court after a state jury trial and verdict requires demonstrating good cause and continuous diligence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1455). Trump's legal team missed that threshold. The judge found their arguments weren't new—they'd essentially made the same points before. The legal standard for post-trial removal simply wasn't met.

More importantly, the judge rejected Trump's argument that the evidence and conduct at issue were protected by presidential immunity. The [Supreme Court's immunity decision protected official presidential acts](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_19m2b.pdf)—things like national security decisions or constitutional powers. Conduct in 2016 involving personal payments and business records doesn't fit that definition. It's private conduct, not official conduct.

Trump's conviction stands. His state-court case remains in New York courts. He can continue appealing through the [state appeals process](https://www.nycourts.gov/courts/1jd/supctmanh/index.shtml). The judge made clear that this particular legal strategy won't work. The pathway to federal court has been blocked, but Trump's legal challenges will continue through state courts.

This is a setback for Trump's legal team, but it's not the final word on everything. It's simply one court saying no to one specific request.

The [Old Farmer's Almanac](https://www.almanac.com/), continuously published since 1792, is predicting a winter for the books in 2026-27. But predicting winter 235 years into a publication's run is more science than guesswork.

The forecast breaks down by region. The [Northeast is expected to see colder temperatures and above-normal snowfall](https://www.weather.gov/wrh/Climate). The [upper Midwest and plains states face frigid conditions](https://www.weather.gov/). The [Southwest will remain mild and drier than normal](https://www.weather.gov/abq/). Much of the [West is forecast to have wetter-than-normal conditions](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/90day/), while portions of the Southwest are forecast to be drier than normal.

So how does a 235-year-old publication predict winter this far out? The [Almanac uses solar science](https://www.almanac.com/gardening/frostdates), historical weather patterns, and modern meteorology combined into its forecasting methodology. The publication itself claims 80% accuracy in its long-range forecasts. That's better than many people expect from seasonal predictions, though it's still short of certainty.

By contrast, the [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)](https://www.noaa.gov/) uses different tools. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center relies on ocean temperatures, atmospheric patterns, and climate models to produce official U.S. government forecasts. [NOAA's approach shows 90%+ probability of very strong El Niño conditions](https://origin.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/ensoyears.shtml), which influences global weather patterns.

The important distinction: both forecasters are looking at the same underlying climate patterns—specifically El Niño—but interpret those patterns differently using their own methods. This is where seasonal forecasting gets complicated. Different legitimate approaches can produce different results.

What that means for you: The Almanac's regional breakdown gives you guidance, but treat it as one data point, not absolute truth. If you live in the Northeast and the forecast calls for heavy snow, it's sensible to prep for that possibility. If you're in the Southwest expecting mild conditions, don't be caught off-guard if an unusual cold snap arrives. Weather forecasting, even for patterns, involves uncertainty.

The [Almanac's methodology](https://www.almanac.com/) combines centuries of observation with modern climate science. This hybrid approach has earned it credibility among millions of readers. But no seasonal forecast, whether from the Almanac or NOAA, guarantees specific outcomes. They're probabilities, not certainties. That's the nature of weather prediction at this scale.

For those planning winter projects or property maintenance, these forecasts are worth noting. They're informed predictions based on rigorous science—just not guarantees.

A federal court has ordered an environmental review of a [land exchange proposal involving Yosemite National Park](https://www.nps.gov/yose/), potentially blocking or delaying the transaction as conservation groups challenge the adequacy of federal environmental analysis.

The case centers on a proposed land exchange where private interests would trade parcels near or within the park's management area for federal land. Supporters argue the exchange modernizes land ownership patterns and could benefit park operations. Conservation groups contend the exchange hasn't received proper [environmental scrutiny under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)](https://ceq.doe.gov/).

[The National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/yose/) is responsible for evaluating land exchanges affecting park resources. Federal law requires that significant federal actions—including land disposals—undergo environmental review through preparation of an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) when the action may have significant environmental consequences.

The federal judge found that [the initial environmental analysis was insufficient](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/4321), ordering completion of a more thorough review before the exchange can proceed. This is a procedural victory for the environmental groups that challenged the deal. It doesn't necessarily kill the exchange—it ensures the process follows proper legal requirements.

What triggered the court order? Conservation advocates argued that the agency hadn't adequately addressed impacts on park resources, visitor access, or long-term land management implications. They also raised concerns about compliance with the [Organic Act](https://www.nps.gov/policy/) establishing the National Park Service's mission to protect park resources.

The court's decision reflects a principle underlying all federal land management: [decisions affecting public lands must follow established procedures and consider environmental impacts](https://www.federalregister.gov/) before they're finalized. This doesn't mean land exchanges are prohibited—it means they must be done transparently and with full environmental analysis.

[Yosemite National Park](https://www.nps.gov/yose/), established in 1890, has complex land ownership patterns due to its history. Some parcels are privately held; others are federal. Modernizing these patterns through exchanges makes administrative sense, but only if done with appropriate environmental review.

The [land exchange procedure itself involves coordination between the Park Service, private owners, and sometimes state agencies](https://www.nps.gov/policy/). When federal land is involved, the process must comply with NEPA and related environmental laws. The court's order ensures that happens here.

For the park and the parties involved, the next step is completing the environmental review. Depending on findings, the review might conclude the exchange poses minimal environmental risk, or it might identify conditions that need to be addressed. Either way, the process becomes more rigorous and transparent—which is the point of environmental law.
