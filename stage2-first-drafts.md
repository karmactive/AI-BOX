An ABC Four Corners investigation published September 22 showed that BYD quietly rewrote its Australian privacy terms days before airing, while a separate controlled cybersecurity test demonstrated that a specialist could remotely access secondary systems on a moving BYD Shark 6 via a standard mobile network.

The BYD privacy policy update removed explicit references to "China" and "surveillance" from the Australian consumer terms. ABC reported that the revised document was uploaded just 76 minutes before the company sent it to the Four Corners team.

In the cybersecurity test, CyberCX specialist Mike Hreszczuk accessed a BYD Shark 6 while it was travelling at 30 km/h. He remotely triggered indicator lights, hazard lights, windows, air conditioning, and the infotainment system. He also accessed live cabin audio and location data via the vehicle's mobile network connection. Steering and mechanical brakes sit on a separate physical control network and were not accessible through the same exploit.

BYD is currently Australia's fastest-growing electric vehicle brand. The Shark 6 is a plug-in hybrid ute positioned against petrol-powered commercial vehicles in the fleet and tradie market.

Owners of connected smart vehicles now face a practical trade-off between digital convenience and personal security. While critical safety systems like steering and mechanical braking remain isolated from remote takeover, secondary controls and personal location data can be accessed over standard mobile networks. Drivers who want to limit data transmission can disable cellular connectivity through dashboard settings—though doing so also shuts down live navigation, remote climate pre-conditioning, and over-the-air firmware updates.

### Martha's Rule Expanded to Every NHS Emergency Department Across England

Patients and families in English emergency departments with a worsening relative now have a formal, protected way to push for another medical opinion—even when ward staff have moved on. NHS England announced September 22 that Martha's Rule will extend to every accident and emergency department in England, building on a rollout that already covers 143 acute hospital trusts.

Martha's Rule gives patients, relatives, and hospital staff the right to request an urgent, independent clinical review when they believe a patient's condition is deteriorating and their concerns are not being addressed. The rule was introduced following the death of 13-year-old Martha Mills in 2021, who died from sepsis at King's College Hospital after sustaining a pancreatic injury. Her family's campaign for a formal escalation right led directly to the policy.

Phase 1 of Martha's Rule rolled out to 143 acute trusts across England. The new A&E expansion will be phased across every emergency department in England, completing by March 2028. NHS England confirmed the expansion on September 22, with the Health Secretary acknowledging the family's advocacy in the official statement.

Patients and relatives in English emergency departments now have the formal right to bypass the bedside team and request an immediate review from an independent critical care outreach team. If a family member shows signs of sudden deterioration—confusion, breathing difficulty, or a rapid change in condition that staff are not actively addressing—anyone present can invoke Martha's Rule and compel an external clinical assessment. The process applies in A&E and urgent care settings, not just inpatient wards.

### How the Escalation System Works in Practice

The seven-trust A&E pilot that preceded the national announcement recorded 69 calls. Some resulted in urgent surgery or transfers to intensive care. NHS England also reported that July 2026 produced 1,678 Martha's Rule activations across all participating trusts—the highest monthly total since the rule launched.

The escalation process runs through a dedicated hospital contact point, usually displayed on patient information boards in wards and emergency areas. Patients or relatives state they are requesting a Martha's Rule review. An independent critical care outreach team—separate from the treating clinicians—then assesses the patient's observations, charts, and current treatment plan. For a full step-by-step guide to patient rights in NHS settings, see [Karmactive's NHS patient rights explainer].

Electronic patient record logging of every family escalation is mandatory under the Phase 2 expansion. That requirement prevents verbal dismissals going undocumented, a gap that earlier NHS complaints data identified.

NHS Phase 1 trial data showed that 70% of Martha's Rule calls were resolved through clearer communication between families and clinical teams. The remaining 30% identified genuine clinical deterioration that had not been picked up on routine chart rounds—a figure that directly challenged early concerns that family-activated reviews would overload clinical teams with unnecessary calls.

**How do you trigger Martha's Rule in an NHS hospital?**
Tell ward staff or call the dedicated hospital escalation number displayed on patient information boards. State that you are requesting a Martha's Rule review for a patient whose condition is worsening. An independent critical care team is dispatched to review the patient's vital signs and treatment plan, separately from the team already treating the patient.

The full A&E rollout across England is scheduled to complete by March 2028. NHS trusts face phased compliance deadlines as the expansion progresses. NHS England will publish rollout milestones and compliance data as the March 2028 deadline approaches. Check back for updates as those figures are released.

Moving Triple Zero from Telstra to a government authority would change administrative and operational responsibility but would not directly change the mobile network infrastructure that carries emergency calls. That distinction is central to the industry debate around the inquiry.

### Why Scientists Still Can't Pin Down the Exact Value of Gravity's Fundamental Constant

More than 225 years after the first laboratory measurement, the gravitational constant—known as big G—remains the least precisely known fundamental constant in physics. A NIST result published in April 2026 has re-emerged in science media this week, reviving a long-standing measurement puzzle: why do independent, carefully conducted experiments keep producing slightly different answers for the same number?

The September circulation of this story is a republication of that April result by science news aggregators. This article is framed as an evergreen explainer accordingly. The NIST result, published April 16, 2026, measured the gravitational constant at approximately 6.67387 × 10⁻¹¹ cubic metres per kilogram per second squared, with a published precision value of 6.67366 × 10⁻¹¹ ± 0.00020 × 10⁻¹¹. NIST used small test masses and independently replicated the approach used by France's International Bureau of Weights and Measures in its torsion-balance experiment. NIST's result was again lower than the French measurement, continuing a discrepancy between independent laboratories that has persisted for years.

Big G is used in calculating gravitational forces between objects—from the orbital mechanics of satellites to the large-scale dynamics of galaxies and cosmological models. The measurement discrepancy does not undermine practical space calculations, which use an averaged best-estimate value. But the inability to pin down G to the same precision as other fundamental constants is a genuine open problem in metrology and fundamental physics.

Most physical constants—like the speed of light or the electron's electric charge—have been measured to extreme precision across multiple independent labs with consistent results. Big G is different. Measuring it requires detecting an extraordinarily small gravitational attraction between objects in a laboratory setting. Those measurements are extremely sensitive to environmental interference: seismic vibrations, temperature changes, the gravitational pull of nearby buildings and equipment, and slight variations in how test masses are shaped and positioned.

NIST's April 2026 experiment used small test masses—a different configuration from its previous G measurements. The result contributed to a pattern in the published literature: G measurements from different labs cluster in two distinct groups rather than converging on a single value. That pattern suggests a systematic difference in experimental methodology between the groups rather than simple measurement error—a problem harder to fix than random noise.

NIST independently replicated the BIPM torsion-balance approach and still obtained a lower value than the French result. That means the discrepancy persists even when two labs use the same fundamental method, pointing to subtle differences in experimental conditions rather than a conceptual error in the approach.

**Why is the gravitational constant G so hard to measure?**
Unlike most physical constants, G is determined by detecting a tiny gravitational attraction between laboratory objects. The measurement is extremely sensitive to environmental interference—including seismic vibration, temperature, and nearby structures—and independent labs using different methods consistently produce slightly different results, a discrepancy the physics community has not yet resolved.

The Inishowen Sub Aqua Club carried out the underwater clean-up during the national Big Beach Clean 2026 campaign. The group pulled nine bags of waste weighing a combined 90kg from the seafloor at Leenan Pier. The wider campaign registered more than 350 groups and 7,000 volunteers across Ireland by September, organised by Clean Coasts. Underwater clean-ups form a distinct data category in Clean Coasts' recording system because the litter they target would not be captured by land-based beach collections.

Marine litter that reaches the seabed poses different environmental risks from surface debris. It entangles bottom-dwelling and reef species, creates physical hazards for divers and fishing equipment, and breaks down more slowly into microplastics in cold, low-light conditions. Removing it requires specialist diving skills and equipment, which limits how often it can be done and how many sites can be covered in a single campaign.

The Inishowen team used a search-and-recovery swimline technique during the underwater portion of the clean-up. Divers moved in coordinated parallel lines across the pier floor due to challenging visibility and current conditions before moving to the adjacent beach to continue collecting. That method is used when underwater conditions make individual free-search approaches ineffective. For more on the Big Beach Clean campaign and how to get involved, see [Karmactive's Clean Coasts and Big Beach Clean guide]. For background on how marine litter becomes microplastics, see [Karmactive's ocean plastics explainer].

Marine litter in Irish coastal waters includes fishing gear, rope, packaging, and single-use plastics. Inshore piers and boat ramps are known litter accumulation points because waste discarded from vessels or washed from land settles in the relatively sheltered water near structures rather than dispersing into open water.

The 2026 campaign registered more than 7,000 volunteers nationally, with data on litter types and volumes fed into Clean Coasts' national marine litter database.
