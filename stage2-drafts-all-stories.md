**The four bills and what each one changes**

The legislation consists of SB 907, AB 1546, AB 1685, and AB 1687.

SB 907 is the bill formally called Braun's Law. It requires that when a DUI charge is reduced to a lesser offense through a plea deal, the defendant must be warned about the dangers of impaired driving and the potential seriousness of any future offense. It addresses the so-called "plea-down" situation where someone charged with DUI ends up convicted of a lesser charge without receiving that formal warning.

AB 1546 strengthens penalties for repeat DUI offenders. AB 1685 changes how DMV points are assessed for DUI-related violations. AB 1687 modifies license revocation rules, including extending the third-DUI license revocation period from three years to eight years.

The bills were named partly in recognition of Braun Levi, whose death was connected to a repeat DUI offender. At the time Newsom signed the package, the California Highway Patrol had recorded more than 50,900 DUI arrests in 2026.

**What the new law does — and what it doesn't**

The most important distinction to understand about Braun's Law specifically is what it does not automatically do: a repeat DUI does not automatically become a murder charge under this legislation. SB 907 deals with how plea negotiations are handled and what warnings are given — not with creating new murder statutes for impaired driving offenses.

Under AB 1687, a third DUI now triggers a license revocation period of eight years rather than three. Previously, the three-year period left a meaningful window where repeat offenders could regain driving privileges relatively quickly. The eight-year period changes that calculation significantly for anyone with two prior DUI convictions.

The ignition interlock requirements — devices that prevent a vehicle from starting if the driver has alcohol in their system — are also strengthened under the package, with tighter timelines for installation and less room for delay through court continuances. ## Scientists Identify Drug Target That Reversed Age-Related Muscle Weakness in Aging Mice

Researchers have identified a specific molecular mechanism behind age-related muscle weakness and demonstrated that blocking it improved muscle function in old mice. The study, published in the Journal of Clinical Investigation on September 1, 2026, points to a drug target that could eventually lead to treatments for sarcopenia — the progressive loss of muscle strength that affects most people as they age.

**What the research found**

The study focused on the neuromuscular junction — the connection point between motor nerves and muscle fibers. In older adults, this junction degrades, and the signal from the nerve to the muscle weakens. The researchers found that this degradation correlates with a loss of a protein called NaV1.4 at the junction.

In a series of experiments on aging rodents, they tested whether blocking a different protein called ClC-1 — a chloride channel in muscle cells — could compensate for the NaV1.4 loss. It did. Old mice that received a ClC-1 inhibitor showed measurable improvement in muscle force output compared to untreated old mice. Younger mice did not show the same benefit, which suggests the effect is specifically tied to the aging-related changes rather than a general muscle enhancement. [INTERNAL LINK: what is sarcopenia]

**The human-animal boundary matters here**

This is where careful reading of the research matters. The study included observations of older human subjects, who showed the same neuromuscular junction abnormalities that the researchers found in aging rodents. That parallel supports the relevance of the mechanism to human aging.

However, the actual drug intervention — the ClC-1 inhibitor that improved muscle function — was tested in mice, not in humans. No human clinical trial of a ClC-1 inhibitor for sarcopenia has been reported yet. The finding identifies a promising target and a plausible mechanism. It does not establish that a drug is available, approved, or proven safe and effective for older people. [INTERNAL LINK: clinical trial phases explained]

**Why this approach differs from prior muscle research**

Most prior attempts to treat age-related muscle loss have targeted hormones — testosterone, growth hormone, or myostatin — pathways that often carry cardiovascular and cancer-related risks. The ClC-1 approach works at the level of cellular muscle physiology, specifically targeting the electrical signaling that tells muscle fibers to contract. This means the intervention addresses muscle function directly rather than through systemic hormone manipulation, which is one reason researchers consider it a potentially cleaner therapeutic pathway.

The practical improvement in the mice was in force output — how much strength the muscle could actually generate — not simply in muscle size. Sarcopenia is as much a problem of muscle quality and neurological signaling as it is a problem of muscle mass, so a treatment that improves force generation without requiring large gains in mass could be meaningful for older adults managing frailty.

**What this means right now for older adults**

The honest short answer is: not yet a treatment option. The research is published in a high-quality peer-reviewed journal and represents a real advance in understanding why muscles weaken with age. But moving from an animal model result to a safe, effective, and approved human therapy typically takes years and involves multiple phases of clinical trials.

For people managing sarcopenia or early-stage frailty today, resistance training and adequate protein intake remain the evidence-based interventions with demonstrated benefit. [INTERNAL LINK: resistance training for older adults] A drug that specifically targets the neuromuscular junction may follow — but not yet.

**FAQ**

**Can drugs currently reverse age-related muscle weakness?**
Not yet. The study published in the Journal of Clinical Investigation identified a drug target — the ClC-1 chloride channel — that improved muscle function in aging mice. No ClC-1 inhibitor has been approved or tested in human clinical trials for sarcopenia at this time.

**What causes age-related muscle weakness?**
The Journal of Clinical Investigation study points to degradation at the neuromuscular junction — the connection between motor nerves and muscle fibers — as a key mechanism. Specifically, a reduction in a protein called NaV1.4 at the junction impairs the nerve signal to the muscle, reducing force output.

**What is sarcopenia?**
Sarcopenia is the progressive loss of muscle mass, strength, and function that occurs with aging. It affects most people over 60 to some degree and is associated with falls, frailty, and loss of independence in older adults.
