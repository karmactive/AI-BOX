Critical Status · June 2026

Science Preserved Its Bones. Now We Must Save Its Life.

The vaquita — Earth's rarest marine mammal — has just been digitally preserved by scientists. Meanwhile, Mexico's government is moving to shrink its last protected water by 85%.

Species: Vaquita (Phocoena sinus)  ·  Location: Northern Gulf of California
Population: ~8 individuals  ·  IUCN Status: Critically Endangered

Eight. That's how many vaquita porpoises scientists believe are still alive in the northern Gulf of California. In June 2026, researchers at Florida Atlantic University published the first complete digital skeleton of a vaquita — a high-resolution 3D scan preserving every bone and measurement of a species with no room left to lose a single individual. It is the most complete anatomical record ever made of this animal.

While that scan was being assembled, something else was happening. Mexico's government was reviewing a proposal to shrink the vaquita's last protected waters — the Zero Tolerance Area in the Gulf of California — by 85%. That means reducing 11,000 square kilometers of protected sea to just 225. The IWC issued its first-ever extinction alert for any species over this situation in 2022. Scientists at the IUCN Cetacean Specialist Group have tracked the population since the 1990s. Sea Shepherd has maintained survey operations in the area for years.

The vaquita was not always on the edge. It once shared these waters with gray whales that have faced their own crisis in nearby Baja lagoons, and with the full range of marine life that populated the upper Gulf of California before large-scale commercial fishing changed it. The situation now is the documented result of decades of bycatch, illegal fishing, and the compounding threats that a 2026 study found are accelerating wildlife collapse globally. The digital preservation is real. The remaining question is whether it becomes a record of survival or a record of what was lost.

Timeline
90 Years of Decline, One Moment of Preservation
Scroll or drag to explore key events from first discovery to the June 2026 digital archive.  ← Scroll →
1930s
Population estimated at ~5,000. Vaquita lives undisturbed in the far northern Gulf of California, largely unknown to science.
1958
First scientific description by biologists Kenneth Norris and William McFarland. The species is formally documented for the first time.
1985
IUCN recognizes vaquita as vulnerable. Gillnet bycatch is already identified as a serious and growing risk to the population.
1997
First formal population survey: ~600 remain. Mexico establishes the Upper Gulf of California Biosphere Reserve.
2005
Survey: 280 vaquita. Gillnet bycatch confirmed as primary cause. Totoaba poaching, driven by demand in China, begins to surge.
2008
Mexico bans gillnets across much of the vaquita range. ~200 remain. Enforcement proves difficult in this remote marine area.
2015
Survey: only 97 individuals. Totoaba poaching intensifies severely. Mexican Navy begins patrols in the upper Gulf.
2016
Population drops to ~30. Zero Tolerance Area established. Mexico deploys Navy vessels and removes gillnets from the zone.
2019
Sea Shepherd surveys document 22 vaquita — including calves. Signs of active reproduction recorded for the first time in years.
2022
IWC issues its first-ever extinction alert for any species. ~10 individuals estimated. International pressure on Mexico intensifies.
2024–25
Mexico reviews proposal to cut ZTA by 85% — from 11,000 km² to 225 km². Conservation groups and scientists submit formal objections.
June 2026
FAU publishes complete 3D digital skeleton in Marine Mammal Science. 8 vaquita are believed alive. The anatomical archive is complete.
Why It's Happening
Six Documented Threats — Tap Each to Expand
The vaquita's collapse is not from one cause. Each factor below has been documented in peer-reviewed research and government assessments.
🎣
Gillnet Bycatch
Vaquita drown when caught in nets set for other fish, unable to surface to breathe.
Vaquita are air-breathing mammals. When entangled in gillnets — vertical mesh walls set in the water column — they cannot surface and drown within minutes. Despite bans in 2008 and 2015, illegal gillnet use continues inside the Zero Tolerance Area. A single net set in the wrong place can kill one of the remaining eight individuals. This remains the most direct cause of mortality.

🐟
Totoaba Poaching
Illegal fishing for an equally endangered fish keeps gillnets active in the ZTA.
The totoaba (Totoaba macdonaldi) is itself Critically Endangered and fully protected under CITES. Its swim bladder sells for tens of thousands of dollars on Chinese black markets, where it's used in traditional medicine. Poachers use the same gillnets that catch vaquita as bycatch. The totoaba trade is the economic reason illegal fishing continues despite Navy patrols and international enforcement pressure.

📉
Habitat Reduction Proposal
Mexico's proposed boundary change would shrink the protected zone by 85%.
The current Zero Tolerance Area covers approximately 11,000 square kilometers of the northern Gulf of California. The proposal under review would reduce this to 225 square kilometers — an 85% cut. Scientists have warned this would expose most of the vaquita's remaining habitat to unregulated fishing activity, removing the legal basis for enforcement across the vast majority of the area where the animals live.

🧬
Genetic Bottleneck
At 8 individuals, genetic diversity is extremely low — but not necessarily fatal.
With only 8 known individuals, inbreeding risk is severe. However, a 2021 genetic study found vaquita genomes carry fewer harmful mutations than expected — a possible sign the species adapted to low genetic diversity over a long period. The Northern elephant seal offers a reference: it recovered from an estimated 20–30 individuals in the 1890s to over 170,000 today. Biological recovery from these numbers is rare but documented.

🚢
Enforcement Gaps
The Gulf is large, patrols are limited, and illegal nets keep being found.
Mexico has deployed Navy vessels, surveillance drones, and international observers to the ZTA. Sea Shepherd has maintained a physical presence. Despite this, surveys have repeatedly found illegal gillnets in the water since the ZTA was established in 2016. Continuous enforcement in a remote marine environment is difficult to sustain — and even brief gaps create lethal risk for a population of 8.

🌐
International Demand
Demand for totoaba parts in China is what makes illegal fishing in Mexico profitable.
Totoaba swim bladders command prices exceeding USD $20,000 per kilogram on Chinese black markets. This demand has persisted despite international trade bans, CITES listings, and Chinese government statements of cooperation. Conservation groups have documented trafficking routes from the Gulf of California through the United States to China. Without demand reduction, enforcement alone has not stopped the trade, and the trade is what puts gillnets in the water.

What's Being Done
Active Developments That Bear on Survival
These are documented, current actions. None of them guarantee recovery. All of them are ongoing.
🔬
Complete 3D Digital Record (June 2026)
FAU's publication in Marine Mammal Science gives scientists the most detailed anatomical dataset ever produced for a vaquita specimen. This ensures the species is fully documented in the scientific record, regardless of what happens in the water.
🐣
Documented Reproduction (2019)
Sea Shepherd's 2019 survey documented calves among the 22 individuals sighted. This was direct evidence that the remaining population was still reproducing — a critical biological finding that changed short-term viability assessments. Calves in 2019 would be juveniles or adults today.
⚡
IWC Extinction Alert (2022)
The International Whaling Commission's first-ever species extinction alert created a formal international record that Mexico must address in diplomatic forums. This is a legal mechanism, not a press statement, and it keeps the vaquita on the agenda of international marine governance bodies.
📡
Acoustic Monitoring Network
NOAA Fisheries and partner organizations maintain passive acoustic buoys in the northern Gulf that detect vaquita echolocation clicks. This system tracks individuals without disturbing them and provides the most reliable population data available between visual survey expeditions.

Eight vaquita were documented in the northern Gulf of California in the most recent population assessments. The species exists in one location on Earth — a narrow strip of sea between Baja California and mainland Mexico — and that habitat is now the subject of a proposed 85% reduction in protected coverage.

In June 2026, Florida Atlantic University researchers published a complete 3D digital skeleton of a vaquita specimen in the journal Marine Mammal Science. The International Whaling Commission issued an extinction alert for the species in 2022 — the first time the IWC used that mechanism for any marine mammal. IUCN classifies the vaquita as Critically Endangered. Sea Shepherd has maintained a presence in the Gulf. Illegal totoaba gillnets — the primary source of vaquita bycatch mortality — continue to be documented in the area despite Navy operations.

The species shares its waters with other animals facing pressure in the same region, including gray whales documented losing calves in nearby Baja lagoons. The pattern is part of a wider record — one that a 2026 study on compound threats to wildlife populations addressed directly, and one that connects to the pressures on marine mammal migration routes across the eastern Pacific. The data on the vaquita is as complete as it has ever been. The digital record exists. The living population is eight.
