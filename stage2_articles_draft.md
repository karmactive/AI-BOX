### A 152% Surge in Homes for Sale, But Buyers Can't Afford Them: How to Use Seller Desperation to Your Advantage

There are more homes listed for sale in the United States right now than at any comparable point in recent years. In Nashville, inventory is up 152% year-over-year — the second-highest seller surplus in the country behind Miami, according to Redfin data. In Las Cruces, New Mexico, new listings are tracking 18% above the same period last year. The listings are real. The buyers are largely absent. Mortgage rates hovering near 7% have made the monthly payment on a median-priced home unworkable for the majority of households who don't already have a low-rate mortgage locked in.

The result is a paradox: a market that looks like a buyer's market on paper — more supply, less competition, sellers sitting on stale listings — but functions like a freeze because the cost of financing has priced out the demand that would normally absorb that supply. Redfin's data shows homes priced above $400,000 have seen the sharpest pullback in discretionary purchase activity, as buyers who could stretch to that price point at 3% cannot qualify for the same home at 7%.

This gap is directly actionable for buyers who can still qualify. Sellers who have been sitting on overpriced listings for 60 or 90 days are in a structurally weaker position than they were 18 months ago, and there are specific financing tools that shift part of the rate burden from the buyer to the transaction itself — tools many buyers don't ask for because they don't know they exist.

## Two Tools Buyers Can Demand in This Market

**2-1 rate buydowns.** A 2-1 buydown is a seller-funded concession that temporarily reduces the mortgage interest rate — by 2 percentage points in year one and 1 percentage point in year two — before the rate returns to its contracted level in year three. On a $400,000 loan at a note rate of 7%, a seller-funded 2-1 buydown means the buyer pays at the equivalent of a 5% rate in year one and 6% in year two. The seller funds the cost of the buydown at closing, typically between $8,000 and $14,000 depending on loan size. In a market where sellers are already cutting prices, a buydown often costs the seller less than a price reduction while delivering more monthly relief to the buyer. [INTERNAL LINK: mortgage rates and home buying guide]

**Float-down locks.** A rate lock commits the lender to a specific rate for a set period — typically 30 to 60 days — while a purchase is in contract. A float-down provision added to that lock allows the buyer to capture a lower rate if rates fall before closing, without being exposed to rate increases if rates rise. In a market where the Federal Reserve's rate path remains uncertain, a float-down lock costs a small premium upfront but removes the timing risk that causes buyers to delay. Not all lenders offer float-down provisions; asking at the pre-approval stage, before entering contract, is the only way to access them.

For buyers considering either tool, the starting point is the seller's days-on-market. A listing that has been active for more than 45 days in a 152% inventory surplus market is a listing where a buydown or closing cost concession request is unlikely to be rejected outright.

Energy efficiency upgrades are a related factor for buyers using FHA or VA-backed financing: both programs offer energy efficiency mortgages that can add efficiency improvements — insulation, HVAC, windows — to the loan balance at close, which partially offsets higher operating costs when oil and heating prices are elevated.
