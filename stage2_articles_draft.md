## Women Set to Receive Higher State Pension Than Men From 2027, Expert Says

Women approaching State Pension age have a straightforward question right now: will they actually be paid more than men? For years the answer was no — women routinely received less. That is expected to flip as early as next year, according to pension experts, driven by changes to the State Pension system introduced a decade ago. Here is what the latest data show and what it means for your own entitlement.

**What the current figures show**

The Department for Work and Pensions published its latest statistics showing that at March 2026, men receiving the new State Pension were paid an average of £217.99 a week, while women received £214.54. The gap has narrowed dramatically. Under the old system, men routinely received far more because payments were partly tied to lifetime earnings.

The full new State Pension for 2026/27 stands at £241.30 a week — the same ceiling for men and women alike. Whether you reach that amount depends on how many qualifying National Insurance years you have built up, not your gender.

Pension expert Sir Steve Webb, who helped design the current system as Pensions Minister, told reporters he expects women to receive higher average payments than men from 2027 onwards. That expectation is based on demographic trends, not an official Department for Work and Pensions forecast.

**Why the crossover is expected to happen**

The new State Pension replaced the old system in April 2016. The old arrangement included the State Earnings-Related Pension Scheme, known as SERPS, which paid higher amounts to workers with higher lifetime earnings. Since men historically earned more and had fewer career gaps, they built up larger SERPS entitlements.

As the older generation of pensioners — who built up those SERPS rights — gradually leaves the system, their payments no longer pull up the male average. Newer retirees on both sides are assessed almost purely on their National Insurance record. That change in composition is what experts believe will tip the average in women's favour.

The DWP confirmed to the House of Commons Library that the new State Pension was specifically designed to narrow the gender gap by phasing out earnings-related additions. Understanding [how National Insurance years are built up](/national-insurance-contributions-guide) is important context for anyone approaching retirement.

**What this does not mean for individual women**

The crossover in averages does not mean every woman will receive more than every man. Your own payment still depends entirely on your qualifying years. You need 35 years of National Insurance contributions or credits to receive the full £241.30. Fewer years means a reduced amount, regardless of gender.

Women who have gaps in their record — from years spent in part-time work, career breaks, or caring responsibilities — may receive less than men with unbroken records. Conversely, women with strong NI histories will be entitled to the same full pension as anyone else.

The gap is currently £3.45 a week on average, based on the latest March 2026 figures. That is a significantly smaller difference than existed under the pre-2016 system.

**How to check your own entitlement**

The Government's Check Your State Pension forecast tool at Gov.uk shows your personal entitlement based on your actual NI record. It also shows whether you have any gaps worth filling before you reach State Pension age. Checking your own record takes minutes and gives you a more accurate picture than any national average. If you are looking at [topping up NI contributions](/national-insurance-top-up-guide), the deadline rules have changed in recent years and are worth reviewing separately.

The State Pension age is currently 66 for both men and women.

**What to watch next**

The official DWP statistics are updated annually. The March 2027 figures — due for release around autumn 2027 — will show whether the projected crossover has happened. Any change to the State Pension rate for 2027/28 will also be announced by the government in advance of the new tax year. Check back when those figures are published for an updated breakdown.

## Drumcree Parade Allowed for First Time in 28 Years as Court Upholds Ruling

An Orange Order march through one of Northern Ireland's most contested parade routes has taken place for the first time since 1998, after the Parades Commission permitted it under strict conditions and a court upheld that decision following an overnight legal challenge. Residents and protesters blocked the route. Here is what was approved, what happened, and why this matters.

**The decision and its conditions**

The Parades Commission listed the September 27 parade from Drumcree Church towards Garvaghy Road in Portadown with a set of specific conditions. Those conditions included a limited number of participants, no supporters permitted to accompany the march, restrictions on bands and music, and defined rules on the route and timing.

The Northern Ireland Department of Justice called publicly for the determination to be respected and for any protest to remain peaceful.

Nationalist residents and protesters had opposed the march. When some blocked the route, the Commission's conditions and the court ruling framing the events became central to how police and legal authorities responded.

**The overnight court challenge**

A legal challenge to the Parades Commission decision was heard by a court on the night of September 26. Reporting from ITV News confirmed the court upheld the Parades Commission ruling after what was described as an unprecedented late-night hearing. The march went ahead on September 27.

**Why Drumcree has been a flashpoint**

The Drumcree dispute concerns an Orange Order parade route that passes through the predominantly nationalist Garvaghy Road area of Portadown. During the 1990s, the standoff over the route became one of the most serious flashpoints of the final years of the Troubles, with repeated clashes between protesters, loyalists, and security forces.

The Parades Commission restricted the route from 1998. The 2026 determination marks the first time a parade has been permitted along the route in 28 years.

The Commission's role is to assess notifications for parades in Northern Ireland and set conditions where it judges them necessary to reduce the risk of public disorder. Its determinations are legally binding. Understanding the [history of the Parades Commission](/parades-commission-explainer) is useful context for anyone following Northern Ireland's community relations more broadly.

**What the conditions meant in practice**

The restrictive conditions attached to this parade are not a minor detail. No supporters accompanying the march meant the event was smaller and more controlled than a standard parade. Restrictions on music and bands directly limit one of the most visible and contentious aspects of loyalist marching culture in contested areas.

That combination — permission granted, but tightly bounded — reflects how the Parades Commission has approached contested routes in other parts of Northern Ireland. Critics of the decision argue that any permission for this route reopens a wound the 1998 restriction was meant to close. Proponents argue that conditions have changed enough after nearly three decades to allow it.

The Department of Justice's call for peaceful protest, and the court's swift overnight decision to uphold the determination, suggest both branches of authority anticipated the legal and public-order challenge this decision would generate.

**What happens next**

This story is developing. Whether the parade's conditions were fully observed, what the police response was to the blocked route, and whether the Parades Commission will revisit this or related applications are all open questions. [Coverage of Northern Ireland community tensions](/northern-ireland-parades-community-relations) will be updated as further detail emerges. The Parades Commission's future determinations on contested routes, and any response from the Orange Order or Garvaghy Road residents, are the most likely next developments to watch.

# ARTICLE 4 — Priority 4
## Trump Rolls Back Biden-Era Fuel Economy Rules as Final Standards Await Publication

The Trump administration announced on September 26 that it is lowering the fuel economy requirements that American car manufacturers must meet across their vehicle fleets. The move reverses Biden-era targets that had pushed automakers toward higher average miles per gallon and, indirectly, toward more electric vehicles. The detailed final standards have not yet been published by the Transportation Department — meaning the precise new numbers are not yet confirmed.

**What was announced and what is still pending**

President Trump stated on September 26 that his administration was rolling back the fuel economy rules put in place under the previous administration. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announced what the administration called a "Freedom Means Affordable Cars" initiative, framing the change as reducing costs for consumers.

The Transportation Department had published a proposal in December 2025 that set a target of approximately 34.5 miles per gallon across manufacturers' fleets by 2031. Biden-era standards had set substantially higher targets over the same timeframe, with the explicit aim of accelerating the shift to electric vehicles to meet emissions goals.

The final detailed rule had not been formally published by the Transportation Department at the time of the September 26 announcement. Until that document appears, the exact fleet-average MPG requirements that manufacturers will face should not be treated as settled.

**What CAFE standards actually are**

CAFE — Corporate Average Fuel Economy — is the federal framework that sets how fuel-efficient American manufacturers' vehicle fleets must be on average. The system does not require every individual vehicle to hit the same number. Instead, automakers add up their sales across models and must meet a weighted average. A company selling many large trucks alongside smaller cars has a different calculation than one selling mostly compact vehicles.

Manufacturers who miss their CAFE targets face financial penalties. The requirements cover both car and light truck categories separately. For a deeper look at how the standards affect vehicle manufacturing decisions, [the relationship between fuel economy rules and car prices](/cafe-standards-car-prices-explainer) has been covered separately.

**What changes for car buyers**

The practical effect on consumers is indirect. Automakers must meet the fleet targets, but the rules do not ban any individual vehicle from being sold. Buying an electric vehicle or a gasoline-powered car remains a buyer's choice. What changes is the financial calculation for manufacturers who want to keep selling high-selling, lower-MPG models — they need either to offset those with higher-MPG vehicles or face penalties.

Lower fleet standards reduce the pressure on automakers to include EVs in their mix to hit compliance numbers. Whether that results in lower vehicle prices for consumers — the stated aim of the policy — depends on production decisions manufacturers have not yet publicly detailed.

The environmental thread is direct: CAFE standards set a floor on average fuel consumption. Lower standards mean less pressure to cut fleet-average fuel use and therefore emissions from the light-vehicle sector. The [Sabin Center for Climate Change Law](https://climate.law.columbia.edu) tracks how NHTSA rule changes affect emissions projections, and their database will be updated once the final rule is published.

**What to watch for next**

The Transportation Department's formal publication of the final rule is the single most important next event in this story. That document will contain the specific fleet-average MPG requirements, the model-year timeline, and the compliance framework. Until it is published, reports stating precise final numbers should be treated as preliminary. Check back when the final rule is released for a breakdown of what the specific standards mean for each vehicle category.

## US Bond Yields Near 5% as Higher Borrowing Costs Hit Mortgages and Household Budgets

American households are paying more to borrow. The 10-year Treasury yield — a rate that does not directly appear on any consumer loan statement, but that quietly sets the floor for mortgages, car finance, and business loans — has been hovering near 5% through September. Oil prices have also risen sharply. The combination is adding pressure to household budgets that were already stretched by inflation.

**What the 10-year yield actually does**

When the yield on a 10-year US Treasury bond rises, lenders who compete with that risk-free government return have to offer higher rates on their own products to attract money. That transmission mechanism is why a number that sounds abstract — the yield on a government bond — ends up on your monthly mortgage statement.

The 10-year yield briefly touched multi-year highs in September, with The Wall Street Journal reporting it hovering near 5% at points during the month. Mortgages in the US are typically priced against the 10-year, so sustained pressure at that level feeds directly into what a first-time buyer or someone refinancing will be quoted.

For context, the Federal Reserve's target for short-term rates and the 10-year yield are related but separate. The Fed sets the overnight rate; longer-term yields reflect what bond market investors expect about inflation and growth over a decade. When those expectations shift — as they have, in part because of elevated oil prices — longer yields can rise even when the Fed is not actively hiking. Anyone tracking [Federal Reserve interest rate decisions](/federal-reserve-rate-decisions) alongside longer-dated yields will notice they can move in different directions.

**Where oil fits in**

Reporting by Yahoo Finance placed oil prices at around $107 a barrel during late September, driven by supply constraints and persistent demand. Elevated oil prices feed into inflation expectations — if energy stays expensive, prices across the broader economy stay elevated, which in turn keeps bond investors demanding higher yields as compensation for lending long-term.

The effect on American households is layered. Higher oil prices directly raise fuel and heating costs. They also push up the cost of transporting goods, feeding into grocery and retail prices. And they influence the bond market in a way that keeps mortgage rates elevated. The three effects arrive separately on different bills, but they share the same starting point.

**What this means for specific borrowing decisions**

Mortgages are the most direct channel. A one-percentage-point increase in mortgage rates adds meaningfully to monthly payments on a typical home loan. Rates in the high 6% to 7% range — which have been reported during this period — represent a significant cost increase compared to the sub-4% rates available before 2022.

Car finance rates, business lending, and credit card rates are similarly affected, though the precise channels differ. [Fixed versus variable rate mortgages](/fixed-vs-variable-rate-mortgage-guide) and their relationship to Treasury yields have been covered in a separate explainer.

For savers, the same dynamic creates an opportunity. High-yield savings accounts, money market funds, and short-term Treasury products are paying meaningfully more than they did two years ago — a direct consequence of the same rate environment that raises borrowing costs.

**The political context, briefly**

With the November 2026 midterm elections approaching, the economic backdrop is part of political coverage. The documented effect of elevated yields and oil prices is higher borrowing and energy costs for households. Whether those conditions affect how people vote, and for which party, is a question polling tracks separately — and current polling should not be substituted for economic data in any article on this subject.

## Switzerland Votes Against Stricter Neutrality Rules That Would Have Limited Sanctions and Defence Ties

Swiss voters rejected an initiative on Sunday that would have tightened the country's constitutional neutrality, restricting its ability to impose sanctions on countries at war and limiting military cooperation with defence alliances. The existing framework stays in place. Here is what was actually on the ballot and what the result means.

**What the initiative would have changed**

Swiss neutrality is already written into the constitution. The initiative did not ask whether Switzerland should abandon neutrality. Instead, it asked whether that neutrality should be applied more strictly — in a way that would have constrained several things Switzerland currently does.

Under the initiative, Switzerland would have faced new limits on joining international sanctions regimes against belligerent states, except in narrow specified circumstances. It would also have faced restrictions on military and defence-alliance cooperation with other countries. Supporters — led by the Swiss People's Party — argued the existing arrangements had drifted away from Switzerland's traditional neutral position, particularly after the country joined EU sanctions against Russia following the invasion of Ukraine.

**What the result means**

Voters rejected the initiative. The official Swiss Federal Chancellery voting dashboard confirms the proposal failed. The existing constitutional and legal framework remains intact. Switzerland can continue to cooperate with other states on defence and security matters and can participate in international sanctions regimes as it does now.

The "no" vote does not mean Switzerland is reconsidering neutrality itself. It means voters decided not to constitutionally tighten how it is applied. The Federal Department of Foreign Affairs describes neutrality as a long-standing principle of Swiss foreign policy, and that principle is not affected by this result.

For anyone following European security policy, [Switzerland's relationship with NATO and the EU](/switzerland-nato-eu-relations) is covered in a broader explainer that gives context on what defence cooperation currently involves.

**What Switzerland does and does not do under current neutrality**

Switzerland is not a NATO member. It does not participate in collective defence arrangements. But it cooperates on specific defence and security matters with other states and has been part of sanctions regimes. The initiative would have made those activities harder to justify under the constitution.

The distinction matters because commentary after a referendum result sometimes collapses several separate questions. Switzerland did not vote on whether to join NATO. It voted on whether to apply a stricter interpretation of the neutrality it already has.

The result is that the country's foreign policy toolkit remains unchanged. Future decisions about whether to join or leave specific sanctions regimes, or deepen or reduce security cooperation, will be made through the same political and legal channels as before.

For readers tracking European security more broadly, the result removes one variable from the discussion but does not settle how Switzerland will position itself on specific future questions — that depends on political decisions that have not yet been taken.

# ARTICLE 7 — Priority 7
## Eating 100g More Ultra-Processed Food Per Day Linked to 14% Higher Heart Risk, Study Finds

A large meta-analysis published this month found that people who eat an extra 100 grams of ultra-processed food per day are associated with a 14% higher risk of cardiovascular events, compared to those eating less. The research pooled data from nearly 9 million adults. Before that number gets misread, here is exactly what it means — and what it does not.

**What the study found**

The research, published in the BMJ's Family Medicine and Community Health journal on September 22, analysed data from 8,819,894 adults drawn from 51 prospective cohort studies across multiple countries. It is a dose-response meta-analysis — meaning it looked at whether the association between UPF consumption and health outcomes changed as intake went up.

Per each additional 100 grams per day of ultra-processed food:

- Cardiovascular events: 14% higher associated risk
- Cancer: 4% higher associated risk
- All-cause mortality: 3% higher associated risk
- Metabolic syndrome and diabetes: 2% higher associated risk

The study's DOI is 10.1136/fmch-2026-003925.

**What 14% actually means here**

That figure is a population-level association from observational data. It does not mean your personal risk of a heart attack rises by exactly 14% if you eat one more biscuit packet per day. There is no clinical test that can turn this association into an individual prediction.

The research is a meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies — a design that tracks groups of people over time and looks for patterns, rather than one that randomly assigns people to eat specific diets and measures outcomes. That means the study can identify a consistent association across a large population, but it cannot rule out that people who eat more ultra-processed food also differ from those who eat less in other ways that affect their heart health.

The researchers themselves acknowledge dietary-assessment limitations and residual confounding as important caveats. EurekAlert! reported these qualifications alongside the headline findings.

**What ultra-processed food actually includes**

Ultra-processed food is defined by the NOVA classification system, which groups foods by the degree to which they have been processed industrially. Products in the ultra-processed category include mass-produced bread, packaged snacks, flavoured yoghurts, ready meals, soft drinks, and reconstituted meat products. The category includes a wide range of products — some consumed in small amounts, some forming a large part of many people's diets.

One hundred grams is approximately one standard bag of crisps, one chocolate bar, or a single portion of many packaged snacks. For context on [how to assess your own ultra-processed food consumption](/ultra-processed-food-nova-guide), existing dietary guidance offers practical approaches.

**How strong is the evidence overall**

This study adds to a growing body of research connecting UPF consumption with health outcomes across multiple disease categories. The strength of the finding comes from its scale — nearly 9 million people across 51 studies — and the consistency of the dose-response relationship across disease categories.

At the same time, observational studies on dietary habits are among the most methodologically challenging in medicine. People who eat fewer ultra-processed foods typically differ from heavy consumers in income, education, access to fresh food, and other health behaviours. Separating the effect of UPF from those confounding factors is a recognised limitation. [Dietary research methods](/how-to-read-nutrition-studies) and why association does not equal causation is worth understanding if you are trying to apply this research to your own eating habits.

**What the bottom line looks like**

Across a population of nearly 9 million adults, people who ate more ultra-processed food showed consistently higher rates of cardiovascular events, cancer, and earlier death. The association strengthened as intake rose, which is what a dose-response relationship means. That is a meaningful finding at a population scale.

It is not a guarantee that reducing your UPF consumption will cut your personal heart-attack risk by 14%. But it is part of a consistent body of evidence pointing in the same direction. Any updated national dietary guidelines informed by this research will be worth revisiting when published.

## Andy Burnham Proposes Free Social Care for England Funded by Universal Contributions

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has set out a proposal for a universal social care service in England that would be free at the point of use — an arrangement modelled on the NHS. The plan would appear in the next Labour manifesto rather than the current parliament, and the funding mechanism has not yet been decided. Here is what has been proposed, what remains unresolved, and what it would mean for people who use or pay for care.

**What Burnham announced**

Speaking in a BBC interview and at Labour's annual conference in September 2026, Burnham said he wants to create a universal social care service where everyone contributes and care is free when people need it. He used the NHS as the explicit reference point — a system where the principle is access based on need rather than ability to pay.

Key points from his statement: the service would be universal; contributions would be general rather than means-tested; it would be free at the point of use; it would not be implemented in the current parliament; it is intended to feature in the next Labour general election manifesto; and the funding model is still under active review.

Baroness Casey, who has been reviewing adult social care in England, is expected to inform the policy's development. The Guardian reported Burnham's framing directly from the BBC interview.

**What the proposal does not yet include**

There is no final funding mechanism. Burnham explicitly said that question is still being worked through. That matters because social care in England currently costs tens of billions of pounds annually, with a combination of local authority funding, personal contributions from care recipients, and NHS spending. A universal, free-at-point-of-use system would require a different and substantially larger funding base.

Whether the contributions Burnham described would take the form of a new hypothecated tax, higher national insurance, general taxation, or some other arrangement has not been specified. That gap is the most significant unresolved element of the proposal.

**Why the current system is under pressure**

England's adult social care system is means-tested. People with assets above a threshold — currently above £23,250 in most cases — must pay for their own care until their assets are depleted. This means many families face substantial care costs in old age that they had not anticipated or planned for.

The result is a system where identical care needs result in very different costs depending on personal financial circumstances. People who have saved, owned property, or received inheritances can face bills of tens of thousands of pounds per year. Those without assets receive publicly funded care — but face postcode variation in quality and availability. [Adult social care costs in England](/adult-social-care-costs-england) have been covered in depth separately.

The NHS comparison is instructive: the principle Burnham is describing would extend to care the logic already applied to healthcare — that cost at the point of need should not determine access. Whether that is practically achievable depends on funding solutions that have eluded multiple governments.

**What this means for families now**

Nothing changes immediately. The proposal is intended for a future manifesto, which means it would need to be included in a general election campaign, then legislated after a Labour victory in that election. The earliest possible implementation would be well into the next decade.

For families currently arranging or funding care, the existing rules and costs apply. [Understanding care funding in England](/care-funding-means-test-guide) remains relevant to any immediate planning decision.

**What to watch next**

The Baroness Casey review is the next substantive input into this policy area. Its findings, and Burnham's conference speech in full, will give more detail on the shape of any formal proposal. When the next Labour manifesto is drafted, this commitment and its funding mechanism will be one of the most closely scrutinised elements. Check back when the Casey review publishes for an update on the funding model that emerges.

## Nor'easter Hits US East Coast: Deaths, 150,000 Power Outages and Coastal Flooding

A nor'easter storm has struck the US East Coast, killing at least one person and cutting power to nearly 150,000 households. Coastal flooding, high winds, and dangerous surf conditions have affected states from New York and New Jersey through New England. The storm began impacting the region on September 27.

**What a nor'easter is**

A nor'easter is an extratropical storm that develops over the Atlantic and moves up the coast, with its strongest winds coming from the northeast — hence the name. These storms are not tropical cyclones and do not follow the hurricane warning framework. They bring sustained wind, coastal flooding from wave action and storm surge, and heavy rain or snow depending on the season.

September nor'easters are less common than those in winter months but can produce comparable coastal and wind damage. The distinction matters because the hazards — coastal inundation, tree damage from wind, power outages, surf conditions dangerous for swimmers and surfers — differ from tornado or hurricane profiles.

**What has been confirmed**

The Guardian reported at least one death linked to the storm and nearly 150,000 households losing power across affected areas as of September 27.

Affected areas include New York and New Jersey as well as states further north into New England. Coastal flooding has been reported at multiple points along the Eastern Seaboard. Travel has been disrupted, with road conditions affected by flooding and fallen trees in some areas.

The National Weather Service, through its National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration forecast offices, is issuing current advisories for specific zones. Anyone in an affected area should check the National Weather Service directly for their specific location rather than relying on general reports, as hazard levels vary significantly between coastal and inland areas.

**Specific hazards still active**

Beyond power outages, three categories of hazard deserve attention:

Coastal flooding: Storm surge and wave action can affect low-lying coastal areas even after the main wind event has passed. People near shorelines should check current tide and surge data.

Dangerous surf: Rip currents, high waves, and rough surf conditions can persist for 24-48 hours after a coastal storm. Beaches in affected areas should be treated as unsafe for swimming until cleared.

Travel: Fallen trees, flooded roads, and reduced visibility from rain remain live risks during and immediately after the storm's passage.

For anyone tracking [major weather events affecting the US East Coast](/us-east-coast-storm-coverage), this continues developing.

**What to watch for**

Power restoration timelines will depend on the extent of tree damage and infrastructure effects, which take time to assess. The National Weather Service will issue updated advisories as the storm system moves away. Death and injury tallies, restoration figures, and any additional fatalities are all likely to be updated in the 24-48 hours following the storm's peak impact. Check back for updated figures as restoration and full damage assessment progress.
