### Meteor Showers October 2026: When and How to See the Draconids and Orionids in the UK

Two meteor showers are active this month. The Draconids peak this week, on the nights of 8 and 9 October, and the Orionids follow later in the month, peaking on 21 and 22 October. Neither shower requires binoculars, a telescope, or a trip to a remote dark-sky site — but the Draconids have a timing quirk that most stargazing guides get wrong.

**Draconids: watch in the evening, not the early hours**

The Draconids are one of the very few meteor showers best watched in the early evening rather than after midnight. Their radiant point — the area of sky the meteors appear to streak from — sits in the constellation Draco, which reaches its highest point in the northern sky shortly after dark in early October.

For UK viewers, the optimal window on 8 and 9 October is roughly 7:30 PM to 10:00 PM. Looking north-northwest and allowing 20 minutes for your eyes to adapt to the dark will give you the best chance of seeing individual streaks. After about 10 PM, the radiant begins to descend toward the horizon, and Draconid activity drops off sharply.

The parent comet is 21P/Giacobini-Zinner. The Draconids travel at around 20 kilometres per second on atmospheric entry — slow, by meteor standards — which means they produce longer, more gradual streaks rather than fast white flashes. In a typical year, observers under dark skies might see between 5 and 15 meteors per hour at peak. Activity can occasionally spike higher when Earth passes through a denser filament of comet debris, though 2026 predictions are for a moderate display.

The 2026 Draconid peak falls near a new Moon, which means the sky will be genuinely dark during the optimal evening window — good news for visibility. [INTERNAL LINK: UK dark sky areas guide]

**Orionids: pre-dawn viewing from 21 October**

The Orionids are active from 2 October through to 7 November, but the peak falls on 21 and 22 October. Unlike the Draconids, these are pre-dawn meteors. The radiant point lies in Orion, which rises above the horizon in the early hours and is highest in the southern sky between roughly 2:00 AM and 5:00 AM.

The Orionids are fast-moving — around 66 kilometres per second — and often leave brief glowing ionisation trails in their wake. The International Meteor Organization puts the expected peak rate at around 20 meteors per hour under ideal dark-sky conditions, though typical suburban viewing will be lower.

One complication for the 2026 Orionid peak: the full Moon falls on 24 October, meaning that on peak nights, the Moon will be nearly full and washing out faint meteors. The pre-dawn hours remain the best bet because the Moon will have set or be low by then, but expect a reduced visible count compared to a dark-Moon year. The Orionids' parent body is 1P/Halley — the same Halley's Comet that last passed through the inner solar system in 1986. [INTERNAL LINK: Perseid meteor shower 2026 results]

**What actually helps you see more meteors**

Telescopes and binoculars restrict your field of view and work against you for meteor watching. The more sky you can see at once, the better your chances. Lie flat on your back on a clear night, look straight up or slightly toward the radiant direction, and give your eyes at least 15 to 20 minutes to adjust before you start counting.

Urban light pollution can reduce visible meteor counts by up to 80 percent compared to a rural dark-sky location. If you're in a city, even moving to a local park away from streetlights makes a noticeable difference.

Both showers can be seen across the UK, Ireland, the northern US, Canada, and Australia — though for Southern Hemisphere observers, the Draconids are lower in the sky and harder to see.

The Orionid peak on 21 to 22 October is the next opportunity if the Draconid display disappoints. Weather forecasts for your specific location are the most useful tool in the 24 hours before each peak night.

This article explains what the two systems actually do, what the modelling shows, and what the documented arguments for each position are — without endorsing either.

**How the current system works**

Under First-Past-The-Post, the UK is divided into 650 parliamentary constituencies. In each constituency, voters choose a single candidate. The candidate with the most votes wins that seat — they do not need a majority of votes, just more than any individual rival.

A party can win a general election and form a government with a parliamentary majority while receiving well under half the national vote. In the 2019 general election, the winning party received about 44 percent of votes cast. In 2024, the winning party received about 34 percent of votes cast but won a large parliamentary majority. The Electoral Reform Society records that in both elections, tens of millions of votes cast for losing candidates in safe constituencies had no effect on the outcome.

This disproportionality is the core objection from reform advocates. They argue that the gap between vote share and seat share means parliament does not reflect how the country actually voted. [INTERNAL LINK: how UK general elections work]

**How Proportional Representation works**

Proportional Representation is not a single system — it is a category of voting methods that share a common aim: making a party's seat count roughly match its vote share nationally or regionally.

The main variants discussed in the UK context are:

- **Party List PR**: Voters choose a party, not a candidate. Seats are allocated to parties in proportion to their national vote share. Used in Israel, the Netherlands, and Spain.
- **Additional Member System (AMS/MMP)**: Voters cast two votes — one for a local constituency representative (as under FPTP) and one for a party list. The list seats are used to correct the disproportionality created by the constituency vote. Used for Scottish Parliament, Welsh Senedd, and Bundestag elections in Germany.
- **Single Transferable Vote (STV)**: Voters rank candidates in order of preference in multi-member constituencies. Seats are filled by eliminating candidates and redistributing preferences until all seats are filled. Used in Northern Ireland, the Republic of Ireland, and Australian Senate elections.

Each system produces different outcomes. The Guardian's interactive modelling — based on polling data, not an actual election result — shows that under various PR scenarios, third parties such as the Liberal Democrats, the Green Party, and Reform UK would have held substantially more seats in recent parliaments than FPTP delivered. The Guardian's figures are modelled seat distributions based on polling assumptions, not a projection of what any future election would produce.

**What supporters of FPTP argue**

Proponents of the current system, including both major party leaderships, make several documented arguments:

- FPTP typically delivers clear single-party majorities, allowing governments to implement their full manifesto without coalition negotiation
- Constituency representation — every voter having a named local MP — creates direct accountability between elected representatives and their geographic area
- Coalition governments formed under PR systems can be unstable or require post-election deals that voters did not vote for
- The Hansard record of parliamentary debates on the subject includes formal arguments that stability and decisive government are public goods that outweigh proportionality concerns

**What critics of FPTP argue**

The Electoral Reform Society and academic studies it cites make several documented counterarguments:

- Under FPTP, up to one-third of votes cast in recent UK elections are estimated to have been "tactical votes" — voters choosing their second or third preference to block a candidate they oppose rather than voting for who they actually support
- Constituencies are classified as "safe seats" — where the incumbent party's majority is so large that the result is not in doubt — creating millions of voters whose choices have no practical effect on the outcome
- Parties campaigning primarily on environmental or other niche policies regularly receive 7 to 10 percent of the national vote but under 1 percent of parliamentary seats, suppressing their policy influence regardless of their support level
- Recent UK elections have produced governments elected on historically low vote shares, which critics argue has worsened public disengagement from voting

**What the Guardian's modelling shows and doesn't show**

The Guardian's interactive tool models how the 2024 general election results might have looked under different PR systems, using its polling assumptions as the input. The results vary considerably depending on which PR variant is applied.

This is modelling, not a prediction, and not a result. The assumptions built into the model affect every output — and different polling methodologies would produce different figures. Presenting modelled seat distributions as if they were the certain outcome of a PR election would misread what the tool actually does.

What it does show clearly is that the seat distributions produced by FPTP and by various PR systems can diverge substantially, and that smaller parties with distributed national support are the most affected by the choice of system. [INTERNAL LINK: Scottish Parliament voting system explained]

**What happens next in the debate**

The parliamentary bill on electoral reform is at an early stage. Both major parties have historically resisted reform, and no legislation has yet passed to change the general election voting system. The debate is active because of the 2024 election result, in which a large parliamentary majority was won on a 34 percent vote share, reigniting proportionality arguments.

The Electoral Reform Society and campaign groups on both sides of the argument are active. Parliamentary debates on the subject are recorded on Hansard and publicly accessible. [INTERNAL LINK: UK constitutional reform tracker]

No vote in parliament on changing the general election system has been scheduled at the time of publication.
