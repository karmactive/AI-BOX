Marineland told Canadian authorities it was running out of money and needed the animals moved or euthanised to avoid court-ordered intervention. Two other belugas — one from Marineland and one resident at Chicago's Shedd Aquarium — also died within weeks of the wider transfer operation.

For animal welfare advocates and taxpayers tracking captive wildlife policy, Jelly Bean's sudden death shows that commercial aquarium transfers do not resolve the hazards facing captive marine mammals. Long-distance relocation carries severe biological risks, often accelerating fatalities rather than preventing them. As calls intensify to halt transatlantic transfers, visitors and donors can pressure regulatory bodies to move faster on [open-water sea sanctuary projects](/sustainability/wildlife-sanctuary-models/) rather than sanctioning high-risk international moves that perpetuate captive commercial cycles.

**Transport Stress and the Flaws of Captive Relocation**

Cetaceans subjected to long-distance air transport face acute physiological stress — surges in glucocorticoid and catecholamine hormones that suppress immune function, accelerate latent infections and can trigger fatal cardiac events on arrival in an unfamiliar environment. Jelly Bean reportedly passed pre-flight veterinary clearances, which illustrates how quickly conditions can change after the stressors of loading, flight and introduction to a new tank.

Canada's 2019 captive cetacean law, Bill S-203, banned keeping whales and dolphins in captivity for entertainment and prohibited captive breeding. But the legislation included an exemption: emergency relocation permits could be issued when provincial authorities declared an on-site welfare crisis. Jelly Bean was moved under exactly such a permit — the kind issued to prevent euthanasia rather than to arrange a planned transfer with full quarantine. Standard multi-month biological quarantine protocols for transatlantic cetacean movements were not applied.

The result is a legal contradiction in [captive marine mammal protection standards](/biodiversity/marine-protection-standards/): Canadian law now prohibits the kind of tank-based captivity Jelly Bean was placed into in Spain, but it allowed her to be exported into it through a welfare-emergency mechanism.

**Why did Jelly Bean the beluga whale die in Spain?**

Jelly Bean died suddenly on September 18, 2026 at Oceanogràfic Valencia, three weeks after arriving from Marineland in Niagara Falls, Ontario. Veterinary pathologists are conducting a full necropsy; results have not yet been released. Initial assessments point to acute transport-induced physiological stress, but no official cause has been confirmed. The death follows two other post-transfer beluga fatalities connected to the Marineland relocation operation.

Oceanogràfic Valencia's formal necropsy and tissue toxicology findings are expected within the next three to four weeks. The results will shape whether international transfers of captive cetaceans face renewed regulatory scrutiny in Canada and the European Union. Check back for updates as those findings are released.
