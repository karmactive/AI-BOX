Scientists have now confirmed microplastic particles in human cardiac tissue, in newborn blood, in surgical lung samples, and in breast milk. These are not findings from a single study — they have been replicated across multiple peer-reviewed publications over the past four years. The same materials found accumulating on coastlines have been traced inside the human body.

Eight days from now, delegations from 175 nations convene in Nairobi for an informal session of the UN Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee on Plastic Pollution — the body tasked with finalizing a binding global plastics treaty. The session runs June 30 to July 3. It is the last scheduled conversation before a final diplomatic conference later this year, and the window for a strong agreement is closing.

More than 460 million tonnes of plastic are produced globally each year — roughly double what was produced two decades ago, according to the OECD Global Plastics Outlook. Between 19 and 23 million tonnes enter freshwater systems and marine environments annually. Less than 10% of all plastic ever made has been recycled. The rest has been incinerated, landfilled, or released into the environment — where it does not break down. It breaks apart.

How We Got Here — The INC Negotiation Timeline
Nov 2022
INC-1 · Punta del Este, Uruguay
▼
175 nations agreed under UN Environment Assembly Resolution 5/14 to develop a legally binding instrument on plastic pollution. The UN Environment Programme was designated secretariat. A mandate to address the full lifecycle of plastics — production, design, and waste — was formally adopted.
May 2023
INC-2 · Paris, France
▼
The first substantive session produced a compiled draft text. Over 50 countries indicated support for production reduction measures. A smaller group of petrochemical-producing nations began organizing opposition to production caps, arguing the treaty should cover waste management only.
Nov 2023
INC-3 · Nairobi, Kenya
▼
The production cap debate became the central fault line. The High Ambition Coalition — over 60 nations including EU states, Pacific Island countries, and Canada — formally called for binding reduction targets. A negotiating text was compiled but bracketed language multiplied.
Apr 2024
INC-4 · Ottawa, Canada
▼
Deep divisions persisted on scope and ambition. The revised draft carried hundreds of bracketed options. Financing for developing nations, chemical restrictions, and implementation plans remained unresolved. The session tested whether meaningful agreement was achievable.
Nov 2024
INC-5 · Busan, South Korea
▼
Talks concluded without agreement. Production cut provisions and chemicals coverage remained the primary sticking points. An additional session — INC-5.2 — was scheduled for early 2026, and the deadline for a completed treaty was extended.
Feb 2026
INC-5.2 · Geneva, Switzerland
▼
An informal technical session in Geneva failed to narrow the gap on core provisions. Progress on chemicals annexes and financing remained limited. An additional informal session — INC-5.3 — was announced for June 30–July 3, 2026, in Nairobi.
Jun 30, 2026
INC-5.3 · Nairobi, Kenya — THIS WEEK
▼
Informal session, June 30 – July 3, 2026. Delegations will work through bracketed language, align on chemicals provisions, and address financing for developing nations. No formal adoption at this stage — but what comes out of Nairobi shapes the final adoption session. Independent reporting at the IISD Earth Negotiations Bulletin.
Where the Blocs Stand
High Ambition Coalition — 60+ Nations
EU · Pacific Islands · Canada · Chile · Rwanda · and others
  • Full lifecycle treaty — address production, design, and end-of-life waste together
  • Binding production reduction targets with a phase-out timeline by 2040
  • Mandatory restrictions on the most hazardous plastic-associated chemicals
  • Common but differentiated responsibilities — financial support for lower-income nations
  • International monitoring and compliance mechanisms
Against Production Caps — Major Oil Producers
Saudi Arabia · Russia · Iran · and others
  • Waste management focus only — collection, sorting, and recycling infrastructure
  • No binding cap on plastic production volume
  • Voluntary national action plans instead of internationally mandated targets
  • Opposition to mandatory chemical restrictions in the treaty text
  • Argue that production limits would constrain economic development
Where Microplastics Have Been Found in the Human Body

Over the past four years, peer-reviewed research has documented plastic particles in organs and tissues that were not previously considered exposure points. These findings come from independent research groups across multiple countries. The World Health Organization has called for urgent further research into health effects. No safe intake threshold has been established.

❤️
Cardiac Tissue
Microplastics found in human heart muscle in 2024. Patients with plastic particles in their cardiac tissue had measurably higher post-surgery complication rates in a peer-reviewed study.
🧠
Brain
Nanoplastics detected in human brain tissue in 2024 studies. Concentrations were found to be higher in samples from individuals who had died with dementia diagnoses.
🫚
Lungs
Found in all 11 surgical lung tissue samples in a 2022 study. Airborne microplastics, including fibers from synthetic textiles, are the primary inhalation pathway.
🧠
Blood
Detected in over 80% of blood samples tested in a 2022 Dutch study. The most common plastic found was PET — the same material in most single-use water bottles.
🭌
Breast Milk
Found in 75% of breast milk samples in a 2022 Italian study. Heating food in plastic containers accelerates the release of nanoplastic particles.
👶
Placenta
First confirmed in human placental tissue in a 2020 study — the first confirmation of plastic particles crossing a barrier previously thought to be protective. It focused scientific attention on prenatal exposure.

The treaty under negotiation would be the first legally binding international agreement to address plastic pollution across its full lifecycle — from the petrochemicals used in production to post-consumer waste disposal. That scope is precisely where the talks have stalled.

A coalition of over 60 nations wants a treaty that includes production caps and binding phase-outs of the most hazardous plastic-associated chemicals. A separate group, largely made up of major oil and petrochemical-producing countries, wants the treaty limited to waste management: improved collection, sorting, and recycling infrastructure. That gap has not meaningfully narrowed after four years of formal negotiations.

The implications are practical. Currently, less than 10% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled. Even considerable improvements in recycling infrastructure cannot reverse the overall trend while production continues growing. The OECD projects that without policy intervention, global plastic production will nearly triple by 2060 — to 1.2 billion tonnes per year.

For communities living alongside freshwater bodies where plastic concentrations now rival ocean levels, the treaty's scope is not an abstraction. In floodplains across South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, plastic waste and flooding compound each other — blocking drainage, contaminating water sources, and creating physical hazard. A waste-management-only treaty does not address what is already embedded in rivers, lakes, and soils.

The science has been consistent for several years now. By 2022, microplastics were documented in Antarctic snow — reaching one of the most remote environments on Earth through atmospheric transport. By 2023, Japanese scientists traced microplastics inside clouds, confirming aerial dispersal as a pathway. By 2024, studies had confirmed plastic particles in human brain tissue and cardiac muscle.

The plastics industry uses an estimated 400 associated chemicals during manufacturing — many classified as hazardous under existing regulatory frameworks. PVC-based products, including pipes and building materials, leach chemical compounds at a household level that waste management alone cannot touch. That is why the treaty's chemicals annex — listing which substances would be restricted — has become one of its most contested sections, alongside the broader architecture of ocean and environmental governance it would fit into.

The Nairobi session beginning June 30 is informal — no votes, no text adoption. Delegations will work through the bracketed language in the existing draft, attempt to build common ground on chemicals and financing, and prepare a document that a formal adoption session can act on. That final formal step, if it comes, would be one of the more consequential moments in international environmental law of this decade.

Researchers have been tracking biological pathways for plastic breakdown in parallel — marine fungi, enzyme engineering, and polymer reformulation offer long-term possibilities. But none of those pathways scales to the volume of plastic already in circulation, or to the production trajectory without binding policy intervention upstream.

Things Worth Knowing and Doing
🔍
Follow the negotiations directly
The IISD Earth Negotiations Bulletin provides real-time independent coverage of what is being discussed inside the room — the most reliable non-governmental source for tracking the treaty text.
♻️
Reduce single-use plastic at home
Reusable bottles, bags, and containers reduce the volume entering the waste stream. Avoid heating food in plastic containers — nanoplastics are released at a faster rate when plastic is microwaved.
📦
Know your local recycling limits
Most recycling systems accept only a narrow range of plastic types. Soft plastics, multi-layer packaging, and black plastic are typically not recyclable. Knowing what actually gets processed locally avoids contaminating recycling streams with non-recyclables.
📢
Let your government know where you stand
The positions governments take at UN talks are shaped by domestic political context. Contacting elected representatives and supporting the UNEP INC process — including its public engagement channels — feeds into that context.

The evidence on plastic pollution has been confirmed across scientific disciplines — in cardiac tissue studies, in atmospheric sampling, in sediment cores from Antarctic snow, and in blood tests from newborns. What was once visible only on shorelines is now measurable in the human bloodstream. The treaty under discussion in Nairobi is where the international response to that body of evidence is being shaped.

The talks are informal, but the stakes around them are not. A treaty limited to waste management is considered insufficient by the scientists and the environmental bloc at the table. A treaty covering production is considered unacceptable by others. That gap — and what happens to it in Nairobi over the next few days — is the central question of this week in international environmental law.

The date is June 30. The delegations are heading to Kenya. The brackets in the treaty text remain open.
