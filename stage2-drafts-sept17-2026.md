# STORY 1: Manitoba Permanent Daylight Time

**Headline:** Manitoba will stop changing clocks this November as province adopts permanent daylight time

**Meta:** Manitoba will not turn clocks back on Nov. 1, 2026 as the province moves to permanent daylight time. Here is what changes for residents.

---

Manitobans have adjusted their clocks twice a year for decades. That ends this fall. Premier Wab Kinew's government announced September 17, 2026 that the province will stay on daylight saving time permanently—meaning clocks will not roll back when November arrives.

The announcement makes Manitoba the first province to formally act on permanent daylight time rather than simply discussing it.

The change is straightforward in practical terms. Clocks would normally fall back one hour on November 1, 2026. Under the government's decision, they will not. Manitoba will remain on Central Daylight Time through the winter months, which is one hour ahead of the standard time the province would otherwise return to.

Premier Kinew framed the decision around the disruptions caused by seasonal time changes—the sleep disruption, the adjustment period, the knock-on effects on health and productivity that come twice a year. Saskatchewan has operated on a single time standard year-round for decades, and Manitoba's announcement puts it on a similar path.

Manitobans will not make the usual one-hour move back to standard time on November 1, 2026. Winter schedules will hold on daylight time rather than shifting back an hour. Morning darkness will arrive later in the day, and evenings will stay lighter longer than residents are used to in winter. The change affects work schedules, school routines, travel planning, and any device—phones, computers, smart-home systems—that updates automatically based on time-zone rules. Some devices may need a manual correction if their software has not been updated to reflect the change.

The September 17 announcement sits within a longer legislative history that most coverage has glossed over. Manitoba passed an amendment in 2023 designed to eliminate seasonal time changes. That law, however, was never formally proclaimed in force. A further 2025 amendment set March 14, 2027 as the commencement date for those provisions under the Official Time Act. The government's September 17 announcement effectively moves the practical application of permanent daylight time forward by several months, ahead of what the existing legislation had scheduled. The province has not yet released the specific proclamation mechanism that will give the announcement legal effect. [INTERNAL LINK: Manitoba time change history]

**Will Manitoba clocks change on November 1, 2026?**

No. The provincial government has announced that clocks will not return to standard time on November 1. Manitoba plans to remain on daylight saving time permanently, ending the seasonal one-hour change that residents have followed for decades. The province has not yet published the full proclamation details.

November 1 is the date to note. No clock change, no one-hour adjustment on the first Sunday in November. The Manitoba government is expected to publish further details on the legislative process in the coming weeks. [INTERNAL LINK: Canadian provinces permanent time change]

# STORY 2: Humpback Whale Grief

**Headline:** Humpback whale stayed close to stillborn calf for hours off Gold Coast. What scientists say the footage actually shows

**Meta:** A humpback whale remained near her dead calf off Australia's Gold Coast in rare footage. A new study says the behaviour is significant—but stops short of calling it grief.

---

Off Australia's Gold Coast, a female humpback whale stayed close to her stillborn calf for hours after it died. Scientists have now documented the behaviour in a peer-reviewed study—and their conclusions are more careful than the word "grief" suggests.

The event was recorded on July 17, 2025, at Palm Beach reef near the Gold Coast. Researchers observed the female humpback from a private vessel and captured 15 minutes of GoPro footage. The study, published September 16, 2026 in the journal Discover Animals, is the first detailed documentation of this kind of behaviour in a baleen whale.

The calf was stillborn. The mother did not attempt to carry or surface the calf. She remained in close proximity to the body for an extended period—hours, and possibly longer, based on the researchers' observations.

The footage is scientifically valuable because it gives marine biologists a comparable record. Post-mortem behaviour has been documented in dolphins, orcas, and other toothed whales—sometimes called odontocetes. But baleen whales, the group that includes humpbacks, are rarely observed in this context. The Gold Coast case adds a baleen-whale data point to a small and growing body of evidence about how cetaceans respond to death. For researchers studying animal cognition and social behaviour, the rarity of the observation makes it worth examining closely, even from a single event. [INTERNAL LINK: whale behaviour and intelligence]

## What the science actually found

The paper's authors describe the behaviour as "post-mortem attentive behaviour." That phrase is deliberate. Grief is a subjective internal state, and scientists cannot measure it directly in a wild animal without access to physiological or neurological data. The researchers say the footage documents sustained proximity and apparent attention to the dead calf—but they explicitly stop short of claiming the whale was grieving in the way humans understand the term.

One detail the paper highlights is particularly telling. The mother did not attempt to bring the calf to the surface. In documented cases involving dolphins, mothers have sometimes tried to push dead calves above the waterline, seemingly attempting to get them to breathe. The humpback mother's behaviour was different. The researchers suggest this may mean she recognised the calf was dead shortly after birth, rather than persisting in a response driven by the expectation of a live animal. That distinction is meaningful: it suggests the whale's behaviour reflected awareness of the calf's state, not simply a failed nurturing instinct. [INTERNAL LINK: cetacean cognition research]

The distinction between documented behaviour and interpreted emotion matters for how this study gets reported. The paper records what the whale did. It does not record what she felt. Saying a humpback "grieves" borrows emotional vocabulary that the evidence does not fully support. What the evidence does support is that a humpback whale showed sustained post-mortem attention to her dead calf—behaviour that has rarely, if ever, been recorded this clearly in a baleen species.

**Do whales grieve?**

Evidence suggests some cetaceans show behaviours consistent with grief-like or post-mortem attention, but scientists cannot directly establish subjective grief in wild whales without physiological or neurological measurements. The Gold Coast case is notable because it documents prolonged attention by a humpback whale to a stillborn calf—behaviour rarely recorded in baleen whales—and gives researchers a new point of comparison with post-mortem behaviour already documented in dolphins and orcas.

The Gold Coast footage now sits in the scientific record as a documented case. Whether more observations follow, and whether they show similar patterns, will shape how researchers interpret this one. The study is available through Discover Animals for those who want to read the researchers' language directly rather than through media summaries.

**Meta:** A federal judge has ordered the Kennedy Center to give 30 days' notice before any major physical changes, including demolition. Here is what the court record shows.

---

A federal judge has ordered the Kennedy Center to provide 30 days' notice before making any major physical changes to the building. The order comes as President Trump has raised the possibility of demolishing the facility—and as the Kennedy Center itself says nothing is currently being demolished.

The legal case is Beatty v. Trump, No. 25-cv-4480, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. The September 17 development is a court order from Judge Christopher Cooper requiring the Kennedy Center to give advance notice before significant physical work begins. That notice requirement applies to demolition as well as other major changes.

The practical effect of the court order is a 30-day window before any major physical change can proceed. That includes demolition, should it be formally planned. The notice requirement gives plaintiffs—and the public—time to respond legally if the Center moves toward changes that have not been disclosed. Without the order, changes could begin with no advance warning.

Separate from the court order, Trump has made public comments raising the possibility that the Kennedy Center could be "ripped down" and redeveloped. A photograph circulating September 17 shows Trump apparently looking at a document that seems to refer to a Kennedy Center demolition. ## What the court record shows—and what it doesn't

These are three distinct things, and they are not interchangeable: Trump's public comments about possible demolition; the Kennedy Center's statement that no demolition is happening; and the court's requirement for advance notice before major physical changes.

Trump's comments are statements about what might happen. The Kennedy Center's denial is its official position. The court order is a legal mechanism that creates a procedural safeguard—it does not establish that demolition is planned, and it does not prevent the Kennedy Center from eventually making changes if it follows the required process.

The photograph of Trump with the document marked apparently "Kennedy Center DEMOLISHED" has been widely circulated. That photograph does not confirm that demolition was approved, ordered, or scheduled. Photographs of documents do not establish what is in those documents or what was decided. No demolition has been established by the available evidence. The federal court has required 30 days' notice before major physical changes, including demolition. The Kennedy Center says nothing is currently being demolished. Trump's public comments have raised the possibility of demolition, but a comment about a possibility is different from an approved demolition project. No demolition permit, approved plan, or court finding of imminent demolition has been publicly confirmed.

The 30-day notice requirement means any formal move toward major physical changes to the Kennedy Center must be made public before work begins. Watch for filings in Beatty v. Trump, No. 25-cv-4480, for the next developments in the case.

# STORY 6: T. Rex Body Temperature

**Headline:** Scientists measured T. rex's body temperature using fossil teeth. The result puts it close to a human's

**Meta:** UCLA researchers used the chemistry of fossilized T. rex teeth to estimate the dinosaur's body temperature at around 97°F. The finding is consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism.

---

Researchers have worked out T. rex's body temperature by analysing the chemistry locked inside fossil teeth—and the number they landed on is close to a human's.

A team at UCLA used three fossilized T. rex teeth to estimate the dinosaur's body temperature at approximately 36.3°C, or about 97°F. The research was published in the journal Science Advances and represents one of the clearest measurements yet of how T. rex regulated heat internally. The result is consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism rather than the cold-blooded biology associated with modern reptiles.

The finding puts T. rex in a thermal range that overlaps with living mammals and birds, not with crocodiles or lizards. A crocodile's body temperature tracks the environment around it. T. rex, if this measurement holds, maintained an internal temperature independent of external conditions—which has implications for how it moved, hunted, and functioned as an animal.

The practical weight of this research is what it tells us about T. rex as a living animal. A warm-blooded T. rex would have had higher energy needs than a cold-blooded one, meaning it required more food and likely had a faster metabolism. It also suggests T. rex was capable of sustained physical activity in a way that cold-blooded animals are not—they slow down dramatically in cold conditions. The T. rex's thermal independence would have made it effective regardless of the ambient temperature of its Cretaceous environment. [INTERNAL LINK: dinosaur physiology and palaeontology]

## How a fossil tooth becomes a thermometer

The technique behind this measurement is called clumped isotope thermometry. It works on a specific chemical property of tooth enamel: the way certain isotopes of carbon and oxygen bond together inside the mineral as it forms. The ratio of those bonded pairs changes with temperature—when enamel forms at higher temperatures, the ratio shifts in a predictable direction.

Tooth enamel is one of the hardest biological materials in the animal kingdom. It survives fossilisation better than bone, which means the chemical signals inside it can survive tens of millions of years with minimal alteration. The researchers were able to read those signals from three T. rex teeth and calculate the temperature at which the enamel originally formed inside the living animal.

The method has been validated against modern animals with known body temperatures. It has also been used on other fossil species. Applying it to T. rex teeth gave a result of approximately 97°F, which the team at UCLA says is consistent with other lines of evidence pointing toward warm-blooded biology in large theropod dinosaurs. The study used three teeth, which is a small sample. The researchers acknowledge this and note that the technique's consistency with other evidence strengthens rather than weakens the measurement. [INTERNAL LINK: palaeoclimate science and fossil chemistry]

This finding applies to T. rex specifically. It does not establish the body temperature of all dinosaurs, and it does not prove every dinosaur species operated the same way. The diversity of the dinosaur family tree means generalising from T. rex to Triceratops, or to small feathered dinosaurs, requires separate evidence.

**How did scientists determine T. rex's body temperature?**

Researchers analysed chemical patterns preserved in fossilized tooth enamel. The technique—clumped isotope thermometry—uses the ratio of certain bonded carbon-oxygen pairs in enamel, which varies with temperature. That ratio was measured in three T. rex teeth, allowing the team to calculate the temperature at which the enamel originally formed inside the living animal. UCLA's result: approximately 97°F, consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism.

The Science Advances paper is available for researchers and interested readers who want to examine the methodology and sample details. Further work with additional specimens would strengthen the finding. For now, the three teeth have put T. rex's temperature on the record at roughly human body heat.

The most important word to define clearly: "associate member" is not a path to EU membership. Canada is not applying to join the European Union, and the EU's membership process applies to European countries under the bloc's founding treaties. The concept is not new in European political vocabulary, but it has never been applied in quite the way Carney described. The geopolitical backdrop matters here. Carney said Canada welcomes the ambition to move beyond CETA toward a deeper alliance. The September 17 speech is the starting point, not the conclusion. Formal negotiations, if they begin, would take years. The next signal to watch is whether the EU's institutions formally respond with a proposal of their own.
