**Northern Lights Alert: NOAA Issues G1 and G2 Geomagnetic Storm Watches for August 27–28 — Here's What to Know**

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) has issued back-to-back geomagnetic storm watches for August 27 and 28. The first day brings a G1 (minor) watch; the second escalates to G2 (moderate). That's the kind of solar activity that can paint the northern sky with shades of green, purple, and red.

Here's what triggered the event. The Sun's active region AR4513 produced multiple M-class solar flares, with the most significant being an M6.9 flare on August 25, peaking at approximately 10:02 UTC (06:00 EDT). This flare was powerful enough to generate an R2 (Moderate) high-frequency radio blackout affecting radio transmissions across Europe, Africa, and the Arctic for approximately one hour during the sunlit phase.

The flare also produced a coronal mass ejection. In addition, a separate coronal-hole high-speed stream is contributing to geomagnetic activity. The first storm watch, on August 27, stems primarily from this coronal-hole high-speed stream. The second watch, on August 28, is associated with the arrival of the coronal mass ejection from the August 25 flare, which NASA modeling projects will arrive around August 28, though different forecast models show somewhat varying arrival times.

What does G2 actually mean? NOAA uses a scale from G1 (minor) to G5 (extreme). A G2 is the second tier—strong enough to affect high-latitude power systems, satellite operations, and radio navigation. More importantly for skywatchers, a G2 watch can bring aurora sightings visible at high geomagnetic latitudes—particularly across northern states including Washington, Idaho, Montana, the Dakotas, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, and northern Maine. Under stronger geomagnetic conditions, aurora can occasionally be observed further south, with historical reports from locations like New York and Idaho during comparable storms.

To check if aurora may be visible from your location, use the live NOAA 30-minute aurora forecast, which updates in real time based on current Kp-index readings. The Kp index measures Earth's magnetic disturbance; on August 28, readings could reach 5 to 6, which supports aurora activity extending toward lower latitudes.

The viewing window is dynamic and depends on local darkness and cloud cover. Rather than relying on a fixed time window, check the NOAA forecast in real time to determine optimal viewing conditions for your specific location.

Something extraordinary is also happening on August 27–28: the "Sturgeon Moon"—the August full moon—will undergo a partial lunar eclipse. The eclipse will be substantial, with 96.2% of the moon slipping into Earth's shadow. Maximum eclipse occurs at 12:12 a.m. EDT on August 28. The eclipse lasts five hours and 38 minutes. For observers in the right locations with clear skies, both the reddish eclipsed moon in the south and green aurora to the north could potentially be visible simultaneously—a rare dual celestial display.

Viewing tips: Find a dark location with a clear, unobstructed horizon. No special equipment is needed—your naked eye is safe for viewing an eclipsed moon. For faint aurora that your eye struggles to detect, a mobile phone with night mode set to a 3–5 second exposure can capture images of displays. Check NOAA's real-time aurora forecast, stay flexible as conditions change, and remember that solar storms and aurora activity can shift quickly.

**Meta's Massive Teen-Safety Settlement Explained: The New Instagram Rules, $17.1B Payment, and What Changes for Families**

Meta is writing a check for up to $17.1 billion to settle a lawsuit from a coalition of 52 state and territorial attorneys general that accused the company of deliberately designing Facebook and Instagram in ways harmful to young people. The case moved toward trial in August 2026 before the company agreed to the terms—announced August 26—just before CEO Mark Zuckerberg was scheduled to testify.

The lawsuit was born from concerns that Meta knew its platforms posed mental-health risks to teenagers and designed features specifically to maximize engagement time. The suit alleged the company had built products engineered to increase time spent, regardless of the toll on youth well-being. Meta's settlement does not include an admission of wrongdoing, but the company is committing to significant structural changes in how teenagers experience its platforms.

Here's what Meta has agreed to do for users under 18. The company will impose a two-hour daily combined limit on Instagram and Facebook. The limit includes built-in pauses that notify users after 15 minutes, 60 minutes, and 90 minutes of continuous use. Turning off the limit requires parental permission.

Nighttime access is blocked entirely from midnight to 6 a.m. On school days, notifications will be muted from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. so the apps don't interrupt class. Meta is also removing cosmetic appearance-altering filters and hiding public like counts on teen accounts.

The legal case was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. The case's legal foundation strengthened in April 2026 when Massachusetts' highest court ruled that Section 230—a federal law that typically shields social-media companies from liability—does not automatically protect them when they are sued over how they design and operate their own products.

The settlement requires court approval. Once the judge formally enters the consent order, Meta will implement these platform changes through software updates and app store deployments in the coming weeks and months.

**Tampa Airport Drops TSA Gold+ Private Security Plan: What Changed and What It Means for Travelers**

Tampa International Airport announced on August 24 that it is withdrawing from the TSA Gold+ program, a proposed pilot initiative that would have used private security contractors for checkpoint screening under TSA oversight. The Hillsborough County Aviation Authority made the decision following an evaluation of the program. Days later, TSA Administrator David Cummins announced the agency was canceling the Gold+ program nationwide.

What was Gold+, and why did Tampa decide against it?

Gold+ was the TSA's proposed new model for private airport security. Under the program, a private security operator would have conducted checkpoint screening at selected airports while the TSA remained responsible for setting security standards and overseeing compliance.

Tampa, which processes approximately 70,000 passengers daily, was under consideration for the pilot because airport leaders had identified operational advantages. The Florida airport had experienced disruptions during a previous partial Department of Homeland Security shutdown when security staffing became unpredictable. Airport executives had reasoned that private screening could provide more stable operations during government shutdowns, greater flexibility around screening technology decisions, and potentially enhanced passenger experience.

But Tampa's Chief Operating Officer identified critical unresolved questions during the evaluation. How would private contractor equipment integrate with federal TSA systems? What reliability guarantees would apply? How would data flow between private operators and TSA headquarters? These technology questions were significant enough that Tampa elected to continue with its existing federal TSA officer screening rather than move forward with the private model.

Following Tampa's withdrawal, TSA Administrator David Cummins announced the agency was discontinuing development of the Gold+ program.

The TSA is refocusing on the existing Screening Partnership Program (SPP), which was established under the Aviation and Transportation Security Act in 2001. Approximately 20 airports currently operate under the SPP framework using private screening companies.

For Tampa air travelers, the outcome is operationally straightforward: security checkpoints will continue to be staffed by federal TSA Transportation Security Officers. TSA PreCheck, CLEAR, and standard security screening will remain unchanged.

Labor unions, particularly the American Federation of Government Employees (AFGE), had filed a federal lawsuit on August 5 seeking transparency regarding the private screening procurement process. The union contended that privatization could affect job quality and security standards.

The Boeing 757 departed Edinburgh Airport in Scotland bound for Chicago O'Hare International Airport. When pilots encountered dangerous convective weather over the Chicago area, air traffic control directed them to divert. Green Bay Austin Straubel International Airport in Wisconsin became the diversion point.

The delay unfolded in phases. During the first phase, passengers remained aboard the aircraft for approximately four hours. Ground operations and flight dispatch were attempting to secure a window to continue toward Chicago as the weather situation evolved or to find an alternative routing.

The second phase lasted approximately five hours. This is where operational constraints tightened. The flight crew was approaching its maximum allowable duty period under Federal Aviation Regulations. FAA Part 117 establishes flight duty period limits that vary based on the number of flight segments, the start time of the duty period, and other operational factors. In this case, the crew could not legally exceed its duty limit while conducting the flight to Chicago. As the aircraft prepared to depart, the crew reached the point where it could not legally continue.

At that same time, Green Bay's U.S. Customs and Border Protection port facility was operating under a limited inspection window. Passengers could not legally deplane into the terminal without CBP processing. According to CBP port procedures, inspection services at Austin Straubel International Airport are available for aircraft arrivals at designated times, subject to port operational schedules and staffing. With the crew unable to continue and passengers unable to access the terminal, they remained in a secure holding area while dispatch arranged for a relief flight crew.

Late that evening, a replacement crew arrived and conducted a crew change. The aircraft departed Green Bay at 10:19 p.m. and arrived in Chicago O'Hare at 10:55 p.m. For passengers with connecting flights, the total travel disruption extended to approximately 20 hours.

Now, the key question: did the airline violate passenger-protection rules?

The Department of Transportation sets strict rules for tarmac delays on international flights. Carriers cannot hold passengers on the tarmac longer than four hours without providing a legal opportunity to deplane—subject to very limited exceptions for safety, security, air traffic control constraints, or mechanical issues. Carriers must also provide food and potable water no later than two hours into a tarmac delay, maintain operable lavatories, and provide necessary medical attention. The incident raises questions about passenger protections when international diversions, crew duty limitations, and customs processing constraints converge at a regional airport where staffing and operational hours are limited.
