# Manga Creator Kamome Shirahama Meets Animator Dana Terrace - Cross-Cultural Artist Collaboration Celebrates Shared Creative Vision

Kamome Shirahama and Dana Terrace connected at Kodansha House LA on July 5, 2026, creating a rare moment bridging Japanese manga and American animation. The in-person meetup featured creator Q&A and fan signing, with both artists publicly celebrating each other's work.

Shirahama created fan art of The Owl House in her signature pen-and-paper style. Terrace's response was immediate and emphatic: "Go read WITCH HAT ATELIER!!!" The public endorsement carries weight—major animation creators rarely actively promote manga titles, suggesting both genuine admiration and professional respect.

This isn't typical online cross-promotion. The in-person event (limited to 60 attendees) created genuine connection between creators working in different mediums, different countries, different artistic traditions. What emerged from their conversation revealed shared creative DNA despite working in entirely distinct industries.

Both artists drew inspiration from overlapping sources that shaped their respective works. Shirahama cited manga legends Moto Hagio and Akira creator Katsuhiro Ōtomo as foundational influences alongside Western artists Arthur Rackham and Alphonse Mucha. Terrace developed her visual language across animation drawing similarly from classical illustration traditions. Those shared influences created common visual vocabulary—both construct magical spaces through similar compositional philosophies.

The narrative parallels between their works are substantial. Both Witch Hat Atelier and The Owl House center on ordinary characters who discover they've been chosen for magical worlds where they don't belong. Both build storytelling around characters learning magic while grappling with being outsiders in fundamentally different environments. The thematic similarities weren't accidental—they reflect similar interest in how characters grow through magical education and community.

Shirahama's artistic process remains notably analog. She works entirely on pen and paper, building effects and compositions without digital tools. This contrasts sharply with much modern manga production relying on digital tablets. Her commitment to traditional techniques shows in character designs and intricate backgrounds that clearly resonated with Terrace's artistic sensibilities.

Witch Hat Atelier's anime adaptation, produced by Studio BUG FILMS, released in Spring 2026. The timing of Terrace's endorsement amplified fresh momentum from successful anime launch into both communities. Her endorsement reached The Owl House's dedicated fanbase while introducing Witch Hat Atelier to animation viewers who might not follow manga.

The creative communities bridging this moment aren't insignificant. Some viewers follow both properties. Others discovered Witch Hat Atelier specifically through Terrace's recommendation, exploring both manga and anime adaptations. Industry recognition that these properties speak to similar audiences influences broader perceptions about which stories deserve adaptation and promotion.

The meeting exemplifies how creative communities increasingly operate globally. A Japanese manga artist and American animator connecting publicly would have been impossible a decade ago. Streaming, digital platforms, and international distribution collapsed geographic barriers between entertainment industries. Now creators routinely discover and appreciate work across traditional national boundaries.

For gaming culture specifically, this moment reinforces how narrative-driven games and anime increasingly occupy similar creative spaces. Both mediums tell sophisticated stories about character development, magical systems, and outsider perspectives. Creators working across these mediums acknowledge shared artistic goals.
