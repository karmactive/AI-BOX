## ARTICLE 9: FERRITIN FACE TREND

**Headline:** Ferritin Face: What Low Iron Actually Does to Your Appearance — and What Medical Evidence Says

**Primary keyword:** ferritin face | **Word count:** ~710 | **FAQ: Yes**

---

A term called "ferritin face" is spreading across social media, with people linking changes in their facial appearance to low ferritin or iron deficiency. The claim is that you can identify low iron by looking in the mirror. That is not what medical evidence supports.

NHS guidance confirms that iron-deficiency anaemia can make skin look paler than usual. It does not say facial appearance is a diagnostic tool for iron levels. Those are two different things — and the gap between them is where this trend is causing confusion.

Ferritin is the protein your body uses to store iron. When ferritin levels are low, your iron stores are depleted, and this can eventually lead to iron-deficiency anaemia — a condition where you do not have enough red blood cells to carry oxygen around the body. Iron-deficiency anaemia has recognised symptoms: unusual tiredness, shortness of breath, heart palpitations, headaches, and skin that looks paler than usual.

What "ferritin face" content typically shows are before-and-after images of people who say their complexion changed after treating iron deficiency, or facial features claimed to look different at low versus normal ferritin levels. The implication is that someone can assess their own iron status by comparing their face to a reference image or a trending checklist.

Pale or dull skin has many causes: tiredness, cold temperatures, hydration levels, lighting conditions, skin tone variation across individuals, and a range of medical conditions unrelated to iron. A facial check cannot differentiate between those causes. NHS guidance lists pale skin as one possible symptom of iron-deficiency anaemia within a cluster of symptoms — not as a standalone identifier.

**Why you cannot diagnose low iron from your face**

Blood tests are used to assess iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anaemia. A full blood count and ferritin test can help determine whether iron stores are low and whether anaemia is present. A GP can request those tests. No amount of mirror-checking or social media comparison replaces that process.

Paleness associated with anaemia has a physiological basis, but appearance alone is not specific enough to identify the cause. Someone with iron-deficiency anaemia may appear pale, but so may someone who is simply tired, cold, or fair-skinned by nature.

Low iron stores can occur without anaemia, and symptoms may be absent or less specific. Some people have depleted iron stores without reaching an anaemic state. At that stage, symptoms — if present — tend to be subtle: persistent tiredness or hair changes that are easily attributed to other causes.

The "ferritin face" framing encourages self-diagnosis based on appearance alone, which can lead in two directions. Some people who need testing may seek a GP appointment — that is a positive outcome. Others may take iron supplements without a confirmed diagnosis. Unnecessary or excessive iron supplementation can cause harm and should not be taken without a confirmed diagnosis.

For related health information, see [INTERNAL LINK: NHS iron deficiency anaemia guide] and [INTERNAL LINK: Social media health trends — how to check what's real].

If you are concerned about iron deficiency, the right step is a GP appointment and a blood test, not a visual comparison with social media images.

## ARTICLE 11: FIJI HIV NATIONAL CRISIS

**Primary keyword:** Fiji HIV crisis | **Word count:** ~600

---

Fiji has declared HIV a national crisis after one of the sharpest increases in new HIV infections globally, according to UNAIDS. UNAIDS — the United Nations agency coordinating the global HIV response — says new infections in Fiji increased 12-fold between 2010 and 2025.

The declaration came on September 16, 2026. What it reveals about testing and treatment gaps is as concerning as the infection numbers themselves.

UNAIDS estimates approximately 9,100 people were living with HIV in Fiji in 2025. Of those, only 39% knew their HIV status. The majority of people living with the virus had not been tested and did not know they had it. Just 22% of people estimated to be living with HIV were accessing antiretroviral treatment.

Antiretroviral treatment — ART — is the medication that allows people with HIV to live full lives. Effective antiretroviral treatment can suppress HIV to an undetectable level, at which point a person living with HIV does not transmit the virus sexually. A treatment rate of 22% is far below global targets. Fiji remains far below the 95-95-95 testing and treatment benchmarks on the measures cited by UNAIDS. The UNAIDS global target is 95% of people living with HIV knowing their status, 95% of those on treatment, and 95% of those achieving suppressed viral levels.

UNAIDS reported sexual transmission and injecting drug use as major reported routes — among cases with a known mode of transmission, more than half were linked to sexual transmission and more than 42% to injecting drug use.

**Why the declaration matters — and what it is intended to do**

The rise in new infections has occurred alongside major gaps in testing access, prevention services, and treatment availability. People who do not know they have HIV cannot access treatment; people not on treatment carry higher viral loads that increase transmission risk; and transmission without treatment access creates conditions where the infection rate climbs further.

Fiji said the declaration was intended to accelerate testing, treatment and prevention efforts and to support requests for international assistance. UNAIDS said it would continue working with Fiji and countries across the Pacific to support the response.

HIV is not transmitted through everyday contact. It is transmitted through specific routes including blood, sexual fluids, and from a parent to a child during birth or breastfeeding. UNAIDS is explicit on this point. Stigma around people living with HIV, or around people who inject drugs, reduces the likelihood that people will seek testing or treatment — which directly worsens the public health outcome. Reporting and community response need to reflect that.

UNAIDS notes Pacific countries are at different stages of their HIV epidemics, but the region is closely connected. Fiji's declaration reflects a broader concern about rising infection rates in some parts of the Pacific. **Why has Fiji declared HIV a national crisis?**

UNAIDS says Fiji experienced one of the sharpest increases in new HIV infections globally — a 12-fold rise between 2010 and 2025. An estimated 9,100 people were living with HIV in Fiji in 2025, but only 39% knew their status and just 22% were on antiretroviral treatment. The combination of rapid infection growth, very low diagnosis rates, and very low treatment access led to the September 16 national crisis declaration. UNAIDS said it would continue working with Fiji and countries across the Pacific to support the response.
