# Zambian Wild Dogs Set Record for Longest African Mammal Movement in History

---

## ARTICLE

Three male African wild dogs have completed a journey of **4,126 kilometres** across southern Africa — the longest recorded movement ever documented for any African mammal and the longest wild dog dispersal in scientific history. The distance is roughly equivalent to travelling from Cairo to Cape Town.

The dogs, tracked by GPS collars as part of ongoing research by the Zambian Carnivore Programme (ZCP), left their birth pack in Zambia's Kafue National Park and spent nine months crossing the continent before settling in an area between South Luangwa and Lower Zambezi National Parks. The journey has reshaped scientific understanding of what endangered predators require to survive.

### The Journey of the Mayukuyuku Coalition

The three males, known to researchers as the Mayukuyuku coalition, dispersed from Kafue National Park in search of unrelated females with whom to form a new breeding pack. Dispersal is a natural behaviour in African wild dogs — young males typically leave their birth pack once they reach adulthood — but the distance these three individuals covered far exceeded anything previously recorded.

For wildlife conservationists and regional governments, this record-shattering 4,000-kilometre journey proves that endangered predators cannot survive confined to isolated national parks. The Mayukuyuku coalition's trek directly demonstrates that investing in transfrontier wildlife corridors across southern Africa protects endangered gene pools from extinction. Without connected habitat, dispersing predators die trying to reach other packs or fail to breed with unrelated individuals, accelerating population collapse through inbreeding.

The research was co-authored by Dr. Matt Becker of the Zambian Carnivore Programme, the team responsible for the GPS collar monitoring that made tracking possible.

### What Made This Journey Scientifically Remarkable

The raw distance — 2,564 miles — is extraordinary. But the more scientifically consequential detail is where the dogs spent that distance.

Nearly **40% of the Mayukuyuku coalition's journey took place outside national park boundaries** — across agricultural land, communal areas, and human-settled corridors. They navigated farms, roads, villages, and urban fringes without suffering lethal conflict with people, which is the leading cause of wild dog mortality in unprotected landscapes across Africa.

That survival rate through human-dominated terrain is not guaranteed. African wild dogs are among the most endangered carnivores on the continent. They are frequently killed in snares set for bushmeat, shot by livestock farmers, and struck by vehicles. A journey of this length that ends without casualty is, by any measure, exceptional.

The route the Mayukuyuku males took passed through the Kavango–Zambezi (KAZA) Transfrontier Conservation Area — a cross-border conservation zone spanning parts of Zambia, Zimbabwe, Botswana, Namibia, and Angola. Their survival reinforces the argument that KAZA-style international wildlife corridors are not optional infrastructure for wild dog conservation; they are essential.

### Why Genetic Diversity Drives These Journeys

African wild dogs live in tightly bonded packs with strict social hierarchies. Young males must leave their birth pack to avoid inbreeding — if they stay and breed with related females, the genetic diversity of their population collapses over generations.

Only around **6,600 African wild dogs** are estimated to remain in the wild, down from a historic population estimated at 500,000 across the continent. Their numbers are concentrated in isolated protected areas across southern and eastern Africa. Without corridors connecting those populations, the surviving individuals cannot exchange genes, and even a well-managed reserve can become a genetic dead end.

The 4,126-km journey of the Mayukuyuku males, tracked through GPS collar data analysed by the ZCP, provides the clearest evidence yet that these animals will travel extraordinary distances to find genetically unrelated mates — and that they can navigate human landscapes if those landscapes are not actively hostile to them.

### What This Means for Conservation Policy

The Mayukuyuku coalition's record dispersal was not a freak event. It was a genetic necessity — an animal doing exactly what its biology demands. The conservation implication is clear: protected areas designed as islands cannot sustain viable wild dog populations indefinitely.

Biodiversity discussions at upcoming international summits have increasingly focused on habitat connectivity as a survival mechanism. This research provides a concrete, GPS-verified data point for those arguments.

**What is the longest distance an African wild dog has travelled?**

**Why do African wild dogs travel thousands of kilometres?**
African wild dogs must leave their birth pack to find genetically unrelated mates and avoid inbreeding. With only around 6,600 individuals remaining in the wild, dispersing males must cross vast distances between isolated populations. Without connected wildlife corridors, this genetic exchange becomes impossible.

**Where did the Zambian wild dogs travel from and to?**
The Mayukuyuku coalition began their journey in Kafue National Park, Zambia, and were eventually tracked to an area between South Luangwa and Lower Zambezi National Parks — crossing agricultural land, communal areas, and parts of the KAZA transfrontier conservation zone during their nine-month journey.
