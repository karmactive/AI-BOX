### Sydney hits 33°C in early September — here's why there's a Total Fire Ban and snow in the mountains at the same time

Sydney is [entering spring](https://www.karmactive.com/australia-spring-forecast-2026-el-nino-bushfire-risk-bom/) with weather that feels like the middle of summer. Temperatures across parts of the city are forecast to reach 33°C this weekend — that is 10 to 13 degrees above what the opening days of September normally bring. This is not a small variation. That reading challenges historical records at the Observatory Hill weather station, which has tracked Sydney's climate without interruption since 1858.

The heat is driven by hot, dry air flowing in from inland Australia on strong northwesterly winds. Those winds are forecast to reach around 50 km/h across Greater Sydney and up to 100 km/h along some coastal and mountain stretches. When you put high temperatures, dry conditions, and wind at that speed together, fire danger climbs sharply.

That combination prompted the NSW Rural Fire Service to declare a [Total Fire Ban](https://www.rfs.nsw.gov.au/fire-information/fdr-and-tobans) for Greater Sydney on September 5. The ban runs from 12:01 AM to 11:59 PM. What that means practically: no fires lit outdoors, no hot-work like welding or grinding near uncleared bush, and anyone holding an existing burn permit must suspend operations. These rules exist because a single spark on a day like this can become a major fire faster than crews can respond.

The fire danger rating across parts of Sydney has reached Extreme — the second-highest category on the Australian fire danger scale. Many tracks, trails, and campgrounds across the Greater Sydney area were closed as a precaution.

While the city bakes, the Snowy Mountains are dealing with the opposite. Thredbo recorded temperatures of -1°C, with snow falling. This is because the same large weather system sending hot air toward the coast is simultaneously pulling cold polar air into the alpine areas in the south.

That contrast spreads further. Wind gusts of up to 110 km/h were tracked over parts of South Australia, including the Mount Lofty Ranges and the Flinders Ranges. Northern Victoria and Tasmania are under flood watches because saturated ground cannot absorb additional heavy rainfall quickly. Multiple catchments across the region carry active flood watches.

The climate driver connecting these events is [El Niño](https://www.karmactive.com/el-nino-2026-wmo-forecast-severe-drought-flooding/). Australia's [Bureau of Meteorology](https://www.bom.gov.au/places/nsw/chippendale/forecast/) has assessed the current El Niño as strong to very strong and likely to strengthen further. The [World Meteorological Organization](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/el-nino-set-become-very-strong-raising-risks-of-extreme-weather-2027) separately described the same system as potentially intensifying into a very strong event with global weather implications through 2027. El Niño typically brings drier, hotter conditions to eastern Australia — but it is not the only factor. Soil moisture levels, fuel load in the bush, and wind direction all interact with it.

The Australasian Fire and Emergency Service Authorities Council's seasonal outlook flags dry grass fuel loads across central and northern NSW, southeastern Queensland, and central inland areas, after an unusually warm winter. The fire danger does not stop when this weekend's heat clears.

The practical advice for Sydney residents is straightforward. The Total Fire Ban means no outdoor burning of any kind. Check the NSW RFS fire danger rating before heading anywhere near bushland. Report any smoke you see. Many parks and campgrounds near the city are already closed.

### Lightning blazes over the Washington Monument as severe storms sweep through the DC region

Late Thursday night into early Friday, a fast-moving line of severe thunderstorms cut through Washington, D.C., Maryland, and northern Virginia, bringing dangerous winds, heavy rain, and a lightning display over the National Mall that drew widespread attention online.

The storm system tracked southeastward from western Pennsylvania. It entered northern Virginia and suburban Maryland around 11 PM, with the most intense activity crossing central Washington around midnight.

Wind was the primary threat. [Reagan National Airport](https://marine.weather.gov/product.php?format=txt&glossary=0&issuedby=LWX&product=LSR&site=ILM&version=20) recorded a gust of 64 mph during the overnight period, with sustained winds of 40 mph. A surface station in Loudoun County, Virginia, measured 62 mph. The National Weather Service's Baltimore/Washington office issued a [Severe Thunderstorm Warning](https://forecast.weather.gov/MapClick.php?FcstType=text&lat=38.8977&lg=english&lon=-77.0429), identifying damaging winds as the main hazard alongside heavy rain and frequent lightning.

A standard Severe Thunderstorm Warning is triggered at the 60 mph wind threshold. For Fairfax County, Prince George's County, and the District of Columbia, the NWS issued a "Considerable Threat" designation — a step up that corresponds to a 70 mph wind criterion. That means forecasters expected conditions to exceed even the already-severe threshold.

Rainfall came down at 2 to 3 inches per hour in localized areas, causing short-duration road flooding in Fairfax and Alexandria.

Lightning struck frequently across the National Mall corridor throughout the event. Video footage captured multiple cloud-to-ground strikes near the Washington Monument. Whether lightning struck the monument directly has not been confirmed by an official source — the accurate description is spectacular lightning over and around one of the world's most recognizable landmarks.

Safety guidance from the [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning) is unambiguous on this point: when thunder is audible, anyone in an open area — a national monument, public park, or open plaza — should move into a fully enclosed building or a metal-roofed vehicle. Open canopies and isolated tall trees offer no protection from ground-level electrical discharge. Motorists were also advised against driving through flooded underpasses.

The storm arrived just before Labor Day weekend, which gave it additional profile given the number of tourists in the area. But the hazard was real: a 64 mph gust carries enough force to bring down trees and power lines, and urban flash flooding can develop faster than drivers anticipate.

### Teachers' Day 2026: Why September 5 matters, who Dr Radhakrishnan was, and what is actually new this year

India marks Teachers' Day on September 5 every year. The date is not just a recurring occasion — it marks the birthday of a man who shaped Indian philosophy, education policy, and the country's intellectual standing before becoming its second President.

Dr Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan was born on September 5, 1888. He became Vice-Chancellor of Andhra University and later Banaras Hindu University. He served as Vice President before becoming President of India in 1962. Around the time of his 75th birthday that year, the tradition of observing September 5 as Teachers' Day became established.

He specifically asked that the day not be treated as a personal celebration, but as a recognition of the [teaching profession](https://www.karmactive.com/ctet-september-2026-exam-postponed-application-deadline-extended-september-1-cbse/) as a whole. That framing has stayed with the occasion for more than six decades.

In 2026, the observance has real news behind it. President Droupadi Murmu conferred National Awards to Teachers on [48 school teachers](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2302212&lang=1&reg=48) and on [21 teachers and faculty members](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2302693&lang=1&reg=3) from higher educational institutions and polytechnics. Within the higher education group, the awards went to 12 people from central institutions, five from state universities, and four from polytechnics.

President Murmu's message on the eve of the day was specific rather than ceremonial. She called on teachers to continuously update their knowledge, embrace technology, and use innovative teaching methods — while at the same time encouraging students to think independently and ask questions freely. That is a direct response to the pressure generative AI tools are placing on classrooms right now.

Artificial intelligence can produce essays, answer factual questions, and summarize reading. What it does not do is notice when a student is confused and adjust on the spot, read the mood of a room, or model what it looks like to sit patiently with a hard problem. The frameworks from the National Council of Educational Research and Training draw exactly that line — using technology for routine tasks like attendance and basic assessment drafting, while protecting human-led discussion of reasoning, ethics, and critical thinking.

The National Education Policy 2020 sets a requirement of at least [50 hours of structured professional development training](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/Guidelines50HoursCpd.pdf) per teacher each year, delivered through platforms including DIKSHA. This is a formal competency benchmark, not a recommendation.

Teachers' Day is sometimes discussed alongside Guru Purnima, as if they are two versions of the same thing. They are not. Guru Purnima falls on the full moon day of the Hindu month of Ashadha and marks the birth and teachings of Maharishi Veda Vyasa. It belongs to the ancient tradition of the Guru-Shishya relationship — the spiritual master and the disciple. Teachers' Day is a modern national observance tied to Radhakrishnan's birth anniversary and to institutional education. Each tradition carries its own meaning. Blending them together loses what makes both significant.

### Two siblings die after snakebite in Ludhiana — what happened and what to do if it ever happens near you

In late August, in Bassian village near Jagraon in Ludhiana district, Punjab, an eight-year-old boy and his six-year-old sister were bitten while sleeping in the courtyard of their home. Both died. The sister went first. The brother died roughly 18 hours later. They were cremated together.

Their mother was working in Oman at the time. She could not return immediately because of the international employment contract and travel arrangements involved.

It started around 3 AM. The boy woke his grandmother to show a bite on his hand. In that setting, at that hour, it looked like an insect bite. By the time the family realized the situation was serious, both children were struggling to breathe. They were taken to the Raikot Civil Hospital and then referred to a larger hospital in Ludhiana. Neither child survived.

This kind of story follows a pattern that [doctors who treat snakebite in rural India](https://www.karmactive.com/snakebite-deaths-india-children-healers/) know too well. A bite at night while sleeping on the ground or outdoors, initially dismissed, deteriorating fast — these are the markers associated with the Common Krait, one of [India's four most dangerous snake species](https://clinicalestablishments.mohfw.gov.in/en/standard-treatment-guidelines). The snake species in the Ludhiana case has not been formally confirmed by medical or police authorities, so it should not be stated as fact. But the characteristics of what happened match a documented pattern precisely.

Krait bites are often nearly painless. The fang marks are tiny. There is usually no swelling around the bite, which is why families mistake them for insect bites. The venom is neurotoxic — it attacks the nervous system instead of causing dramatic local damage. The first visible signs are drooping eyelids, then difficulty swallowing, then shallow breathing. By the time breathing is affected, the effective treatment window is narrowing fast.

The only treatment that works is [Polyvalent Anti-Snake Venom](https://clinicalestablishments.mohfw.gov.in/sites/default/files/standard-treatment-guidelines/3941.pdf), known as ASV, at a hospital that also has ventilation support available. Getting there fast is the entire strategy.

If someone is bitten: keep the person calm and still. Immobilize the bitten limb using a piece of cloth or a simple splint — the goal is to slow how quickly venom moves through the lymphatic system. Remove any jewelry, bangles, or tight clothing near the bite before swelling has a chance to develop. Then get to a hospital immediately.

What not to do: Do not apply a tight tourniquet. Do not cut the wound or try to suck out venom. Do not use herbal pastes or ointments. Do not go to a traditional healer before going to a hospital. That last point matters most in rural settings, where the delay this creates is the difference between treatment working and not working.

The children's story — days before Raksha Bandhan, their mother overseas, two young lives lost within hours of each other — is painful enough without elaboration. The more useful thing to carry away from it is knowing what to do.

### Bihar tells people not to eat, buy or sell fish from floodwaters — here is why

Strange fish have been appearing along the flooded edges of the [Gandak River](https://www.karmactive.com/bihar-flood-alert-gandak-river/) in Bihar, and the state government wants people to leave them alone.

Bihar's Dairy, Fisheries and Animal Resources Department issued a public advisory on August 31, 2026, telling residents not to catch, buy, sell, or eat any fish they don't recognize that arrived with floodwaters from Nepal. The department asked people to report unusual fish to their District Fisheries Officer or the local administration. Anyone who has already eaten such fish and develops symptoms — vomiting, diarrhoea, stomach pain, dizziness, or breathing difficulty — is advised to seek medical attention immediately.

The fish came from Nepal. Heavy monsoon rains triggered a major flood in the [Bhote Koshi River](https://www.karmactive.com/nepal-floods-bhotekoshi-trishuli-1252-deaths/) in Nepal's Rasuwa district on August 26, 2026. That flood pushed large volumes of water into the Narayani river system, which crosses into India and becomes the Gandak River. As that water moved through the Valmikinagar Barrage in Bihar, large fish were swept downstream into shallow canals, riverbanks, and flooded farmland across West Champaran and Gopalganj districts.

People gathered along the riverbanks near Valmikinagar, Bagaha, and the Gautam Buddha Setu to catch or collect these fish. Some of the fish reported weigh between 10 kg and 90 kg. The Sub-Divisional Magistrate of Bagaha, Chandni Kumari, coordinated the response along with fisheries teams, who were directed to monitor local markets and collect water samples from the river.

The government's concern is not about the size or appearance of the fish. It is about what floodwater carries. When a flood moves through populated areas, it drags along sewage, dead animals, agricultural chemical runoff, and drainage waste into the river channels. Fish swimming through that water are exposed to high bacterial loads — including E. coli and Salmonella — and can absorb chemical contaminants directly into their tissues. Floods also deplete oxygen levels in the water rapidly, causing fish to die or become distressed before anyone catches them.

A dead or weakened fish pulled from contaminated floodwater and left on a September riverbank for hours is not safe food, whatever its size.

The advisory was still active as of September 4. Some reports have described the fish as possibly being goonch catfish, a large Himalayan river species. That identification has not been confirmed by any fisheries authority. The government warning applies to all unidentified fish from floodwaters, regardless of species.
