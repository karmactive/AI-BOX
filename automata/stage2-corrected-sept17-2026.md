# STORY 1: Manitoba Permanent Daylight Time

**Headline:** Manitoba will stop changing clocks this November as province adopts permanent daylight time

**Meta:** Manitoba will not turn clocks back on Nov. 1, 2026 as the province moves to permanent daylight time. Here is what changes for residents.

---

Manitobans have adjusted their clocks twice a year for decades. That ends this fall. Premier Wab Kinew's government announced September 17, 2026 that the province will stay on daylight saving time permanently—meaning clocks will not roll back when November arrives.

The announcement makes Manitoba one of several Canadian provinces and territories to have adopted or moved toward permanent time—a group that includes Saskatchewan, Alberta, British Columbia, Yukon and the Northwest Territories.

The change is straightforward in practical terms. Clocks would normally fall back one hour on November 1, 2026. Under the government's decision, they will not. Manitoba will remain on Central Daylight Time through the winter months, which is one hour ahead of the standard time the province would otherwise return to.

Premier Kinew framed the decision around the disruptions caused by seasonal time changes—the sleep disruption, the adjustment period, the knock-on effects on health that come twice a year. Saskatchewan has operated on a single time standard year-round for decades, and Manitoba's announcement puts it on a similar path.

Manitobans will not make the usual one-hour move back to standard time on November 1, 2026. Winter schedules will hold on daylight time rather than shifting back an hour. Morning darkness will arrive later in the day under permanent daylight time, and evenings will stay lighter longer than residents are used to in winter—though winter sunrise will also occur later than it would under standard time. The change affects work schedules, school routines, travel planning, and any device—phones, computers, smart-home systems—that updates automatically based on time-zone rules. Devices that rely on operating-system time-zone rules will need updated time-zone data; some systems may require manual intervention if they do not update correctly.

The September 17 announcement sits within a longer legislative history that most coverage has glossed over. Manitoba passed an amendment in 2023 designed to eliminate seasonal time changes. That law, however, was never formally proclaimed in force. A further 2025 amendment set March 14, 2027 as the commencement date for those provisions under the Official Time Act. The government's September 17 announcement effectively moves the practical application of permanent daylight time forward by several months, ahead of what the existing legislation had scheduled. The province has not yet released the specific proclamation mechanism that will give the announcement legal effect. [INTERNAL LINK: Manitoba time change history]

**Will Manitoba clocks change on November 1, 2026?**

No, according to the Manitoba government's September 17 announcement. The province says clocks will not return to standard time on Nov. 1, 2026. Manitoba plans to remain on daylight saving time permanently, ending the seasonal one-hour change that residents have followed for decades. # STORY 2: Humpback Whale Grief

**Headline:** Humpback whale stayed close to stillborn calf for hours off Gold Coast. What scientists say the footage actually shows

**Meta:** A humpback whale remained near her dead calf off Australia's Gold Coast in rare footage. A new study says the behaviour is significant—but stops short of calling it grief.

---

Off Australia's Gold Coast, a female humpback whale stayed close to her stillborn calf for hours after it died. Scientists have now documented the behaviour in a peer-reviewed study—and their conclusions are more careful than the word "grief" suggests.

The event was recorded on July 17, 2025, at Palm Beach reef near the Gold Coast. Researchers observed the female humpback from a private vessel and captured footage of the encounter. The study, published September 16, 2026 in the journal Discover Animals, is the first detailed documentation of this kind of behaviour in a baleen whale.

The calf was stillborn. The mother did not attempt to carry or surface the calf. She remained in close proximity to the body for an extended period—hours, and possibly longer, based on the researchers' observations.

The footage is scientifically valuable because it gives marine biologists a comparable record. Post-mortem behaviour has been documented in dolphins, orcas, and other toothed whales—sometimes called odontocetes. But baleen whales, the group that includes humpbacks, are rarely observed in this context. The Gold Coast case adds a baleen-whale data point to a small and growing body of evidence about how cetaceans respond to death. For researchers studying animal cognition and social behaviour, the rarity of the observation makes it worth examining closely, even from a single event. [INTERNAL LINK: whale behaviour and intelligence]

## What the science actually found

The paper's authors describe the behaviour as "post-mortem attentive behaviour." That phrase is deliberate. Grief is a subjective internal state, and scientists cannot measure it directly in a wild animal without access to physiological or neurological data. The researchers say the footage documents sustained proximity and apparent attention to the dead calf—but they explicitly stop short of claiming the whale was grieving in the way humans understand the term.

One detail the paper highlights is particularly telling. The mother did not attempt to bring the calf to the surface. In documented cases involving dolphins, mothers have sometimes tried to push dead calves above the waterline, seemingly attempting to get them to breathe. The humpback mother's behaviour was different. The researchers suggest this may indicate that she recognised the calf was dead shortly after birth, rather than persisting in a response driven by the expectation of a live animal. The observation raises the possibility that the whale responded differently because the calf was non-responsive, but the study cannot establish what the whale understood. [INTERNAL LINK: cetacean cognition research]

The distinction between documented behaviour and interpreted emotion matters for how this study gets reported. The paper records what the whale did. It does not record what she felt. Saying a humpback "grieves" borrows emotional vocabulary that the evidence does not fully support. What the evidence does support is that a humpback whale showed sustained post-mortem attention to her dead calf—behaviour the researchers describe as the first detailed documented occurrence in a baleen species.

**Do whales grieve?**

Evidence suggests some cetaceans show behaviours consistent with grief-like or post-mortem attention, but scientists cannot directly establish subjective grief in wild whales without physiological or neurological measurements. The Gold Coast case is notable because it documents prolonged attention by a humpback whale to a stillborn calf—behaviour documented as the first detailed occurrence in a baleen whale—and gives researchers a new point of comparison with post-mortem behaviour already documented in dolphins and orcas.

The Gold Coast footage now sits in the scientific record as a documented case. Whether more observations follow, and whether they show similar patterns, will shape how researchers interpret this one. The study is available through Discover Animals for those who want to read the researchers' language directly rather than through media summaries.

# STORY 6: T. Rex Body Temperature

**Headline:** Scientists measured T. rex's body temperature using fossil teeth. The result puts it close to a human's

**Meta:** UCLA researchers used the chemistry of fossilized T. rex teeth to estimate the dinosaur's body temperature at around 97°F. The finding is consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism.

---

Researchers have worked out T. rex's body temperature by analysing the chemistry locked inside fossil teeth—and the number they landed on is close to a human's.

A team at UCLA used three fossilized T. rex teeth to estimate the dinosaur's body temperature at approximately 36.3°C, or about 97°F, with an uncertainty of approximately ±2.5°C. The research was published in the journal Science Advances and represents one of the clearest measurements yet of how T. rex regulated heat internally. The result is consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism rather than the cold-blooded biology associated with modern reptiles.

The finding puts T. rex in a thermal range that overlaps with living mammals and birds, not with crocodiles or lizards. A crocodile's body temperature tracks the environment around it. T. rex, if this measurement holds, maintained an internally regulated body temperature consistent with warm-blooded metabolism—which has implications for how it moved, hunted, and functioned as an animal.

The practical weight of this research is what it tells us about T. rex as a living animal. A warm-blooded T. rex would have had higher energy needs than a cold-blooded one, suggesting it required more food and likely had a faster metabolism—though these are biological inferences from the temperature finding, not direct measurements. [INTERNAL LINK: dinosaur physiology and palaeontology]

## How a fossil tooth becomes a thermometer

The technique behind this measurement is called clumped isotope thermometry. It works on a specific chemical property of tooth enamel: the way certain isotopes of carbon and oxygen bond together inside the mineral as it forms. The ratio of those bonded pairs changes with temperature—when enamel forms at higher temperatures, the ratio shifts in a predictable direction.

Tooth enamel is relatively resistant to alteration, allowing researchers to recover chemical information from some fossil specimens. It survives fossilisation better than bone, which means the chemical signals inside it can survive tens of millions of years. The researchers were able to read those signals from three T. rex teeth and calculate the temperature at which the enamel originally formed inside the living animal.

The method has been validated against modern animals with known body temperatures. It has also been used on other fossil species. Applying it to T. rex teeth gave a result of approximately 97°F, which the team at UCLA says is consistent with other lines of evidence pointing toward warm-blooded biology in large theropod dinosaurs. The study used three teeth, which is a small sample. The researchers acknowledge this, and the technique's consistency with other evidence adds context to the measurement. [INTERNAL LINK: palaeoclimate science and fossil chemistry]

This finding applies to T. rex specifically. It does not establish the body temperature of all dinosaurs, and it does not prove every dinosaur species operated the same way. The diversity of the dinosaur family tree means generalising from T. rex to Triceratops, or to small feathered dinosaurs, requires separate evidence.

**How did scientists determine T. rex's body temperature?**

Researchers analysed chemical patterns preserved in fossilized tooth enamel. The technique—clumped isotope thermometry—uses the ratio of certain bonded carbon-oxygen pairs in enamel, which varies with temperature. That ratio was measured in three T. rex teeth, allowing the team to calculate the temperature at which the enamel originally formed inside the living animal. UCLA's result: approximately 97°F (±2.5°C), consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism.

The Science Advances paper is available for researchers and interested readers who want to examine the methodology and sample details. Further work with additional specimens would strengthen the finding. For now, the three teeth have put T. rex's temperature on the record at roughly human body heat.

**Meta:** Researchers have formally described Leopardus tilcayo, a new tiger-cat species from Bolivia's Yungas cloud forests, using genomic analysis and museum specimens dating back over a century.

---

For the first time in more than 100 years, scientists have formally described a living cat species—and its discovery did not come from a dramatic encounter in the wild. It came from museum drawers, decades-old specimens, and modern genetic analysis.

The new species is *Leopardus tilcayo*, named using the name that local communities in the region use to describe the cat. Researchers published the findings in a study titled "Phylogenomics and museomics reveal five distinct species of tiger cats in South America," DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2026.08.059, in the journal Current Biology. The paper resolves a long-running scientific disagreement about the tiger-cat group in South America—a cluster of small spotted cats that have been confused with each other for decades because they look similar.

The Tilcayo cat lives in Bolivia's Yungas—a strip of cloud forest on the eastern slopes of the Andes, where the mountains drop into lowland rainforest. The same research also identified a new subspecies in Peru. The study used both modern genomic sequencing and specimens held in museum collections, some of which had been sitting in storage for decades without anyone recognising what they actually were.

The significance of this finding is not simply that a new cat was spotted. It is that a species was hiding in plain sight in museum collections and in field records, misclassified as a different species because the genetic tools to distinguish them did not exist until recently. For taxonomists and conservation biologists, the distinction matters: a species that is counted as part of a larger, more widespread group appears more secure than one recognised as its own distinct population. Knowing *Leopardus tilcayo* is a separate species changes how its conservation status can be assessed and whether it qualifies for separate legal protection. [INTERNAL LINK: South American wildlife and conservation]

## How you resolve a 100-year-old taxonomy question

The tiger-cat group in South America has been a source of confusion for most of modern zoology. What appeared to be a single widespread species—or at most two—turns out to be five distinct species once genetic analysis is applied alongside the physical evidence.

The study used phylogenomics, which analyses thousands of genetic markers across the genome rather than relying on a handful of specific genes. Combined with museomics—the analysis of genetic material from museum specimens—the researchers were able to trace the evolutionary history of tiger cats across the continent using animals that died before the field of molecular biology existed.

The Yungas habitat where *Leopardus tilcayo* lives is under documented pressure from deforestation and land-use change. Cloud forests in the eastern Andes are among the most biodiverse environments on Earth. The formal recognition of a new species in this habitat adds weight to conservation arguments for protecting the area—but those arguments should be sourced to the actual conservation assessments rather than assumed from the discovery alone. The paper is a taxonomic finding, not a conservation status assessment; that work follows separately. [INTERNAL LINK: Bolivia biodiversity and the Yungas ecosystem]

The researchers have established that *Leopardus tilcayo* exists as a distinct entity, that it has a specific range, and that previous wildlife surveys and protection strategies may have underestimated its distinctiveness.

**What is the Tilcayo cat?**

*Leopardus tilcayo* is a newly described tiger-cat species from Bolivia's Yungas cloud forests on the eastern Andes slopes. It was formally named in a Current Biology paper published in 2026 and is the first new living cat species formally described in more than a century. The name comes from what local communities call the cat. The research used genomic analysis and museum specimens to distinguish it from other tiger-cat species in South America. The same study also identified a new tiger-cat subspecies in Peru.

The Current Biology paper is the primary record for this species description. Further ecological surveys of the Yungas are expected to follow as researchers work to understand the population size, range, and conservation needs of *Leopardus tilcayo* more precisely.

---

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter of a percentage point on September 16, 2026, moving the federal funds target range to 3.75–4%. The vote among Federal Open Market Committee members was 12–0—unanimous, with no dissent.

The Fed's own statement explains the decision in direct terms: inflation remains elevated, and today's policy action will support a timelier return to the committee's 2% goal. The statement cites the Fed's dual mandate of price stability and maximum employment as the framework for the decision.

The practical impact of this rate increase works through the financial system from the Fed's overnight lending rate outward. Higher rates make it more expensive for banks to borrow money from each other, and those costs tend to be reflected in consumer and business lending—though the timing and amount vary by product and market. Credit card rates, which are typically variable and tied closely to the federal funds rate, tend to move quickly. Mortgage rates are influenced more by longer-term bond yields than directly by the Fed funds rate, but those yields often respond to Fed decisions as well. Variable-rate loans—including home equity lines of credit and some personal loans—typically move up within billing cycles. Fixed-rate products do not change, which is one reason refinancing decisions become more consequential when rates are rising. [INTERNAL LINK: personal finance and borrowing costs]

## What the FOMC statement actually says

The Fed's September 16 statement is more measured than market commentary about it. The statement says job gains "have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little." It also states plainly: "Inflation remains elevated."

The statement notes the Fed's dual mandate as the foundation for the decision and makes clear that future moves depend on incoming economic and financial data rather than a pre-set path. The committee can raise, hold, or cut at future meetings.

The 12–0 vote is notable because it represents a unanimous judgment among all voting FOMC members in support of the decision. [INTERNAL LINK: Federal Reserve policy and inflation]

The implementation note released alongside the main statement sets the technical rates that define the boundaries of the new target range in practice, including the interest rate on reserve balances and the overnight reverse repurchase agreement rate.

**What does the Fed rate hike mean for borrowers?**

A higher federal funds target puts upward pressure on borrowing costs across the economy, though not immediately and not uniformly. Credit card rates are typically variable and tied to the federal funds rate. Variable-rate loans—including home equity lines of credit—adjust at similar speed. Mortgage rates are influenced more by bond markets than by the Fed's overnight rate directly, but bond markets tend to respond to Fed decisions. Fixed-rate loans do not change for existing borrowers. Savers with high-yield savings accounts or money market funds may see some benefit, though returns depend on how institutions respond. The net effect depends on how much debt a household carries and at what rate, and on how much savings they hold.

The next FOMC meeting will determine whether the Fed pauses, continues raising, or signals a change in direction. The September statement makes clear that depends on economic data between now and then. Watch for incoming economic and financial data in the coming weeks—those are the inputs the committee has said will guide future decisions.
