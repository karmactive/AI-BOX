**Amitabh Bachchan Stepped Away From Social Media on Doctor's Orders. Does a Digital Break Actually Help?**

Bollywood legend Amitabh Bachchan announced a temporary pause from social media this week after doctors advised him to rest. The news sent searches spiking — but it also raised a question a lot of people have been wondering about: does stepping away from your phone and social media actually do anything for your mental health?

A major scientific review published on NIH PubMed Central screened 2,578 studies and narrowed it down to 10 controlled trials — seven of which were randomized controlled trials — covering a combined 2,503 participants. The researchers wanted to know whether intentionally cutting back on social media use had any measurable effect on mental health.

The answer is: it depends on what you're measuring.

For depression specifically, the results were clear. People who reduced or stopped social media use showed a statistically significant drop in depressive symptoms — a standardized mean difference of -0.29 on the clinical scale used, with a p-value of 0.01. In plain terms, that means the improvement wasn't a fluke. Critically, the studies were consistent with each other (I² = 20%), which adds confidence to the finding.

But for other things people expect unplugging to fix — general stress, life satisfaction, overall well-being — the science didn't back it up. Changes in those areas were not statistically significant across the trials.

The reason depression specifically responds to a digital break likely comes down to what social media does to the brain. Scrolling through curated highlight reels of other people's lives triggers constant social comparison, which increases cortisol — the stress hormone. Stepping back interrupts that loop. It also gives the brain space to engage in face-to-face conversations, which trigger oxytocin, the hormone linked to social bonding.

You don't need to quit cold turkey to see a benefit. Individual trials within the review found that cutting daily social media use by just 15 to 20 minutes — or taking a dedicated 7-day break — was enough to move the needle. The World Health Organization puts the global number of people affected by depression at 280 million, with another 264 million dealing with anxiety. Mental health conditions have been rising at 13% per year globally.

Amitabh Bachchan stepping back on medical advice is a personal decision — but the clinical evidence suggests that even a modest, intentional break from the feed can do something real, at least for people dealing with depression.

### ARTICLE 4
**Scientists Just Fixed a 178-Year-Old Train Story — and It Changes What We Know About Solar Storms**

For over 150 years, history books recorded the same story: a powerful solar storm disrupted a train on England's Exeter line on October 18, 1841. Scientists from several institutions recently took a closer look — and they found the story had a typo at its core.

The fix mattered. A lot.

Researchers from Lancaster University, RMIT University, the British Geological Survey, Natural Resources Canada, Baylor University, and RAL Space analyzed the original 1871 Nature paper that first reported the train disruption. The paper described a geomagnetic storm powerful enough to delay the 10:05 p.m. train from Exeter by 16 minutes. The only problem: the Exeter-to-Starcross railway line didn't open until 1846. In 1841, the train simply couldn't have been delayed on a line that didn't yet exist.

Associate Professor Brett Carter of RMIT University led the dating correction. Cross-referencing historical timetables from the Trewman's Exeter Flying Post, auroral observation records, and digitized geomagnetic data confirmed the real date was October 18, 1848 — seven years later than the 1871 paper claimed. The study was published in the AGU journal Space Weather.

Moving the Exeter event to 1848 reshuffled the historical timeline. The earliest confirmed case of space weather disrupting human technology is now the March 1847 geomagnetic interference with Britain's Midland Railway telegraph system — not a train signal delay, but a disruption to communication infrastructure that was, even then, critical to running the railways.

Prof. Jim Wild of Lancaster University put the bigger picture plainly: "What this research highlights is that space weather is not a new threat but a long-standing natural hazard. Society has been experiencing the effects of space weather on technology for almost as long as electrical technologies have existed."

The 1859 Carrington Event — still the most powerful solar storm ever recorded — knocked out telegraph systems across Europe and North America. Operators reported receiving shocks from their equipment. Auroras were visible as far south as Hawaii and Central America. Telegraph lines carried electric current without batteries because the geomagnetic disturbance was supplying it directly.

Modern power grids, rail signaling networks, and satellite systems face the same threat. Dr. Mike Hapgood of RAL Space noted that piecing together 19th-century records strengthens the forecasting tools being developed as global infrastructure prepares for the heightened solar activity expected in the 2030s.

### ARTICLE 5
**A Rock From Papua New Guinea Just Told Scientists Something Surprising About Earth's Interior**

A small piece of rock collected from a beach in Papua New Guinea has upended some long-standing assumptions about how the inside of the Earth works.

The rock came from Taleba Bay on Goodenough Island, in the Milne Bay Province of Papua New Guinea. To look at it, it's a red-and-green stone — an eclogite. But what's inside it is what made scientists at the University of Göttingen pay close attention. Trapped within tiny red garnet crystals were microscopic fragments of a mineral called coesite. Each fragment is less than 1 micrometer across — roughly one-hundredth the width of a human hair.

Coesite only forms under enormous pressure. Pressure you can only find at least 90 kilometers below the Earth's surface. The fact that coesite is in this rock tells geoscientists — led by Dr. Jan Schönig at the University of Göttingen — that this rock was once buried that deep inside a tectonic subduction zone, where one of Earth's plates was being pushed beneath another. It then made its way back to the surface over four million years.

The research, funded by the German Research Foundation and published in Nature Geoscience, found something even more unexpected when the team analyzed the rock's temperature history.

At 90 kilometers down, the rock experienced around 800°C — extremely hot. But at 45 kilometers depth, roughly halfway up, the temperature was only about 700°C. That's just 100 degrees cooler, despite being at only half the depth. In a normal subduction zone, temperature drops more gradually as you get shallower. Here, the rock was almost as hot at 45 km as it was at 90 km.

That tells scientists the subduction process itself changed as the rock descended. It started as "hot" subduction — possibly because the plates were grinding against each other and generating heat through friction, or because the subduction zone was still young — and then shifted to "cold" subduction as the rock went deeper.

Dr. Schönig described the finding directly: "Finding this change from hot to cold subduction was unexpected as this contradicts our current thermal models of subduction zones."

Why does this matter beyond geology? Subduction zones are one of the main ways the Earth recycles carbon. When rock gets pushed deep underground, it carries carbon with it. When conditions change — like when the temperature gradient shifts — that affects how much carbon gets stored versus how much eventually makes its way back toward the surface. Over millions of years, that process plays a significant role in regulating the planet's long-term climate.

What started as a piece of rock on a beach turns out to be a record of processes deep inside the planet that affect everything from earthquake risk to long-term carbon cycling.
