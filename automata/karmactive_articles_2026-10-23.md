**Ron Duguay's Death at 69 Puts a Spotlight on a Cancer That's Hitting Younger People Hard**

Ron Duguay died on October 22, 2026. He was 69 years old, and colon cancer — Stage 4 — took his life. The former New York Rangers forward was one of hockey's most recognizable players, known just as much for his long hair and charisma as for his play on the ice.

Over 12 seasons in the NHL, Duguay suited up for the Rangers, Detroit Red Wings, Los Angeles Kings, and Pittsburgh Penguins. He played 864 regular-season games and racked up 620 points — 274 goals and 346 assists. In 89 playoff games, he added another 53 points. On April 6, 1980, he scored just nine seconds into a game, tying the Rangers' fastest-ever goal record.

His passing at 69 is a reminder of how deadly colon cancer can be — but what the latest American Cancer Society numbers say about who's getting it might actually surprise you.

Although colorectal cancer remains more common among older adults, incidence is rising sharply in younger age groups. The American Cancer Society's 2026 report projects 158,850 new cases and 55,230 deaths in the United States this year alone. That makes it the second leading cause of cancer death overall — and the leading cause among adults under 50.

Here's the shift that's alarming researchers: while the rate of new cases is dropping 2.5% per year among people 65 and older, it's climbing 3% per year among adults aged 20 to 49. In people aged 50 to 64, it's going up 0.4% per year. Rectal cancer has grown significantly too, now accounting for 32% of all colorectal cancer cases — up from 27% in the mid-2000s.

What makes this even more urgent is how late younger patients are typically diagnosed. About three in four adults under 50 who get colorectal cancer are already at an advanced stage when they find out. Twenty-seven percent have cancer that has already spread to distant parts of the body.

The numbers look even sharper when broken down by community. Alaska Native people face the highest colorectal cancer rates in the world — 81 cases per 100,000 people, with a mortality rate of 32 per 100,000. Among racial groups, incidence is rising 4% per year in Hispanic populations, 3% per year in White, Asian American, Native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander, and American Indian/Alaska Native populations, and 2% per year in Black populations.

Dr. Ahmedin Jemal, Senior Vice President at the American Cancer Society, put it plainly: "It's clear that colorectal cancer can no longer be called an old person's disease. We must double down on research to pinpoint what is driving this tsunami of cancer in generations born since 1950."

Because about half of all colorectal cancers in people under 50 occur between ages 45 and 49, the American Cancer Society recommends that average-risk adults start screening at 45 and continue through 75. If a stool test comes back positive, getting a colonoscopy quickly matters. Limiting alcohol, cutting processed meat, staying physically active, and getting checked for symptoms like rectal bleeding, ongoing stomach pain, or iron-deficiency anemia can all make a difference in catching this early.

Ron Duguay's career is what made him famous. His death at 69 — from a cancer now increasingly striking people in their 30s and 40s — is what makes this story matter now.

Biles's property attracted a buyer much faster than the national median, though the pace and price of sales vary widely depending on location and market conditions. ### ARTICLE 3 — FINAL
**Amitabh Bachchan Stepped Away From Social Media on Doctor's Orders. Does a Digital Break Actually Help?**

Bollywood legend Amitabh Bachchan told followers on October 21 that he had been advised to rest and recuperate for several days, leaving him unable to maintain his usual written updates. He's 84 years old and one of India's most prolific stars, still active across platforms with millions of followers. The news sent searches spiking — but it also raised a question a lot of people have been wondering about: does stepping away from your phone and social media actually do anything for your mental health?

A major scientific review published on NIH PubMed Central screened 2,578 studies and narrowed it down to 10 controlled trials — seven of which were randomized controlled trials — covering a combined 2,503 participants. The researchers wanted to know whether intentionally cutting back on social media use had any measurable effect on mental health.

The answer is: it depends on what you're measuring.

For depression specifically, the results were clear. People who reduced or stopped social media use showed a statistically significant drop in depressive symptoms — a standardized mean difference of -0.29 on the clinical scale used, with a p-value of 0.01. In plain terms, that means the improvement was statistically meaningful. Critically, the studies were consistent with each other (I² = 20%), which adds confidence to the finding.

But for other things people expect unplugging to fix — general stress, life satisfaction, overall well-being — the science didn't back it up. Changes in those areas were not statistically significant across the trials.

Researchers have suggested that reducing social comparison and making more time for offline activities may help explain the benefits, though the precise biological pathways remain uncertain.

You don't need to quit cold turkey to see a benefit. Individual trials within the review found that cutting daily social media use by just 15 to 20 minutes — or taking a dedicated 7-day break — was enough to move the needle. The World Health Organization puts the global number of people affected by depression at 280 million, with another 301 million affected by anxiety disorders. WHO has reported a 13% rise in mental health conditions and substance-use disorders over a recent decade.

Amitabh Bachchan stepping back on medical advice is a personal decision — but the clinical evidence suggests that even a modest, intentional break from the feed can do something real, at least for people dealing with depression.

### ARTICLE 4 — FINAL
**Scientists Just Fixed a 178-Year-Old Train Story — and It Changes What We Know About Solar Storms**

For over 150 years, history books recorded the same story: a powerful solar storm disrupted a train on England's Exeter line on October 18, 1841. Scientists from several institutions recently took a closer look — and they found the story had a typo at its core.

The fix mattered. A lot.

Researchers from Lancaster University, RMIT University, the British Geological Survey, Natural Resources Canada, Baylor University, and RAL Space analyzed the original 1871 Nature paper that first reported the train disruption. The paper described a geomagnetic storm powerful enough to delay the 10:05 p.m. train from Exeter by 16 minutes. The only problem: the Exeter-to-Starcross railway line didn't open until 1846. In 1841, the train simply couldn't have been delayed on a line that didn't yet exist.

The study was led by Professor James Wild of Lancaster University, with Associate Professor Brett Carter of RMIT University among the researchers who investigated the historical archives. Cross-referencing historical railway timetables from the Trewman's Exeter Flying Post, auroral observation records, and digitized geomagnetic data led the researchers to conclude the event most likely occurred on October 18, 1848 — seven years later than the 1871 paper claimed. The study was published in the AGU journal Space Weather.

Moving the Exeter event to 1848 reshuffled the historical timeline. The earliest credible documented report of space weather disrupting human technology is now the March 1847 geomagnetic interference with Britain's Midland Railway telegraph system — not a train signal delay, but a disruption to communication infrastructure that was, even then, critical to running the railways.

Prof. Jim Wild of Lancaster University put the bigger picture plainly: "What this research highlights is that space weather is not a new threat but a long-standing natural hazard. Society has been experiencing the effects of space weather on technology for almost as long as electrical technologies have existed."

The 1859 Carrington Event — widely regarded as one of the most powerful geomagnetic storms in the historical record — knocked out telegraph systems across Europe and North America. Operators reported receiving shocks from their equipment. Auroras were visible as far south as Hawaii and Central America. Telegraph lines carried electric current without batteries because the geomagnetic disturbance was supplying it directly.

The research also provides historical evidence that may help scientists better understand the vulnerability of modern power grids, rail signaling networks, and satellite systems to geomagnetic disturbances.

### ARTICLE 5 — FINAL
**A Rock From Papua New Guinea Just Told Scientists Something Surprising About Earth's Interior**

A small piece of rock collected from a beach in Papua New Guinea has upended some long-standing assumptions about how the inside of the Earth works.

The rock came from Taleba Bay on Goodenough Island, in the Milne Bay Province of Papua New Guinea. To look at it, it's a red-and-green stone — an eclogite. But what's inside it is what made scientists at the University of Göttingen pay close attention. Trapped within tiny red garnet crystals were microscopic fragments of a mineral called coesite. Each fragment is less than 1 micrometer across — roughly one-hundredth the width of a human hair.

Coesite forms under extremely high pressures, and its presence in this eclogite provides evidence that the rock reached depths exceeding approximately 90 kilometers. The fact that coesite is in this rock tells geoscientists — led by Dr. Jan Schönig at the University of Göttingen — that this rock was once buried that deep inside a tectonic subduction zone, where one of Earth's plates was being pushed beneath another. The rock was subducted more than 90 km below the surface approximately four million years ago before eventually returning to the surface.

The research, funded by the German Research Foundation and published in Nature Geoscience, found something even more unexpected when the team analyzed the rock's temperature history.

At 90 kilometers down, the rock experienced around 800°C — extremely hot. But at 45 kilometers depth, roughly halfway through its burial, the temperature was only about 700°C. That's just 100 degrees cooler, despite being at only half the depth. In a normal subduction zone, temperature drops more gradually as you get shallower. Here, the rock was almost as hot at 45 km as it was at 90 km.

The researchers reconstructed an initially warm subduction regime that transitioned to colder conditions at greater depth, which challenges conventional thermal models of subduction zones.

Dr. Schönig described the finding directly: "Finding this change from hot to cold subduction was unexpected as this contradicts our current thermal models of subduction zones."

Why does this matter beyond geology? Subduction zones are one of the main ways the Earth recycles carbon. When rock gets pushed deep underground, it carries carbon with it. When conditions change — like when the temperature gradient shifts — that affects how much carbon gets stored versus how much eventually makes its way back toward the surface. What started as a piece of rock on a beach turns out to be a record of processes deep inside the planet that influence how carbon is stored and cycled over geological timescales.
