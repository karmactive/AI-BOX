## 1. Why Is Beef So Expensive? Now the Government Is Asking Retailers.

Beef prices have climbed nearly 10% over the past year, and the federal government wants to know why — and whether grocery stores played a role in keeping prices high.

The U.S. Department of Justice has widened its antitrust investigation into beef pricing. It started with the big meatpackers. Now it has reached the checkout line. The DOJ has sent information requests to eight major retailers: Walmart, Kroger, Costco, Amazon, Publix, Albertsons, Aldi, and Ahold Delhaize.

This matters because federal Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows beef and veal prices rose 9.4% year over year through July 2026. Uncooked ground beef was up 9.0%. Beef roasts jumped 13.5%. Steaks climbed 9.6%.

But there is a key distinction that often gets lost in the headlines. Getting an information request from the DOJ is not the same as being accused of wrongdoing. No retailer has been charged with anything. The investigation is in an information-gathering phase.

Part of the price story also comes down to basic supply. USDA counted 28.5 million beef cows on July 1, 2026 — down 1% from a year earlier. The 2026 calf crop came in at 32.5 million, down 2%. Fewer cattle means tighter supply, and tighter supply tends to push prices up regardless of what any company does.

The processing side of the industry is also highly concentrated. The four largest meatpackers handle about 85% of steer and heifer purchases, according to USDA's Economic Research Service. But the same agency notes that concentration alone does not prove anticompetitive behavior or unlawful market power.

This is not the first time federal investigators have looked at meat markets in 2026. In May, the DOJ took action involving Agri Stats, a data firm, over concerns about the sharing of competitively sensitive information among meat processors.

USDA's August 2026 outlook projected total beef production at 24.967 billion pounds this year, with tighter cattle supplies expected to support cattle prices into 2027.

The investigation has not established that retailers did anything wrong. What it has established is that the federal government wants a closer look at how beef moves from the ranch to the supermarket shelf — and who profits at each step along the way.

## 2. Labor Day Weekend Weather: Check Your Region Before You Pack

This Labor Day weekend is not one forecast. It's several — and which one applies to you depends entirely on where you're headed.

Across the Great Lakes and Mid-Atlantic region, forecasters are warning of strong to severe thunderstorms with locally heavy rain. If you're planning outdoor events in that corridor, have a backup plan ready.

The Central U.S. is dealing with persistent heat. Not a brief spike — sustained heat that stretches through the long weekend.

In the Desert Southwest, the monsoon season is adding flash-flood potential. Rain in the desert sounds harmless, but desert soil doesn't absorb water well. Heavy rain can produce fast-moving floods on roads that look perfectly dry just minutes before.

Southern areas are facing both heat and afternoon thunderstorms. The National Weather Service in Atlanta issued a Heat Advisory for the greater Atlanta area and northwest Georgia, with heat index values forecast in the low to mid 100s — around 106°F in some parts of northwest Georgia. That advisory extends through Saturday evening, September 5.

Along Southern California's coastline, the weather risk is different. NWS Los Angeles identified a high risk of strong rip currents and large surf during the holiday weekend. Tropical Cyclone Marie was expected to remain offshore, but even a storm that doesn't make landfall can push dangerous swells and rip currents toward beaches. NWS Los Angeles advises swimming only near lifeguards.

A rip current doesn't feel dangerous until you're in one. It pulls swimmers away from shore faster than most adults can swim. If you get caught in one, swim parallel to the shore rather than fighting the current directly toward the beach.

Before you leave home this weekend, check the NWS forecast for your exact destination — not just the city, but the specific area. Check severe weather alerts right before any outdoor event, not just in the morning. If you're heading to the beach, look specifically for rip current and surf warnings, which are separate from standard temperature forecasts. And have an indoor option ready for any outdoor gathering, because conditions can shift quickly within a single afternoon.

## 4. State Farm Is Sending $5 Billion Back to Auto Customers. Do You Qualify?

State Farm is returning $5 billion to its auto policyholders. The company announced it has already begun sending out payments — and for many drivers, that means a check is on the way.

This is the largest auto policyholder dividend in State Farm's history. It covers more than 49 million insured vehicles. The average payment works out to about $100 per vehicle, though the exact amount varies depending on which state you're in and how much you paid in premiums during 2025.

To qualify, you need to have held an active State Farm Mutual auto policy during the 2025 calendar year. State Farm — not a competitor, not an affiliated company, but State Farm Mutual specifically — is the issuing entity here.

Payments started going out on July 31, 2026. Depending on your account, you may receive a credit applied directly to your bill, a paper check, or the option to claim payment through State Farm's online portal.

This is a dividend, not a premium refund. State Farm has been clear about that distinction. A refund means you overpaid. A dividend is a distribution that a mutual insurance company can issue to policyholders when it performs well financially. State Farm's auto underwriting improved significantly — the company reported a property-and-casualty underwriting gain of $1.5 billion in 2025, compared to a $6.1 billion underwriting loss in 2024.

State Farm has also separately reduced auto insurance rates in 40 states by roughly 10% on average, generating about $4.6 billion in annual premium savings.

One thing to watch out for: State Farm has flagged phishing scams where fraudsters contact policyholders claiming to need banking information or debit card numbers to process dividend payments. State Farm handles this through its official portal. If someone calls or texts asking for your bank details to release your dividend check, that's a scam.

The dividend amount in your state may differ from what you've seen reported elsewhere. State Farm says the $100 average is exactly that — an average, not a guaranteed per-vehicle amount.

## 5. Atlanta's Labor Day Weekend Comes With a Heat Warning You Should Take Seriously

If you're in the Atlanta area this Labor Day weekend, the biggest concern isn't rain or storms. It's the heat.

The National Weather Service issued a Heat Advisory for the greater Atlanta metropolitan area and parts of North Georgia. Heat index values — that's the temperature your body actually feels when you factor in humidity — are forecast to reach the low to mid 100s. In northwest Georgia, the heat index is expected to hit around 106°F. The advisory runs through Saturday evening, September 5.

There's an important difference between air temperature and heat index. The thermometer outside might read 95°F. But when Georgia's late-summer humidity combines with that temperature, your body experiences something closer to 106°F. Humidity slows down the body's ability to cool itself through sweating. That's when heat stops being uncomfortable and starts being dangerous.

The Atlanta airport forecast also showed the Heat Advisory flag extending into that same Saturday window, which matters for anyone traveling through or arriving for outdoor events.

Heat-related illness moves faster than most people expect. Early signs include heavy sweating, weakness, cold or pale skin, and nausea. If someone stops sweating despite extreme heat, that's a medical emergency — move them to a cool area immediately and call for help.

For outdoor events this weekend: stay in the shade where possible, drink water before you feel thirsty, and check on anyone who may be more vulnerable to heat — elderly neighbors, young children, and anyone working or exercising outdoors.

This is not a "hot and muggy" advisory. A formal Heat Advisory means conditions are expected to cause significant discomfort and potential health risks. Plan accordingly.

That storm had estimated peak winds of 75 mph, carved a path 0.35 miles long and 175 yards wide between 7:19 and 7:21 p.m. EDT, and caused sporadic tree damage. There were no fatalities and no injuries.

The following afternoon, on Thursday, September 3, a separate severe storm tracked across Wayne County and hit Detroit's east side.

On Friday morning, September 4, the NWS dispatched a storm survey team to inspect damage along a corridor stretching from Hamtramck across the east side toward Belle Isle. The team documented snapped utility poles, uprooted trees, and damaged roofs. On Friday afternoon, the National Weather Service confirmed the tornado, and Detroit Mayor Mary Sheffield also publicly confirmed the event.

The confirmed Detroit tornado is Michigan's 28th of 2026 and the first to strike inside Detroit city limits since the July 2, 1997 outbreak — a span of nearly 30 years.

Detroit's network of 56 outdoor warning sirens covering seven districts activated as warnings were issued ahead of Thursday's storm, giving residents time to seek shelter before the tornado struck.

Anyone in the Detroit area during a tornado warning should immediately move to a basement, an interior room on the lowest floor, or a designated safe room away from windows — consistent with guidance from the City of Detroit's own preparedness resources.

## 8. Can You Bring Peanut Butter Through Airport Security? Here's the Actual Rule.

Peanut butter is not banned from airplanes. But a full jar of it can get pulled at security — and here's why.

The TSA classifies peanut butter as a liquid or gel-type substance for carry-on screening purposes. That might sound strange, but the reasoning is consistent. TSA applies its liquid/gel framework to any food that can be poured, pumped, spread, or sprayed. Peanut butter spreads. So does hummus. So does gravy, salsa, yogurt, and soft cheese. They all fall under the same rule.

The rule is called the 3-1-1 rule. Containers of liquids or gels in your carry-on bag must be 3.4 ounces (100 milliliters) or less. All of those containers must fit in a single clear, quart-sized bag. One bag per traveler.

So a small snack-sized packet of peanut butter — under 3.4 ounces — is fine in your carry-on. A standard 16-ounce jar is not. That jar needs to go in your checked luggage.

Here's where it gets a little counterintuitive. If you made a peanut butter sandwich before you left home, that sandwich is fine. A pre-made solid food item is treated differently from the spread on its own. TSA classifies it as a solid, not a liquid.

Medically necessary foods and baby formula are handled separately — they're exempt from the 3.4-ounce limit if you declare them at the checkpoint.

The main practical takeaway: if you're traveling with a full jar of peanut butter, pack it in checked luggage. If you're carrying a small individual-serving pack, you're fine. And if a TSA officer asks for additional screening on any food item, that's within their standard authority even if the item appears to meet the size requirement.

Peanut butter isn't the problem. The jar size is.

## 9. Hurricane Lowell Is Weakening — But It's Still a Major Storm Near Hawaii

Hurricane Lowell briefly reached Category 5 strength this week before weakening. By Thursday morning, it had come down further. But it's still a major hurricane, and it's still in the Central Pacific.

The National Hurricane Center's September 4 advisory placed Lowell at maximum sustained winds of 125 mph and a central pressure of 947 mb. It was moving west at about 8 mph. At the time of that advisory — 5 a.m. Hawaii Standard Time — the storm was approximately 600 miles south of Lihue and 570 miles southwest of Honolulu.

No coastal watches or warnings were in effect for Hawaii at the time of that advisory. The NHC noted that watches could become necessary for portions of the westernmost Hawaiian Islands by Saturday.

Here is the intensity timeline so far. On September 2, an NHC discussion briefly classified Lowell as a Category 5 hurricane. Later that day, NOAA's hurricane hunters measured maximum sustained winds of 155 mph, and the storm was classified as Category 4. By September 3, it had come down to 140 mph. The September 4 advisory placed it at 125 mph.

Weakening is a relative word in hurricane forecasting. A storm at 125 mph sustained winds still has enough energy to generate dangerous surf, strong ocean swells, and hazardous marine conditions well beyond its immediate center. Hawaii's shores can see wave impacts before the storm itself gets anywhere close to land.

The NHC will continue issuing updated advisories. Forecasts for storms at this distance change frequently — track and intensity can shift in either direction between advisories.

As of the September 4 advisory, Lowell was moving west and no coastal watches or warnings had been issued for Hawaii. That can change. Anyone in Hawaii should check the National Hurricane Center and Central Pacific Hurricane Center directly for the most current advisory before making any weekend decisions.
