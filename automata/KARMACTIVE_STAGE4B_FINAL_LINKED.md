## STORY 1: ChatGPT Water Use—What Sam Altman Really Said (And What Matters)

**Word count: ~460 | External links: 2 | Internal links: 2 | Total: 4**
**Ratio: ~1 link per 115 words ✓**

Using ChatGPT can have a water footprint, depending on how water use is accounted for across the computing and electricity systems involved. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said—while acknowledging he was recalling the figure from memory—that roughly 38,000 ChatGPT queries use as much water as producing one California almond. A California almond requires between 1.1 and 3.2 gallons of water to grow, which calculates to roughly 0.1 to 0.3 milliliters of water per query under Altman's estimate.

One difficulty in comparing AI water-use estimates is that studies don't always use the same definition of "water use."

Water-footprint studies can include direct cooling-water consumption, water associated with electricity generation, and, depending on the study boundary, water used elsewhere in the technology supply chain. These aren't interchangeable numbers.

There's the water that evaporates directly from cooling towers on the facility's roof. There's water consumed at distant power plants that generate electricity for the [data center](https://www.karmactive.com/ais-growing-energy-demand-data-centers-to-triple-power-use-by-2030/). And then there's water used when the computer chips themselves were manufactured.

In 2025, [Google published a methodology for measuring Gemini inference and reported a median water consumption of 0.26 mL per Gemini Apps text prompt](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference#:~:text=0.26%20mL).

Modern data centers use a range of cooling systems. Evaporative cooling remains common, while closed-loop, dry and hybrid systems are also being deployed. Closed-loop systems recirculate water instead of constantly dumping it and pulling in new supplies. But in evaporative and recirculating systems, water minerals build up over time, requiring periodic flushing and fresh water input. The Department of Energy specifically discusses cooling towers as sources of water consumption and describes direct-liquid cooling and closed-loop approaches as alternatives being explored in federal facilities.

The more significant question isn't whether a single ChatGPT query uses much water. It's whether the total water footprint of billions of [AI infrastructure](https://www.karmactive.com/ai-data-centers-water-pollution-community-impact-2026/) operations across the world strains local water supplies—especially in areas already experiencing drought. [A 2023 study estimated that training GPT-3 could have consumed about 700,000 liters of freshwater](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271#:~:text=700%2C000) through on-site evaporation under the study's assumptions.

There's a meaningful difference between what one query uses and what global AI deployment requires. Altman also argued that discussions about AI water consumption have become detached from actual measurements.

AI infrastructure uses some water, and across billions of daily operations at scale, the combined effect matters. Especially in water-stressed regions like California, where tech infrastructure is concentrated and water resources are limited. And in places like Texas and Arizona, where data centers are rapidly expanding, regional water availability is a growing consideration.

The conversation needs to move past individual query estimates toward understanding the cumulative water impact of AI infrastructure expansion in water-stressed regions. Researchers are actively studying this, though the full future scale and regional distribution of demand remains uncertain.

## STORY 3: Magnitude 3.5 Earthquake Shook Santa Rosa—But Why People Felt It

**Word count: ~360 | External links: 2 | Internal links: 1 | Total: 3**
**Ratio: ~1 link per 120 words ✓**

At 10:33 a.m. on September 3, [seismic instruments recorded a magnitude 3.5 earthquake near Larkfield-Wikiup, close to Santa Rosa in Sonoma County](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/eventpage/nc75429272/dyfi). The quake occurred about 7.4 kilometers underground—roughly 4.6 miles deep.

People felt the shaking across a wide area. The shaking registered as Instrumental Intensity IV (Light) in Santa Rosa and Larkfield-Wikiup. That's the category where you definitely notice movement, objects might shift on shelves, and people stop what they're doing.

But here's what matters: magnitude 3.5 is small. A magnitude 3.5 earthquake is generally unlikely to cause significant damage, although shaking can still be noticeable. This wasn't a major earthquake. The public question isn't "was this a big quake" but rather "how can a small earthquake shake so many people?"

The answer is simple. The epicenter was shallow—only 7.4 kilometers deep. Depth affects how seismic energy reaches the surface. Shallower earthquakes tend to produce stronger surface shaking in the area directly above the event, which is why a relatively modest magnitude can still be widely felt across a populated region.

The earthquake occurred along the [active fault system](https://www.karmactive.com/san-leandro-m38-earthquake-bay-area-hayward-fault/) that's part of the broader San Andreas Fault zone. USGS describes the total slip rate on the Rodgers Creek Fault as approximately 9 mm per year, with creep in some sections estimated at up to 6 mm per year.

No surface rupture was reported. No structural damage was reported. No injuries resulted from the event.

One important clarification: a small earthquake by itself does not provide a reliable prediction that a larger earthquake is imminent. This is the misconception that causes the most concern after minor seismic events.

Seismologists have long sought reliable earthquake precursors—warning signs before major events. No broadly reliable precursor has been established. Small quakes and large quakes follow different patterns. Faults can accumulate strain over long periods and release it in larger earthquakes; minor tremors are not evidence that this buildup is being safely discharged.

The September 3 earthquake was newsworthy because it was widely felt. But it was a minor seismic event, and the [Rodgers Creek Fault continues to be closely monitored](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/search/) as part of California's broader seismic hazard management.

## STORY 4: Beneath Quiet English Farmland Lies a 454-Million-Year-Old Volcano

**Word count: ~480 | External links: 1 | Internal links: 2 | Total: 3**
**Ratio: ~1 link per 160 words ✓**

Beneath The Wash—a flat coastal area in Norfolk and Lincolnshire—geologists found evidence of an ancient volcanic system that erupted 454 million years ago. Headlines called it a "supervolcano," but that label requires serious scientific qualification.

Here's what researchers actually discovered. Deep borehole samples pulled from locations 65 kilometers apart contained tiny zircon crystals. These crystals formed in extreme volcanic heat and preserve a chemical signature matching a massive volcanic ash deposit called Kinnekulle tephra, found across Norway, Sweden, the Baltic region, Belarus, and Poland. [British Geological Survey researchers](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/news/secrets-of-an-ancient-supervolcano-uncovered-that-change-our-understanding-of-englands-geological-past/#:~:text=454%20million) and scientists from the University of Oslo linked these findings to a concealed volcanic complex beneath eastern England.

The discovery matters because it rewrites understanding of Britain's geological past. During the Ordovician Period—roughly 454 million years ago—the geography was completely different. England didn't exist as we know it. The region was a collision zone where tectonic plates were converging, generating the magma chambers that powered extensive [volcanic](https://www.karmactive.com/axial-seamount-seafloor-inflates-with-200-daily-quakes-signaling-possible-2026-eruption/) activity.

This wasn't random. Tectonic collisions drive volcanism. The Caledonian Orogeny, as geologists call this mountain-building event, shaped modern Scotland, Scandinavia, and surrounding areas.

Now, the "supervolcano" question. In formal volcanological terms, a supervolcano means a volcano that produced an eruption rated VEI-8 on the Volcanic Explosivity Index—that's more than 1,000 cubic kilometers of erupted material. BGS describes the buried system as a supervolcano and indicates the available evidence points to an eruption involving several hundred to around a thousand cubic kilometers of material. Reconstructing the exact original eruption volume precisely is difficult after hundreds of millions of years of burial and chemical change, but the scale of the eruption was substantial.

What's scientifically clear is this: a major volcanic complex existed beneath eastern England during the Ordovician. It was significant enough to distribute ash across multiple European countries, from Scandinavia to the Baltic and into what is now Belarus and Poland.

Is it dangerous today? The system is an ancient geological feature and is not regarded as an [active volcano](https://www.karmactive.com/mount-etna-eruption-ash-cloud-catania-airport/) today. It last erupted before dinosaurs even existed.

The real scientific interest is what this discovery tells us about Earth's past. It shows how plate tectonics drove volcanism, how continents collided and shaped modern landscapes, and how far-reaching volcanic events were in deep geological time. That's what makes the finding genuinely important—not whether Britain had a "supervolcano," but what the evidence reveals about ancient plate tectonics and volcanic activity.

## STORY 7: When Stress Breaks Your Sex Drive—What Science Actually Says

**Word count: ~530 | External links: 3 | Internal links: 1 | Total: 4**
**Ratio: ~1 link per 133 words ✓**

Chronic stress affects sexual desire, but not because your "adrenal glands are exhausted." That popular explanation is medically incorrect. Understanding what actually happens requires separating three different concepts that sound related but operate differently.

First concept: stress itself. When you experience stress—whether from work, relationships, or finances—your body activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, or HPA axis. This is a real physiological system. The hypothalamus releases a hormone called CRH. That signals the pituitary gland to release ACTH. That hormone reaches the adrenal glands, which release cortisol. This cascade prepares your body for fight-or-flight response. It's designed to be temporary.

Second concept: chronic stress. When stress continues without relief, HPA axis activity can shift from acute to sustained patterns. Cortisol production can remain disproportionately elevated over time. Your sympathetic nervous system—the accelerator that speeds up heart rate and directs blood to muscles—stays in overdrive. Meanwhile, your parasympathetic nervous system—the system that enables sexual arousal through blood vessel relaxation and genital blood flow—is reduced in its relative activity. This is one pathway through which stress can affect sexual desire.

Here's where the "adrenal fatigue" myth fails: a systematic review examining decades of research found no evidence that adrenal glands become genuinely exhausted or dysfunctional from stress. The [Endocrine Society states there is no scientific proof supporting adrenal fatigue](https://www.endocrine.org/patient-engagement/endocrine-library/adrenal-fatigue#:~:text=adrenal%20fatigue) as a medical condition. The glands continue producing cortisol normally. They don't burn out or fail.

Third concept: DHEA and stress hormones. Under sustained ACTH signaling, the adrenal glands may produce proportionally more cortisol, and researchers have noted that this is associated with reduced production of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) from the adrenal zona reticularis. This is sometimes described informally as the "pregnenolone steal"—a colloquial model suggesting that cortisol synthesis competes with androgen production. The actual physiological reality, according to current endocrine research, is that sustained ACTH preferentially upregulates cortisol-synthesizing enzymes while simultaneously reducing the activity of an enzyme called 17,20-lyase, which is involved in DHEA production. It's not a physical substrate theft—it's enzymatic diversion. The result is lower adrenal DHEA output. It's important to note that most testosterone in adults is produced by the gonads, not the adrenal glands, though adrenal DHEA can contribute to the overall androgen pool.

Recent research confirms the connection between stress and sexual function. A [2026 review examined how both acute and chronic stress can affect testosterone and sexual function](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42477174/), including sexual desire and erectile function. A [2025 daily-life study found associations between higher subjective stress and lower concurrent sexual desire and arousal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40907147/), though the authors explicitly cautioned that generalizability was limited given the study population.

But—and this matters—low libido has many causes. It shouldn't automatically get attributed to stress or hormones. Depression affects sexual desire. Relationship problems affect it. Medical conditions affect it. Medications affect it. Hormonal changes at different life stages affect it. Stress is one factor among many.

The takeaway: chronic stress can affect [sexual desire and function](https://www.karmactive.com/lifestyle-medication-combined-approach-erectile-dysfunction-treatment/) through interacting psychological, autonomic, and endocrine pathways. The evidence does not support the idea that the adrenal glands simply become exhausted. Understanding the actual pathways matters because it changes how you address the problem—often requiring stress reduction, lifestyle adjustments, and in some cases, a conversation with a healthcare provider.

## STORY 8: China's Move Away From Windows—A Longer Technology Story

**Ratio: ~1 link per 100 words ✓**

Chinese government entities have reportedly been instructed to accelerate removal of a customized Windows 10 version, transitioning toward domestic Linux-based operating systems. But this reported directive isn't China suddenly banning Windows. It's one step in a much longer technological independence campaign.

The relevant context goes back years. China's government has long preferred [domestic software](https://www.karmactive.com/deepseek-v4-pro-ai-model-launch-china-us-rivalry/) for sensitive operations. Windows 10, customized for Chinese government use through a joint venture with Microsoft called C&M Information Technologies, was designed to address security concerns while keeping the internationally recognized operating system. But increasingly, Beijing has pushed agencies and state-owned enterprises toward domestic alternatives.

The primary replacement systems are [Kylin OS](https://www.kylinos.cn/), developed by China Electronics Corporation, and UOS, developed by UnionTech and derived in part from the Deepin Linux ecosystem. Both are based on Linux kernels, modified for Chinese government requirements. They run on domestic processors including Loongson (using LoongArch instruction sets), Phytium (ARM-based), and Zhaoxin (x86 architecture).

This strategy, called Xinchuang or "Information Technology Application Innovation," is a policy framework that directs government agencies and state-owned enterprises toward domestic IT infrastructure. It's not new policy—it's been developing for years—but the September 2026 reported acceleration of Windows removal suggests the timeline for some state-linked organizations is compressing.

The actual difficulty isn't switching operating systems. It's everything connected to them. Enterprise environments rely on thousands of peripheral devices, each with specific drivers. Document scanners, printers, card readers, security systems—all need compatible drivers. Legacy administrative utilities written for Windows need translation or replacement. That's the real bottleneck slowing migration, not the operating system itself.

Similar concerns exist globally. The European Union has discussed reducing dependence on U.S. technology. India has promoted domestic software development. China's approach is state-directed through [formal policy frameworks](https://www.karmactive.com/chinas-1200-gw-renewable-surge-357-gw-added-in-2024-emissions-still-rising/) rather than market-driven adoption, as seen in sectors ranging from energy to computing.

The reported September 2026 acceleration matters because it signals the retirement timeline for government-customized Windows in certain agencies is real, not theoretical. Government entities that haven't already migrated to domestic systems are being pushed to move faster.

This doesn't mean Windows has disappeared from China. Private companies still use Windows widely. But for sensitive government operations, the transition to domestic systems under the [Xinchuang framework](https://www.openeuler.org/) is an active, ongoing process.

## STORY 9: How Artists Are Poisoning AI—The Technical Reality Behind Nightshade

**Word count: ~510 | External links: 3 | Internal links: 1 | Total: 4**
**Ratio: ~1 link per 128 words ✓**

[Artists](https://www.karmactive.com/web-stories/artists-vs-ai-the-courtroom-battle-defining-the-future-of-art-copyright/) concerned about AI companies scraping their work have adopted two different protective tools: Glaze and Nightshade. They're often confused as similar, but they operate through completely different mechanisms, and both have specific limitations people don't understand.

Glaze works defensively. It applies subtle pixel-level perturbations—mathematical adjustments imperceptible to human eyes—that alter how AI visual encoders read the image. When an AI system fine-tunes on "glazed" images, it fails to learn the artist's actual style. Instead, the model learns a scrambled representation. The human eye sees an oil painting. The AI's learned representation associates it with something entirely different. For an artist trying to prevent style theft, [Glaze](https://glaze.cs.uchicago.edu/aboutus.html) disrupts the model's ability to imitate that specific aesthetic.

Nightshade operates as a data-poisoning tool. It introduces targeted semantic disruption. The tool alters pixel values through visually inconspicuous mathematical perturbations—changes designed to be invisible to the human eye but detectable to AI feature extraction systems. While humans still clearly see a handbag in the image, the AI system extracts feature representations matching a completely different object—say, a toaster. This matters during model training. When poisoned images are included in training, the model's learned association between a text prompt and the corresponding visual concept gets corrupted for that specific concept.

Here's what the actual research found: Nightshade was designed to exploit [concept sparsity](https://people.cs.uchicago.edu/~ravenben/publications/abstracts/nightshade-oakland24.html). Specific concepts in training data—say, "handbag"—may only have thousands of associated images. Because of this sparsity, the University of Chicago researchers found that fewer than 100 optimized poisoned samples could affect targeted concepts in their experiments with image-generation models like SDXL. This is a fundamentally different mechanism than requiring a significant fraction of the overall training dataset to be poisoned. It works by corrupting the training signal for a specific concept, not by flooding the entire training process.

Here's the critical limitation: Nightshade was designed for text-to-image diffusion models, not as a demonstrated method for altering language models like ChatGPT. Language models process text differently than image-generation models process visual training data.

Both tools operate on the assumption that scrapers include the altered images in training data. Some AI companies employ filtering and denoising techniques—such as Gaussian blurring or autoencoder denoising—that can degrade the effectiveness of perturbations. Researchers at [USENIX Security 2025 published findings on a detection method called LightShed](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/foerster), which reported a 99.98% true-positive detection rate for Nightshade perturbations in their tested settings. This doesn't render Nightshade ineffective in all scenarios, but it demonstrates that defenses are actively being developed alongside the tools.

The University of Chicago project reports more than 8.5 million Glaze downloads and more than 2.5 million Nightshade downloads, figures self-reported by the project.

The real achievement isn't creating an impenetrable defense. It's raising the technical cost and complexity of using artist data without consent—forcing any party wanting to train on that data to invest more heavily in filtering or risk corrupted concept representations in their models.

## STORY 10: Rising Seas Threaten Mumbai and Kolkata—Here's What That Actually Means

**Word count: ~520 | External links: 2 | Internal links: 4 | Total: 6**
**Ratio: ~1 link per 87 words ✓**

A [United Nations assessment](https://india.un.org/en/322054-rising-sea-levels-threaten-hundreds-millions-worldwide-including-india#:~:text=770%20million) identified approximately 770 million people—roughly one in ten globally—living in coastal areas less than five meters above high-tide lines. The report specifically highlighted [Mumbai](https://www.karmactive.com/cities-like-los-angeles-and-mumbai-are-in-peril-one-billion-people-at-risk-unprecedented-sea-level-rise-threatens-major-coastal-cities-worldwide/), Kolkata, and Dhaka as vulnerable low-lying South Asian cities facing accelerating risk from rising seas.

Here's what matters: sea-level rise isn't just "more water entering the ocean." Multiple factors combine to create the threat.

Global sea levels rose 5.9 millimeters in 2024, the highest annual increase recorded in satellite observation history. Between 2014 and 2023, the average rise was about 4.7 millimeters per year. These aren't trivial amounts when compounded across decades.

Two primary mechanisms drive this rise. Warming seawater physically expands, taking up more space. This thermal expansion accounts for a substantial portion of observed rise. Melting glaciers and ice sheets from Greenland and Antarctica contribute freshwater volume to the ocean. The UN identifies both warming waters and melting glaciers and ice sheets as the primary drivers of this acceleration.

But here's where it gets complicated for specific cities. Sea-level rise isn't uniform globally. The bigger challenge for Mumbai and [Kolkata](https://www.karmactive.com/south-bengal-red-alert-deep-depression-nears-kolkata/) is compound risk: sea-level rise plus land subsidence. In the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta where Kolkata sits, the land itself is sinking due to the weight of sediment and natural compaction processes in deltaic regions, compounded by groundwater extraction. From the perspective of someone living there, the sea isn't just rising. The ground is also dropping. These two forces combine to increase the relative sea-level change experienced locally.

Add tropical cyclone storm surges. The Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea generate intense cyclones that push large water masses toward shore. When storm surge combines with already-rising sea levels and subsiding land, inundation risk becomes more severe.

The numbers from the UN report are striking. More than [14 million people in Dhaka, Mumbai, and Kolkata risk losing their homes, livelihoods, and lives](https://india.un.org/en/321915-rising-seas-rising-stakes-what-increasing-sea-levels-mean-humanity). That's not "everyone in these cities." It's the populations in the lowest-lying areas most vulnerable to permanent or regular flooding. The UN says coastal infrastructure damage already exceeds $1.8 trillion—reflecting the scale of losses already occurring along global coastlines exposed to rising seas.

The question "Will Mumbai be underwater?" requires careful answering. The UN report doesn't predict Mumbai suddenly vanishes beneath waves. It identifies Mumbai as a low-lying coastal city facing increasing flooding risk, displacement potential, and infrastructure vulnerability. That's different from permanent inundation.

Seasonal [high-tide flooding](https://www.karmactive.com/high-tide-warning-for-mumbai-red-and-orange-alerts-issued-across-maharashtra/) may increase over time. Monsoon-driven rainfall during high-tide conditions could place greater stress on drainage systems as sea levels continue rising. Storm surge from cyclones may penetrate farther inland as baseline water levels increase. [Saltwater intrusion](https://www.karmactive.com/nasa-dod-study-77-of-coastal-watersheds-to-face-saltwater-intrusion-by-2100/) can affect freshwater aquifers and agricultural productivity in low-lying coastal areas.

India's coastal economic centers—where ports, industries, and dense urban populations concentrate—sit directly in the vulnerable zone. The risk isn't dramatic overnight change. It's gradual, worsening pressure on infrastructure and livelihoods that builds over the coming decades.
