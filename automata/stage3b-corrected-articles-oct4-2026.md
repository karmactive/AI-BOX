The Bureau of Meteorology confirmed the tornado touched down in Ulladulla, generating a wind-gust front of approximately 98 km/h. The SES logged 34 damage reports across the town. Fifteen properties were affected, at least six severely, and two homes were left uninhabitable. SES crews were active for more than 24 hours following the event. No injuries were reported.

Despite the extent of property damage, BOM classified the tornado as sub-severe — a lower formal classification than its visible impact might suggest.

If your home or business was in the storm path, photograph the damage first, contact your insurer promptly and check with the insurer before authorising any repair or debris-removal work. **Does Australian home insurance cover tornado damage?**

Many comprehensive Australian home and building insurance policies cover tornado damage under storm and tempest provisions, but coverage depends on the individual policy wording, exclusions and limits. Photograph all damage — debris, unroofed sections, water ingress — immediately and before emergency clean-up crews arrive to tarp or clear the property.

The SES is continuing property assessments across the affected area. Further damage inspection tallies from Shoalhaven City Council are expected in coming days. If you have unassessed damage, register with the NSW SES at 132 500. Check back as official damage figures are updated.

The dispute centres on the Sunshine Protection Act, which would make daylight saving time permanent across the United States and end the twice-yearly clock change. Trump has publicly backed the legislation. The White House issued an administration policy statement supporting H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act of 2025, in July 2026. The Sunshine Protection Act has cleared the House while Senate resistance has mounted. The measure would still need Senate passage and presidential approval before it could become law.

For the millions of Americans who find the clock change disruptive, this public escalation makes federal legislation to end it less likely to move quickly, not more. The November clock change is expected to proceed on schedule.

**The Battle Behind the Clock Change**

Cotton's opposition has been shaped in part by sustained lobbying from medical groups including the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, which supports permanent standard time rather than permanent daylight saving time. The medical argument centres on winter morning darkness: keeping clocks on daylight saving time year-round produces darker winter mornings, which the medical lobby argues creates health and road safety risks for school-age children.

The result is that the Trump Tom Cotton daylight saving dispute has landed on a bill that is supported by the White House and already through one chamber, but blocked by a cluster of health and safety objections that political pressure has not resolved. Information on [federal clock change legislation](https://www.karmactive.com/tag/policy/) and the ongoing Senate debate is available for readers following the issue.

**Will daylight saving time become permanent in the US?**

No. Permanent daylight saving time is not taking effect in the near term. The Sunshine Protection Act has not passed the Senate, and Cotton and other senators remain opposed. Americans will change their clocks in November under the existing schedule. The measure would still need Senate passage and presidential approval before it could become law.

Cotton has not publicly reversed his position following the post. Check back as the Senate's response to the escalation develops and as the November clock change approaches.

A magnitude 4.8 earthquake struck near Leongatha in Victoria's Gippsland region at 2:01am on October 3, waking residents across Melbourne and the surrounding region. Geoscience Australia logged close to 21,000 felt reports — a high count for an event that produced no major structural damage or injuries at the time of the latest agency updates.

The Victoria earthquake was centred near the town of Arawata, close to Korumburra and Leongatha, at a depth of approximately 10 kilometres. Two aftershocks followed: a magnitude 3.1 at 2:56am and a magnitude 2.5 at 12:25pm on the same day. No significant structural damage had been reported and no injuries were recorded at the time of the latest updates.

After any earthquake, smaller aftershocks are possible in the days and weeks that follow. Geoscience Australia has already recorded two. Further minor aftershocks remain possible but are not certain. Geoscience Australia described the event as a shallow, moderate earthquake.

If you felt the earthquake and are uncertain whether your property sustained damage, check for visible cracks in walls, ceilings and foundations — particularly at corners and around door and window frames — and inspect chimneys, roof tiles and external brickwork. If anything concerns you, contact a licensed structural assessor before assuming the damage is cosmetic. Older brick and masonry structures can be more vulnerable to minor seismic movement than modern timber or steel-framed construction.

A shallow depth of approximately 10 kilometres can contribute to stronger shaking near the surface. This helps explain why the Melbourne earthquake registered close to 21,000 felt reports despite causing limited physical damage — the shaking was widespread without being severe enough to compromise engineered structures.

The Gippsland region sits within one of south-eastern Australia's more seismically active zones. Victoria experienced a magnitude 5.9 earthquake near Mansfield in September 2021. Because earthquake energy rises exponentially with magnitude, that event released roughly 45 times more energy than Saturday's magnitude 4.8 earthquake. The 2021 event caused damage to some older structures in regional towns.

Australia sits on one of the world's more stable continental plates, but intraplate earthquakes occur regularly in the south-east. Geoscience Australia tracks and publishes real-time seismic data nationally.

Geoscience Australia is continuing to monitor the region for aftershocks. If you experience further shaking in the Gippsland area, you can submit a felt report through the Geoscience Australia website. Updates will follow if significant aftershocks are recorded.

Queensland Police seized 558 e-bikes, e-scooters and other electric mobility devices and issued more than 2,400 infringement notices in the first three months of a statewide enforcement operation. If you ride any kind of e-mobility device in Queensland, the rules generating the bulk of those fines may not be the ones you are expecting.

Operation Yankee Surety began on July 1, 2026, as Queensland Police's dedicated e-mobility enforcement program and is continuing through June 30, 2027. The three-month results released to date: 558 device seizures, more than 2,400 infringement notices, approximately 1,500 of which related to helmet non-compliance, 157 speeding offences and 68 drink-riding prosecutions. The Queensland Government granted police seizure powers for illegal and non-compliant devices from July 1, 2026.

Helmet non-compliance generated more than 60 per cent of all fines. Of the 2,400-plus notices issued, roughly 1,500 were for helmet violations. Queensland law requires an approved helmet for all e-bike and e-scooter riders regardless of rider age, device type or riding location.

If you ride an e-bike or e-scooter in Queensland, understanding what gets a device seized matters now. Queensland Police can seize e-mobility devices they reasonably suspect do not meet the legal requirements for use in public places. A seized device is entered into the QPS seized and forfeited e-mobility device list. Owners have 30 days to apply for release, must provide proof of ownership and supporting information, and release is subject to eligibility requirements. If the device was illegal, it may not be releasable.

**What Queensland's E-Mobility Rules Require**

The speed limits are specific. Compliant e-bikes and e-scooters are capped at 25 km/h on roads and shared paths. When passing pedestrians on footpaths or shared paths, the limit drops to 12 km/h. Devices modified to exceed these limits, or that do not meet Australian design rules for e-mobility devices, are classified as non-compliant and are subject to seizure.

Motor output matters too. An e-bike with a maximum continuous rated motor output above 250 watts does not meet Queensland's legal e-bike requirements. Some higher-powered electric vehicles are instead classified as motorcycles under Queensland law and can require registration, insurance and a licence to ride. Licensing requirements depend on the device type and rider's age under Queensland's updated rules — check the Queensland Government website for current requirements.

Queensland Police maintain an official QPS seized and forfeited e-mobility device list where seized devices can be checked. You can find further coverage of [urban transport policy and safety regulations](https://www.karmactive.com/category/public-transportation/) for context across Australian states.

The enforcement pattern emerging from Operation Yankee Surety suggests that police are applying the full suite of powers introduced in July 2026 — enforcing helmet use, speed limits and drink-riding rules across the board. The 68 drink-riding prosecutions in three months indicate that alcohol testing is being actively applied to e-mobility riders, not just bicycle and motor vehicle drivers.

Operation Yankee Surety is continuing through June 30, 2027. Riders should check their device against Queensland Government specifications and confirm helmet compliance before their next ride.
