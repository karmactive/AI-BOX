# US Iran War Cost Reaches About $38 Billion as CBO Details Military Spending

---

## ARTICLE

The United States has spent roughly $38 billion fighting Iran since late February. That number is significant — but it understates the full military cost, and the Congressional Budget Office says it did not receive complete information from the Defense Department to give a final figure.

### What CBO Found

The Congressional Budget Office released its Iran conflict cost assessment in mid-September 2026. Direct Department of Defense spending from February 28 through August 1 reached approximately $38 billion. That figure covers munitions expenditure, equipment losses, aircraft and base damage, additional flying hours, and fuel costs. CBO estimates that spending could increase by another $2 to $3 billion per month while the conflict continues, depending on the intensity of fighting.

For US taxpayers tracking the Iran war cost, the $38 billion figure is the most authoritative public number available. It is not the total cost of the war.

### What the $38 Billion Does Not Include

The roughly $38 billion figure is not a final price tag for the Iran conflict. CBO's estimate covers direct Department of Defense costs through August 1 and includes munitions, equipment losses, additional flying hours and fuel. It does not capture every future cost, including some reconstruction costs and longer-term effects. The monthly spending estimate also changes depending on the intensity of fighting.

### Why the Cost Figures Look Different

Three separate numbers have emerged from the Iran conflict, and they are not the same figure covering the same period.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth put direct costs at approximately $37.5 billion in a July estimate — a different reporting date and a different methodology. The CBO assessment covers February 28 through August 1 at approximately $38 billion. Earlier Pentagon watchdog figures cover shorter windows from the conflict's opening phase.

These numbers are not contradictory. They reflect different dates and different methodologies. A reader who encounters all three and assumes they describe the same costs will get the wrong picture. The correct read is that they are snapshots from different points in the same conflict.

### The CBO's Own Caveat

The CBO report carries an unusual admission: the Defense Department did not provide all the information CBO requested for its assessment. That gap means the $38 billion is the best available public estimate — not a verified and complete accounting of wartime spending. It is reasonable to assume the actual cost is higher than what CBO was able to confirm.

### The Weapons Problem — Separate From the Dollar Cost

Beyond direct spending, CBO estimates that between half and two-thirds of certain US missile-defense interceptor inventories have been consumed since the conflict began in June 2025. Replenishing those stocks, CBO says, could take at least five years.

This is a separate problem from the dollar cost of the war. Missile interceptors are not restocked on a short timeline. The United States could formally conclude the conflict tomorrow and still face a multi-year window during which its missile-defense capacity would be reduced. For anyone tracking US Iran war military losses, this is the number that has longer-term consequences.

Earlier reporting by Karmactive covered the Pentagon watchdog's findings on damaged aircraft and bases from the conflict's opening phase. The CBO's September assessment updates and expands that picture.

### What $2–3 Billion Per Month Actually Means

CBO's monthly cost projection is a range rather than a fixed number. Lower-intensity periods cost less. Escalation costs more. The $2–3 billion figure is a planning estimate, not a monthly invoice. It also excludes base reconstruction and longer-term personnel and healthcare costs that typically follow any military engagement.

### Inflation and Broader Effects

Conflict spending at this scale affects defense procurement costs, energy markets and broader US inflation. The specific inflation effects of the Iran war remain difficult to separate from other economic pressures. But munitions expenditure at this pace — and the procurement cost of replacing depleted stocks — adds to defense industry demand in ways that ripple through defense contractor supply chains for years.

The CBO noted that its assessment was constrained because the Defense Department did not supply all requested information. That is an unusual position for a congressional oversight body — saying publicly that the executive branch limited what it could measure.

## CLOSURE

The CBO's September 2026 assessment puts direct US military spending on the Iran conflict at approximately $38 billion through August 1, with an ongoing monthly cost projected at $2–3 billion. Depleted missile interceptor stocks represent a separate readiness concern with at least a five-year replenishment timeline. CBO's estimate does not include all reconstruction costs and was constrained by incomplete information from the Defense Department. Further cost reports from the Pentagon watchdog and CBO are expected as the conflict continues.
