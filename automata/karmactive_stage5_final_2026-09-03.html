UPCOS is not regularization. It is not converting outsourced workers into permanent employees. What it is: a centralized system designed to standardize wages, ensure statutory compliance, and reduce payment delays that have plagued outsourced employment in the state.

The wage structure under UPCOS is ₹16,000 to ₹20,000 per month, with classifications into skilled and semi-skilled categories. The workable month is set at 26 billable days, and employment arrangements run on three-year term contracts. Salary is distributed electronically directly to worker bank accounts, with remuneration required to reach workers by the fifth of each month. This schedule differs from the erratic disbursement patterns that prompted the creation of UPCOS—workers in some departments had experienced months-long delays.

Statutory compliance is now tracked centrally. UPCOS mandates coverage under the Employees' Provident Fund (EPF), Employees' State Insurance (ESI), and accidental insurance. Previously, outsourced workers often fell through administrative gaps where these benefits were not deducted or submitted correctly. Centralization aims to close those gaps.

Additional benefits promised include medical facilities, maternity leave, accident insurance, pension, and family pension. The government also instituted a ₹15,000 ex-gratia payment for funeral assistance if a worker dies while employed.

Why create this new corporation? The government documented persistent complaints: arbitrary deductions from salaries, late payments stretching months, lack of EPF and ESI compliance, inadequate benefits, inconsistent commission structures, and violations of reservation policies. Some of these issues stemmed from fragmented hiring across departments, each with different contractor arrangements.

The UPCOS portal and website launched alongside the corporation, creating a digital front-end for workers to track grievances, view salary payments, and access information about benefits. This transparency mechanism is intended to prevent the kind of hidden deductions and undisclosed payment delays that characterized outsourced work previously.

The critical test is implementation. Creating a corporation and announcing policies is the easy part. Workers should track whether salary arrives on schedule, whether statutory deductions are correct and tracked, and whether the transparency mechanism actually functions.

UPCOS represents a structural reform. It is not a wage increase or a path to permanent employment, but it is an attempt to bring order to a system that has historically been chaotic and unpredictable for outsourced workers.

Gloria Steinem, Feminist Pioneer Who Reshaped American Politics, Dies at 92

Gloria Steinem died on September 2, 2026, in New York City at age 92. The Gloria Steinem Foundation announced that she died peacefully at home. No cause of death was disclosed.

She was consistently at the center of movements that pushed women's rights from the margins of public conversation into legislation, journalism, and political organizing. She did not accomplish this alone, but few figures in the modern American women's movement were as publicly visible across as many decades.

Steinem was among the founders of Ms., whose preview issue appeared in 1971 and first standalone issue in January 1972. Ms. became the first national magazine in the United States to be run and controlled entirely by women. The magazine published journalism on reproductive rights, workplace discrimination, domestic violence, and equal pay—topics treated as private or peripheral by mainstream media. Ms. gave those topics national prominence and a feminist editorial voice.

In 1971, Steinem co-founded the National Women's Political Caucus alongside Shirley Chisholm, Bella Abzug, Betty Friedan, Fannie Lou Hamer, and others. The organization's specific purpose was training and supporting women candidates to run for public office. The caucus was an explicit attempt to change through political power the composition of elected offices women held.

One of her lesser-known early experiences shaped her approach. In the late 1950s, as a fellow of the Chester Bowles fellowship, Steinem spent time in India, where she was influenced by Gandhian grassroots organizing methods. That experience—working at the community level with people building coalitions for social change—influenced her methodology throughout her career. She remained committed to grassroots coalition-building rather than top-down mandate.

Her influence on specific legislation was substantial. She was a prominent advocate for the Equal Rights Amendment, the constitutional amendment that would have guaranteed gender equality under law. Though the ERA ultimately failed to achieve ratification, Steinem's activism elevated its profile and legitimacy. She was also a prominent advocate for the Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974, which prohibited discrimination in credit based on sex or marital status.

Her coalition work extended beyond white feminist spaces. She organized alongside Dorothy Pitman Hughes and Flo Kennedy, bringing intersectional perspectives to feminist activism.

In 2013, President Barack Obama awarded Steinem the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the nation's highest civilian honor.

On August 27, Ms. republished a 1987 piece by Steinem as part of its coverage. The Gloria Steinem Foundation, which she established, has preserved her archives and maintains her home as a site for feminist organizing and the "Talking Circles" she pioneered—intimate gatherings where people discuss their experiences and build solidarity. Since 2022, the foundation has hosted more than 50 of these circles.

Netherlands Moves 86 Tonnes of Gold to London — Why Central Banks Are Shifting Reserves

The Dutch central bank (De Nederlandsche Bank, or DNB) has transferred 86 metric tonnes of gold from vaults in the United States and Canada to the Bank of England in London between March and August 2026. This is not a headline-grabbing repatriation. It is a quiet reconfiguration of where a portion of the Netherlands' gold reserves sits—and it reveals how central banks are rethinking crisis preparedness.

Previously, DNB held approximately 313 tonnes of gold combined in the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and the Bank of Canada in Ottawa. The transfer of 86 tonnes leaves substantial holdings in both locations but redistributes a meaningful chunk to London. DNB now holds approximately 31 percent of its gold domestically at the Zeist Cash Operations Centre and approximately 31 percent at the Bank of England in London, with the remainder distributed across other locations.

Why move gold across the Atlantic at all? The reasons are practical. London is home to the London Bullion Market Association (LBMA), a major global centre for physical gold trading and settlement. DNB says gold held in London is more readily tradable during a crisis and can be accessed without the logistics delays that cross-oceanic transport entails.

The Netherlands is also diversifying geographic risk. Splitting holdings across New York, London, and domestic vaults reduces concentration in any single jurisdiction. DNB's stated reasons are improved crisis preparedness, better tradability and liquidity through the LBMA, geographic diversification, and easier availability during emergencies.

DNB's move occurs in a context of broader central-bank behavior. Global central banks purchased approximately 850 tonnes of gold in 2025, down from more than 1,000 tonnes annually during 2022–24. Central banks are maintaining interest in gold as a reserve asset—not abandoning it—and they are recalculating where and how to hold it.

A common narrative frames this as "de-dollarization"—countries rejecting the U.S. dollar as global reserve currency. The data tells a murkier story. As of June 2026, the U.S. dollar still comprised approximately 57 percent of global official foreign-exchange reserves. That figure has declined from its historical 70+ percent, but the dollar remains the world's largest official reserve currency. Moving gold between London and New York does not change that.
