## 3. PERM Green Card Suspension

**Headline:** Tech Visa and PERM Green Card Freeze: Sound Economic Policy or Pre-Election Political Theatrics?

The Labor Department moved to halt green card filings for two tech firms weeks before a national election. Whether the action protects domestic workers or positions the administration for a news cycle is a question with economic consequences that run past November.

The US Department of Labor has suspended permanent labor certification filings for Cognizant and Cloudera. PERM is the first step in most employer-sponsored green cards and a required gateway to an employment-based immigrant visa. The Inspector General confirmed the Cognizant suspension in September 2026 and followed with the Cloudera announcement shortly after. H-1B processing for both firms is also suspended. The investigation was run in coordination with the White House Fraud Task Force. Adobe's 215 pending PERM cases show as on hold in the PERM Tracker snapshot dated 25 September 2026, though the Department has not published a formal announcement for Adobe.

Employees at those three firms who have I-140 petitions already approved are in a different category from those still at the PERM stage. The freeze affects labor certification, not approved petitions—but consult an immigration attorney before making any employment decision based on that distinction, because the specifics of each case determine the impact.

**Who the Freeze Actually Protects—and Who It Doesn't**

The framing favored by the administration is that suspending foreign labor certifications opens roles for domestic workers. The Department of Labor's PERM process already requires employers to run documented domestic recruiting efforts before a foreign worker can be certified. Companies must advertise the role, review applicants, and demonstrate that no qualified US worker was available and rejected. Suspending the final certification step does not add new positions; it removes the endpoint of a process that companies passed through to reach this stage.

The more visible economic effect runs in the opposite direction. Firms that rely on specialized visa pathways for high-skill technical workers do not carry vacancies indefinitely. When the US certification path is closed, multinational employers with development operations in multiple countries accelerate transfers of roles and teams to Vancouver, London, and Bangalore—cities with functioning skilled-worker visa pipelines and lower base costs. The jobs lost in that scenario are domestic in location but not in outcome.

The 180-day suspension Cloudera received in May 2026 preceded the September action. That sequence suggests a sustained enforcement posture, not a one-time intervention. Whether the investigation produces findings that justify the scope of the suspension, or whether the timing reflects election-calendar pressures, will be clearer once the Inspector General releases the underlying complaint.

**Why would a company commit PERM fraud?**
PERM fraud most often involves filing labor certifications for positions that were never genuinely open to domestic applicants—creating vacancies on paper to fulfill the regulatory requirement while routing the position to a predetermined foreign hire. It can also involve backdating or falsifying recruiting records.

**What happens to a worker's green card case if their employer's PERM is suspended?**
If your PERM application is pending and your employer's filings are suspended, the case is paused. If your I-140 is already approved, the suspension typically does not revoke that approval—but consult an immigration attorney because case specifics vary.

**Can workers switch employers if their green card case is frozen?**
Workers in certain visa categories can port an approved I-140 to a new employer under AC21 portability rules if the case has been pending for more than 180 days. An attorney can advise whether your case qualifies.

Check your case status through USCIS's online portal and talk to an immigration attorney before taking any action. Do not rely on employer communications alone when a suspension is in effect.

## 4. Iran War Aircraft Losses

**Headline:** 42 US Aircraft Lost in the Iran War: Who Pays the Replacement Bill and Is It Justified?

Congress will be asked to replace what the Iran campaign destroyed. The question is whether the authorization will come with a serious accounting of what the losses cost and what they bought.

A Congressional Research Service report from May 2026 counted at least 42 US aircraft lost or damaged during Operation Epic Fury. About 25 of the 42 were drones, including 24 MQ-9 Reapers and one MQ-4C Triton. Crewed losses included four F-15E Strike Eagles, seven KC-135 tankers, two MC-130J aircraft, an A-10, an E-3, an F-35A, and an HH-60W helicopter. One KC-135 went down over western Iraq on 12 March, killing all six crew members. The Congressional Budget Office estimated total war costs at about $38.1 billion as of 1 August 2026.

The count of 42 may be conservative. The CRS report noted that some losses were still being classified or attributed at the time of publication. Congress has not yet voted on a replacement supplemental.

**Who Pays and How the Debt Gets Structured**

Aircraft replacement does not come from existing Pentagon budget lines. Replacing combat losses of this scale requires an emergency defense supplemental appropriation—a separate vote in Congress that adds to the federal deficit and is financed through Treasury debt issuance. The interest on that debt falls to taxpayers across the life of the bond, typically 10 to 30 years.

The per-unit cost of what was lost varies widely. An MQ-9 Reaper runs approximately $30 million. An F-35A costs more than $80 million. KC-135 tankers are legacy airframes; their replacement value in 2026 dollars, accounting for the KC-46A Pegasus as the modern equivalent, is roughly $150 million per aircraft. Seven tanker losses alone, on replacement-cost terms, exceed $1 billion before the cost of training crews or integrating systems.

**The Strategic Accounting Question**

The losses concentrated in drones and electronic warfare platforms point to a tactical lesson that the supplemental bill does not automatically answer: Iranian missile and drone swarms exacted the highest per-sortie attrition on the assets the US Air Force fields most numerously. Replacing those assets with identical models restores headcount but does not change the vulnerability that produced the losses. Defense analysts advocating for a shift toward autonomous long-range systems argue that funding the same airframe inventory is a strategic decision, not just a procurement one.

Domestically, the supplemental vote will compete against expiring domestic program funding and debt ceiling pressure. Congress has authorized emergency war funding without detailed strategic review before; this is not the first time the question will be asked and not the first time it may go unanswered in the authorization vote itself.

**Where does the money for aircraft replacements come from?**
Combat losses are funded through emergency supplemental appropriations, which are separate from the baseline Pentagon budget and financed by federal borrowing.

**How many of the 42 aircraft were drones vs. crewed?**
About 25 of the 42 were unmanned, including 24 MQ-9 Reapers and one MQ-4C Triton. The remaining losses were crewed aircraft including fighters, tankers, special operations transports, and one helicopter.

**Has the US replaced aircraft lost in previous wars on a full-cost basis?**
Not consistently. After Iraq and Afghanistan, some legacy platforms were retired rather than replaced. The replacement decision is a mix of strategic priorities and budget availability, not a one-for-one automatic process.

Track the supplemental authorization vote through the Senate and House Armed Services Committees. The CRS report is available through the Federation of American Scientists' congressional document archive.

## 5. Trump Accounts for Children

**Headline:** Will 70 Million Kids Actually Benefit from Trump Accounts, or Is the $1,000 Seed Just Cosmetic?

The White House is calling Trump Accounts a generational investment. What they are, for most children, is a $1,000 deposit that will be worth about $3,380 in 18 years—assuming markets cooperate, rules don't change, and nobody needs to touch it.

Trump Accounts are federal savings vehicles for children under 18. The White House says nearly 70 million children have accounts established for them. A $1,000 federal pilot contribution is available for qualifying children: they must be born between 1 January 2025 and 31 December 2028, hold US citizenship, have a Social Security number, and have an authorized adult make the required election. Children born before 2025 can access a $250 contribution from the Dell Foundation's $6.25 billion pledge, provided their account is among the first 25 million activated in ZIP codes with median household incomes below $150,000. Confirm the exact Dell birth-year eligibility range against Dell or Treasury materials before publishing.

Every dollar in the account is locked until the child turns 18. Withdrawals after that follow standard IRA rules, which carry their own withdrawal limits and tax treatment. This is not a college savings account with a dedicated spending pathway; it is a long-term investment account with a fixed lock-in.

**The Compounding Reality**

At a historical 7% real annual return—the figure frequently cited for US equity market long-run averages—$1,000 grows to approximately $3,380 by the time a child born in 2025 reaches 18. That is a real return in inflation-adjusted terms. In nominal dollars, accounting for projected inflation at the Federal Reserve's 2% target, the $3,380 buys roughly the equivalent of $2,370 in today's purchasing power.

In 2025 dollars, the average published in-state tuition at a four-year public university runs above $11,000 per year. The full federal seed deposit, compounded for 18 years, covers less than one semester of tuition—before room and board. The accounts are useful as a savings foundation, not as a tuition solution.

**The Dell Single-Stock Proposal**

The Dell Foundation's $6.25 billion pledge brought a separate policy question with it. Michael Dell has publicly supported allowing Trump Accounts to hold individual company stocks rather than limiting investment options to diversified index funds or target-date funds. Concentrated single-stock exposure in a minor's long-term account creates a volatility profile that diversified instruments do not carry. A single company can lose 70% of its value in a year; an S&P 500 index fund has not.

No final rule on investment options has been published. If the single-stock option is adopted, parents who choose it for their child's account will be assuming equity concentration risk in an account the child cannot access for 18 years. That is a different risk profile than most custodial savings products currently allow.

**Who qualifies for the $1,000 federal contribution?**
Children born between 1 January 2025 and 31 December 2028 who are US citizens with a Social Security number, provided an authorized adult makes the required election.

**Can parents add money to a Trump Account?**
Yes. The accounts can receive voluntary contributions above the federal and foundation deposits, subject to IRA contribution limits. Check the current annual limit through the IRS or Treasury.

**What happens to the money if the rules change?**
Trump Accounts are a federal program; Congress can amend the rules. The lock-in period, investment options, and contribution limits are all statutory, meaning they can be changed through legislation.

Review the full program terms at Treasury.gov before making an election. If you are weighing investment options, compare diversified fund choices against single-stock exposure before selecting.

## 6. Mortgage Rates

**Headline:** Mortgage Rates Near 3-Year High: Why Elevated Borrowing Costs Could Heal the Housing Market in the Long Run

Seven percent mortgage rates feel like a wall to anyone trying to buy a home right now. Over a longer horizon, they may be the mechanism that breaks the boom-bust cycle that made buying nearly impossible at 3%.

Freddie Mac reported an average 30-year fixed mortgage rate of 7.40% for the week ending 8 October 2026, up from 7.28% the prior week. On a $400,000 loan, the monthly payment at 7.40% is approximately $2,770 in principal and interest, compared to about $2,398 at 6%. The gap is $372 a month, or $4,464 a year—before taxes and insurance.

That monthly number is the right place to start a buying decision. Compare the total payment, not just the rate or the list price.

**The Long-Term Reset Argument**

The decade of sub-4% mortgage rates that followed the 2008 financial crisis produced a structurally unhealthy housing market. Low rates allowed buyers to absorb rising list prices without a proportional increase in monthly payments, which pushed home values past wage growth and created the affordability gap that persists today. Speculative buying—short-hold flipping, investor-dominated entry-level markets—was subsidized by cheap debt.

Rates above 7% do something those low rates could not: they break the math of speculative housing behavior. A buyer who borrows at 7.40% needs genuine long-term appreciation to offset the carrying cost. That narrows the buyer pool to people with multi-year plans, not exit strategies. The resulting demand compression tends to slow listing price growth, and in some segments, push prices down.

The lock-in effect—existing homeowners with 3% mortgages refusing to sell and trade into a 7.40% rate—limits supply in the short term, which keeps prices elevated despite the demand drop. That tension typically resolves over 3 to 5 years as life events (job relocations, estate sales, family changes) force inventory back to market, regardless of rate environment. When both supply and demand normalize at higher rate levels, price-to-income ratios historically compress toward sustainable multiples.

The recovery is not linear or guaranteed, but the mechanism is clear: sustained elevated rates create the conditions for genuine affordability correction, where wage growth gradually catches up with normalized asset values.

**What does the 10-year Treasury yield have to do with mortgage rates?**
30-year fixed mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield closely because both reflect long-duration lending risk. When investors demand higher yields on Treasuries, lenders raise mortgage rates proportionally.

**Is waiting for lower rates the right strategy?**
Waiting for rates to drop assumes they will, and on a timeline that works for your situation. If rates fall to 6%, you can refinance. If prices rise 10% in the meantime, you refinance into a higher balance. Model both scenarios against your buying horizon before deciding.

**How far could home prices fall if rates stay above 7%?**
Price corrections in a high-rate environment depend on supply. Markets with constrained land and high demand (coastal metros) tend to compress values 5–15% from peak in a rate shock before stabilizing. Markets with more buildable land and lower demand can see steeper corrections.

If you plan to buy in the next 12 months, calculate your full monthly payment at the current rate. Use the Freddie Mac weekly PMMS for current benchmarks. If you are a seller, price to the payment, not the list price—buyers are doing that math before they make offers.

## 8. Medicare Open Enrollment

**Headline:** 48% of Medicare Seniors Skip Necessary Care: How Plan Confusion and Hidden Costs Threaten Open Enrollment Decisions

Medicare Advantage plans are advertising lower premiums this fall. What the ads do not show is the out-of-pocket cost structure that caused nearly half of beneficiaries to skip or delay care in the past year.

Medicare open enrollment runs from 15 October to 7 December 2026. An eHealth survey of more than 1,000 Medicare beneficiaries, fielded in September 2026 and released on 8 October 2026, found that 48% skipped or delayed medical care in the past year because of confusion about their coverage. A separate 54% skipped or delayed care or prescriptions because of out-of-pocket costs. The survey margin of error is plus or minus 3%. eHealth is a private insurance marketplace that sells Medicare plans.

You have until 7 December to switch plans. After that date, you are locked in until the next open enrollment.

**The Premium Paradox**

Medicare Advantage plans consistently advertise zero or low monthly premiums, and many deliver on that headline figure. The cost structure that generates those low premiums shifts spending from the monthly premium to point-of-care expenses: higher copays for specialist visits, tiered drug formularies where brand-name medications carry 30–40% coinsurance, and prior authorization requirements that can delay or deny access to services your doctor has ordered.

The 54% who skipped care over out-of-pocket costs were not confused—they made a rational calculation that the cost of the visit or prescription exceeded what they could pay. That calculation is not a coverage gap in the legal sense; the plan covers the service. But the cost-share structure made the covered service practically inaccessible.

Before 7 December, log in to Medicare.gov and run a plan comparison with your actual prescription list and your current doctors. Read your Annual Notice of Change, which your current plan sent in September. It lists every cost and coverage change for 2027. Check your drug's tier in each plan you're comparing—a drug moving from tier 2 to tier 3 can double or triple its annual cost.

The eHealth survey also found that 58% of beneficiaries suspect they have been targeted by Medicare-related scams. Medicare does not make unsolicited outbound calls about plan changes. If someone calls you about your Medicare coverage, hang up and call Medicare directly at 1-800-MEDICARE.

**Is it better to have Medicare Advantage or Original Medicare?**
The right choice depends on your prescription list, your doctors, and your expected care needs. Original Medicare has no annual out-of-pocket cap in Part A and Part B combined; Advantage plans cap your out-of-pocket exposure but restrict your provider network. Run the comparison on your own situation, not on average-case assumptions.

**What is the Annual Notice of Change, and what should I look for?**
It is the letter your current Medicare Advantage or Part D plan sends each September. Check for premium changes, deductible changes, drug tier changes, and any doctors or pharmacies leaving the plan's network.

**What happens if I miss the enrollment deadline?**
Outside of specific qualifying events (moving, losing other coverage, qualifying for Medicaid), you cannot switch Medicare plans until the next open enrollment period in October 2027. Coverage decisions made during this window take effect 1 January 2027.

Log in to Medicare.gov now. Compare plans with your real prescription list before 7 December 2026.

## 9. Richneck Elementary Shooting Verdict

**Headline:** Unsealed Richneck Bodycam: Why Early Childhood Aggression Turns Lethal and What Drives Young Kids to Violence

The civil verdict against Ebony Parker established legal liability for what happened at Richneck Elementary. What the verdict and the evidence presented at trial do not establish is the claim that a six-year-old acted from ideological conviction.

In January 2023, a 6-year-old student at Richneck Elementary School in Newport News, Virginia, shot teacher Abby Zwerner. Police bodycam footage from the aftermath was shown during the civil trial. In November 2025, a Virginia jury awarded Zwerner $10 million. She had sought $40 million. The case was brought against Ebony Parker, the former assistant principal, on a gross negligence theory. The jury found Parker failed to act on warnings that the child had a gun.

**What the Research Shows About Extreme Aggression at Age Six**

Children under eight lack the cognitive architecture for ideological commitment. Developmental neuroscience establishes that the prefrontal cortex—responsible for abstract reasoning, moral planning, and cause-and-effect projection—is in early formation at age six. A child at that age cannot hold a political doctrine, process radicalization content as adults do, or act from organized ideological intent. When a young child engages in severe physical aggression, the clinical explanation is not ideological; it is neurological and environmental.

Extreme aggression in early childhood typically reflects one or more of the following: severe emotional dysregulation from insecure attachment, exposure to domestic conflict and adult hostility, developmental trauma, or neurological conditions that impair impulse regulation. Children in these circumstances do not plan violence as an adult would. They act on overwhelming emotional states in the only way their developmental stage makes available.

The lethal variable is not the psychological state. It is access to a loaded firearm. Impulsive childhood anger produces bruises and broken objects in households without guns. It produces shootings in households where firearms are unsecured. The Richneck case is not evidence of a radicalization pathway in early childhood. It is evidence of what unsecured firearms do when they are within reach of a child in emotional crisis.

**What can schools do when a student shows signs of severe dysregulation?**
Schools can convene threat assessment teams, coordinate with families on behavior intervention plans, and in extreme cases request law enforcement welfare checks. Parker's failure, as the jury found it, was not activating those protocols when warnings existed.

**What are the storage rules for firearms in homes with children?**
Federal law does not mandate safe storage for all households, but Virginia and 27 other states have enacted child access prevention laws. In Virginia, it is unlawful to store a firearm in a way that a child under 14 can access it.

**Is childhood exposure to media violence a radicalization pathway?**
Exposure to violent media correlates with desensitization to aggression in some studies, but it does not produce ideological radicalization in children under eight. The pathway to early childhood violence runs through emotional dysregulation and gun access, not content consumption.

Store firearms locked and unloaded, in a quick-access safe if rapid adult use is important to you. Trigger locks add a second barrier. Ask your school what its protocol is for students with active behavior intervention plans.

## 12. Michael Douglas and Monogamy

**Headline:** Michael Douglas Ended Five Relationships for Monogamy: What Relationship Ultimatums Do to Mental Health

A monogamy ultimatum issued before a marriage is different from a threat used to control a partner during one. Michael Douglas's memoir offers a case study in a boundary that worked—and in why not all ultimatums function the same way.

In his memoir, *One Helluva Ride*, Michael Douglas writes that he was seeing five women when Catherine Zeta-Jones asked him to commit to monogamy. He ended those relationships. He and Zeta-Jones married in 2000. He is 82.

That sequence—a clear expectation, a decision, a commitment—is what clinicians describe as functional boundary-setting rather than coercive ultimatum. The distinction is meaningful and has measurable mental health consequences for both people in the dynamic.

**Constructive Boundaries vs. Emotional Coercion**

A boundary communicates a personal limit clearly: "I will not continue this relationship without monogamy." The person setting it is prepared to follow through; the consequence is stated rather than threatened. It creates information the other person can act on.

A coercive ultimatum operates differently. It is issued not to communicate a genuine limit but to force compliance—"commit or I'll destroy you"—and is frequently made by someone who will not follow through. The psychological effect on the recipient is chronic anxiety about the relationship's stability, hypervigilance to the other person's emotional state, and reduced capacity for autonomous decision-making within the partnership.

Zeta-Jones's request, as Douglas describes it, fits the former pattern. The result was a 25-year marriage, a demonstrably stable commitment by the evidence available. Whether other factors contributed to that stability is not the subject of the memoir's account, but the boundary-setting mechanism itself aligned with clinical models of healthy relationship formation.

**What the Research Shows About Relationship Ultimatums**

Studies on relational ultimatums find that clarity of expectation is the variable that determines outcome, not the firmness of the demand. Ambiguous expectations—"I need more commitment" without definition—produce the anxiety associated with coercive dynamics even when no threat is intended. Clear expectations, paired with genuine willingness to accept the outcome, reduce that anxiety even when the stated limit is firm.

The mental health benefit of resolving multi-partner ambiguity is documented in research on relational stability and depressive symptoms. Sustained uncertainty about a relationship's exclusive status is associated with elevated cortisol levels, sleep disruption, and ruminative cognition. A clear mutual commitment—reached through a stated boundary rather than prolonged ambiguity—tends to reduce those symptoms in both partners.

**What is the difference between a healthy ultimatum and a coercive one?**
A healthy ultimatum is a genuine communication of a personal limit, with real willingness to follow through. A coercive one is used to force compliance through fear, often by someone who does not intend to follow through on the stated consequence.

**Can relationship ultimatums cause psychological harm?**
Coercive ultimatums can. The harm runs through chronic anxiety, reduced autonomy, and the erosion of psychological safety. Ultimatums issued as genuine boundary statements—with clarity and real follow-through—do not carry the same risk profile.

**Is multi-dating before exclusivity a mental health concern?**
Non-exclusive dating is common before a formal commitment conversation. The mental health risk is not in the arrangement itself but in prolonged ambiguity without resolution—for any of the people involved.

If you are navigating a relationship decision involving exclusivity, consider whether the conversation reflects a genuine personal limit or a mechanism for control. A therapist can help distinguish the two in specific cases.
