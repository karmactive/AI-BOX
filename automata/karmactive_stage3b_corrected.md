HomeView supports up to six household members, with personal content appearing only when the right person is recognized and a recently unlocked paired iPhone or iPad is nearby.

Apple processes the device's camera and microphone data in Secure Exclaves isolated from the operating system; however, Siri requests may be handled on-device or through Apple's Private Cloud Compute depending on the task.

HomeView connects to smart home devices through Thread and Matter as a compatible home hub for Apple's Home app.

HomeView does not have an on-device App Store. There is no way to download apps directly to the device. Compatible third-party audio apps—including Spotify and Audible—and iOS video apps that developers extend to HomeView are available through a connected iPhone or iPad. Some features require a compatible iPhone to be nearby and recently unlocked.

Amazon's Echo Show and Google's Nest Hub have owned the countertop display category for years—HomeView enters late but with tighter integration across the Apple device ecosystem. Whether that integration justifies $299 over a countertop iPad depends on whether your household needs hands-free facial personalization.

For hardware comparisons with competing smart displays and [Apple's broader smart home product line](https://karmactive.com/apple-homekit-smart-home-guide), the gap HomeView most clearly closes is multi-user household identity, not raw functionality. ### Social Security 2027 COLA Announcement Tomorrow: What Bisignano Said and What Medicare Will Take Back

Social Security Commissioner Frank Bisignano appeared on CNBC's *Squawk Box* this week and called the forthcoming benefit adjustment "a good day" for seniors, adding that the program would remain solvent "for another 90 years." The official 2027 cost-of-living adjustment posts tomorrow, October 14, 2026, when the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases September inflation data at 8:30 a.m. Eastern—and while the headline number will be the largest increase in three years, the net change hitting most retirees' bank accounts will be smaller than the percentage suggests once Medicare deductions are factored in.

The COLA is set by a fixed statutory formula: the average of July, August, and September CPI-W figures compared to the same three months in the prior year. The Social Security Administration has no discretion over the number—it comes directly from BLS data. Based on figures already published for July and August 2026, independent analysts project the 2027 adjustment will land between 3.4% and 3.6%. The Senior Citizens League forecasts 3.5%, AARP forecasts 3.6%, and the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget estimates 3.4%. For the average retiree receiving roughly $2,079 per month in 2026—per the SSA's fact sheet—a 3.5% increase would add approximately $73 to monthly payments before any deductions. The adjustment applies to more than 70 million Social Security and SSI recipients. The [original October 5 COLA forecast article](https://karmactive.com/social-security-cola-2027-forecast-october) covered the calculation methodology and the prior year's 2.8% baseline.

Retirees, disabled beneficiaries, and fixed-income household budgeters face meaningful payment increases for 2027 following an uptick in inflation data ahead of Wednesday's official announcement. Beneficiaries planning annual household budgets should anticipate a cost-of-living adjustment between 3.4% and 3.6%, translating to approximately $73 extra per month at the projected midpoint before any deductions. Recipients must also account for rising Medicare Part B monthly premiums, which will offset a portion of next year's gross benefit increase.

### The Medicare Deduction Most COLA Headlines Skip

Commissioner Bisignano's 90-year solvency claim is a political message, not a statutory fix. The Social Security Trust Fund's projected depletion date remains in the mid-2030s under current law, unchanged without congressional action to adjust payroll tax rates or benefit structures. His statement reflects the administration's confidence that legislative reform will come; it doesn't alter the underlying funding math.

The more immediate practical issue for beneficiaries calculating their January check is Medicare Part B. The standard monthly Part B premium—which covers outpatient care for Medicare-enrolled Social Security recipients—is expected to increase in 2027. When Part B premiums rise, the amount is automatically deducted from Social Security payments before the beneficiary sees a dollar. The 2026 Social Security Trustees Report projects the standard monthly Part B premium will rise approximately $6.60 per month, from $202.90 to $209.50, though the final 2027 rate has not yet been officially announced by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services.

For someone receiving $2,079 per month, a 3.5% COLA adds roughly $73 gross. If Part B premiums rise by the projected $6.60, the net increase in spendable income would be approximately $66 per month. That is the number that matters for someone budgeting groceries and utilities, not the percentage figure alone.

This year's projected adjustment would be the largest in three years, reflecting a moderate uptick in inflation following a period of cooling. The 2023 COLA was 8.7% at the peak of inflation; the [2026 adjustment came in at 2.8%](https://karmactive.com/social-security-cola-2026-official-announcement). The 2025 rate was 2.5%. The current projected 3.5%–3.6% for 2027 would surpass all three recent years.

Congress has debated switching the COLA benchmark from CPI-W to CPI-E (the Consumer Price Index for the Elderly), which weights healthcare spending more heavily. No legislation has passed to make that switch.

The official 2027 COLA percentage posts tomorrow morning, October 14, 2026, immediately after the 8:30 a.m. BLS release. The SSA will confirm the exact figure on its website the same day. Beneficiaries will receive an individualized notice by mail before January. Check back here Wednesday morning for updated figures once the official announcement is confirmed.

### Supreme Court to Hear DOGE Public Records Case: What It Means for Elon Musk's Internal Documents

The Supreme Court agreed on October 13, 2026, to hear a lawsuit demanding that Elon Musk's Department of Government Efficiency turn over internal records and communications under federal open-records law. The court did not order any documents released. It agreed to answer a narrower legal question that has moved through the lower courts: does DOGE qualify as a federal agency required to comply with the Freedom of Information Act?

The lawsuit was brought by Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, a government transparency organization. CREW filed under FOIA, arguing that DOGE—despite being formally structured as a presidential advisory body—exercised de facto operational control over agency budgets and federal workforce decisions significant enough to make it an agency in practice. The Department of Justice, representing the administration, argued DOGE functions as an advisory panel exempt from the public-records requirements that apply to formal executive agencies. Both the federal district court and the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals sided with CREW on whether DOGE is subject to FOIA. The administration appealed to the Supreme Court, which took up the case on October 13, 2026.

Federal government contractors, agency civil servants, and transparency watchdogs face significant legal ramifications regarding executive oversight as the Supreme Court reviews DOGE's operational status. Open-government litigators should monitor the case timeline and brief schedules, as a ruling that DOGE is subject to FOIA would open the path for public-records requests targeting DOGE's activities—though any documents would be released through the standard FOIA process, including applicable exemptions and potential further litigation over specific materials.

### What the Court Is Actually Deciding—and What It Isn't

The legal question runs to the definition of "agency" under 5 U.S.C. § 552(f), the provision in federal statute that determines which entities must comply with FOIA. The administration's position is that DOGE is a presidential advisory body and therefore owes less disclosure than a formal agency. CREW's position is that DOGE's actual conduct—instructing agencies to reduce headcount, directing budget cuts, and advising contract terminations—went so far beyond an advisory role that labeling it "advisory" provides no FOIA immunity.

A ruling for CREW would mean DOGE becomes subject to FOIA. Documents would be released through the standard request process, subject to exemptions, agency processing timelines, and the scope of individual requests. It does not mean automatic or immediate disclosure of all internal records.

If the court sides with the administration and draws a clear line protecting advisory-status bodies regardless of how much operational influence they wield, future administrations would have a template for structuring powerful advisory bodies outside public-records reach.

The case's implications extend past DOGE. It directly challenges the modern executive practice of bringing in private-sector figures as special advisors who exercise broad influence over agency operations. A ruling that strips the advisory-status shield from any body that acts like an agency would reshape how presidents structure these arrangements going forward.

Oral arguments are expected in early 2027. A decision is anticipated by the end of the court's 2026–2027 term, typically in June 2027. The [prior coverage of DOGE's federal workforce actions](https://karmactive.com/doge-federal-workforce-cuts-agency-reductions) and [a breakdown of FOIA request procedures for federal records](https://karmactive.com/how-to-file-foia-request-federal-agency) provide additional context on what FOIA disclosure would mean in practice.

### What Was the Space Jellyfish in the Sky After Tuesday's SpaceX Launch?

Residents across Florida and the US Southeast woke up Tuesday morning to a glowing, jellyfish-shaped cloud drifting across the pre-dawn sky. It was not a missile test, a chemical leak, or an atmospheric anomaly. It was a routine SpaceX rocket launch caught at a specific geometric moment that turned exhaust vapor into a natural light show.

SpaceX launched a Falcon 9 rocket from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station's Space Launch Complex 40 at 6:35 a.m. EDT on October 13, 2026. The mission, CRS-35, is a cargo resupply run to the International Space Station carrying a Dragon spacecraft loaded with equipment and supplies, including the final pair of International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Arrays. Booster B1096, flying for the eighth time, was set to return to Landing Zone 40 (LZ-40) at Cape Canaveral after stage separation. Dragon is scheduled to dock autonomously with the ISS's Harmony module on October 15 at approximately 11 a.m. EDT, roughly 52 hours after launch.

Florida residents, coastal commuters, and early-morning skywatchers across the US East Coast can dismiss any hazard concerns after witnessing Tuesday's luminous atmospheric glow. The visual effect was a sunlit rocket exhaust plume—not a weather event or structural cloud formation. Stargazers planning to photograph future dawn or dusk rocket launches should track pre-dawn SpaceX launch windows from Cape Canaveral. Observers near Brevard County should also anticipate brief sonic booms during booster landings at Cape Canaveral.

**What causes a space jellyfish cloud in the sky?** The effect occurs when a rocket launches in the brief window before dawn or after dusk, when the rocket climbs into sunlight while observers on the ground remain in relative darkness. The rocket's exhaust—primarily water vapor and carbon dioxide—expands rapidly into the thin upper atmosphere. Sunlight striking that expanding cloud from below the horizon illuminates it against the dark sky beneath. Observers on the ground see a glowing blue-white bulb with shimmering trailing filaments—the "jellyfish." Tuesday's launch hit that window precisely, which is why the effect was visible from Florida up through the Carolinas. The conditions are predictable in principle: launch timing, altitude, the geometry of sunrise, and the observer's location all factor into whether the plume is visible.

Photographs taken with a long-exposure night mode reveal more vivid colors than the naked eye perceives in those conditions. SpaceX launches from Cape Canaveral frequently during periods of heavy ISS supply demand, so [the SpaceX launch schedule page](https://karmactive.com/spacex-launch-schedule-cape-canaveral) lists upcoming Falcon 9 windows for anyone wanting to watch for a similar effect. For more on [CRS-35's science cargo](https://karmactive.com/spacex-falcon-9-second-stage-explained) and what the IROSA arrays do aboard the ISS, the mission details are covered in the pre-launch briefing.

Dragon's ISS docking is scheduled for October 15. Coverage will follow if any anomalies arise.

### Texas Hiker Sahil Jilani Found Dead at Abrams Falls: What Makes the Great Smoky Mountains Waterfall So Dangerous

The family has warned other park visitors about the hazards at the site.

Abrams Falls sits at the end of a 5-mile round-trip trail in the Cades Cove section of Great Smoky Mountains National Park in Blount County, Tennessee. The waterfall is 25 feet high. A 2008 account in Backpacker Magazine cited 29 water-related deaths on the Abrams Creek trail since 1971, a tally that has grown with subsequent incidents. The National Park Service warns of an undertow and dangerous conditions at the site and strongly discourages swimming. An official medical examiner's report has not been released.

National park visitors, backcountry hikers, and autumn tourists visiting Great Smoky Mountains National Park must recognize deadly hidden hydraulic hazards around mountain waterfalls. Swimmers and hikers exploring Abrams Falls should strictly heed warning signage and avoid entering waterfall plunge pools regardless of swimming ability. Powerful underwater recirculating currents can trap swimmers beneath 25-foot cascades, transforming seemingly calm creek pools into unsurvivable drowning traps.

The danger at Abrams Falls is not the drop height. A 25-foot waterfall delivers an immense volume of water into a confined, deep pool at high velocity. The kinetic energy creates a recirculating undertow at the base—a hydraulic cycle that pulls swimmers toward the base of the falls and keeps them submerged. The pool looks still from the surface. The NPS strongly discourages swimming in the area and posts warning signs about undertow and dangerous conditions. Visitors have entered the water despite those warnings.

The combination of moderate waterfall height, high visitor volume, and the pool's deceptively calm appearance has produced recurring tragedies at this site across decades. [Great Smoky Mountains National Park receives more visitors annually than any other unit in the National Park System](https://karmactive.com/great-smoky-mountains-national-park-visitor-guide), and autumn foliage season is its peak period. ### Purdue University Expedition Reaches Nikumaroro to Investigate Possible Amelia Earhart Aircraft Remains

A 17-person research team from Purdue University and the Archaeological Legacy Institute arrived at Nikumaroro Island in the Pacific on October 12, 2026, beginning what expedition leaders describe as the most targeted underwater search yet for Amelia Earhart's missing Lockheed Electra 10-E aircraft. No wreckage has been identified. The team is investigating a specific underwater anomaly in Nikumaroro's lagoon that satellite imagery identified.

The expedition traveled five days aboard the *Marin Mainiku*, a Kiribati National Shipping Line vessel, departing from the capital Tarawa. The team includes maritime archaeological dive specialists and remote sensing analysts. Their focus is a formation called the Taraia Object—a submerged anomaly first visible in a 2015 satellite image that was analyzed and brought to researchers' attention in 2020. In the view of expedition researchers, its shape resembles features of a Lockheed Electra 10-E. Earhart and navigator Fred Noonan disappeared on July 2, 1937, during their attempted circumnavigation of the globe. Their last known radio position placed them near Howland Island, roughly 400 miles north of Nikumaroro. Purdue University was Earhart's institutional home and employer.

Aviation historians, maritime researchers, and archaeology enthusiasts face a potential, though not guaranteed, resolution to decades of investigation as Purdue University's expedition commences underwater excavation. Observers tracking historic recovery missions should monitor official Purdue Research Foundation dispatches rather than social media speculation over satellite anomalies. The expedition's findings will either produce tangible physical evidence to analyze against Earhart's Lockheed Electra 10-E, or reclassify the Taraia Object as natural lagoon debris.

### What Makes This Search Different From Previous Expeditions

Previous major expeditions, including oceanographer Robert Ballard's 2019 effort, focused on the deep ocean surrounding Nikumaroro, searching the abyssal depths where the aircraft might have sunk. Those expeditions found nothing conclusive. This expedition targets the island's shallow interior lagoon, not the open ocean. The Taraia Object sits in sheltered water accessible to divers, which means results come from direct physical inspection rather than sonar interpretation.

The scientific divide over what the object actually is centers on a competing explanation from veteran researcher Ric Gillespie of TIGHAR, who has investigated the Nikumaroro castaway hypothesis for decades and considers a fallen tree a more likely explanation for the anomaly. The Purdue expedition's purpose is to produce physical evidence that settles that disagreement—or confirms it.

If divers recover metallic debris, the next step is forensic comparison: alloy composition, rivet patterns, and structural measurements would need to match the documented specifications of Earhart's specific aircraft, a Lockheed Electra 10-E. A shape resembling an aircraft outline is not aircraft identification. The expedition has been careful not to claim discovery in its pre-departure statements.

His argument: cities that refuse to build housing are the cause, and Austin, Texas, is the proof that building more actually drives rents down. It's a supply-side argument. The Austin data is real. Whether the lesson applies cleanly to other cities is more complicated.

The Fox News interview came after Gallup published polling showing 43% of Americans viewed socialism positively in a September 2026 survey—a number Bezos called a product of economic frustration rather than ideology. He argued that when young Americans can't afford rent, capitalism fails its own argument, and the solution is supply. "Success is not villainy," he told Baier, framing the case against treating landlords as the primary obstacle to affordability.

Urban renters, real estate investors, and municipal housing policymakers face a direct lesson in regulatory economics from Jeff Bezos's analysis of Austin's housing market. Housing advocates assessing local rent relief should consider whether local zoning and permitting reforms that increase dense multifamily supply—rather than rent caps alone—can produce measurable results. Building developers and landlords should note that rapid supply additions have directly suppressed rental yields in Austin's market.

### What Austin Actually Did—and What Happened to Rents

Austin's affordability shift was policy-driven. In 2023, Austin removed citywide parking minimums for new development, alongside earlier permitting reforms and rezoning for higher-density housing. The result was a surge in housing construction that peaked in 2024 and added substantially to the city's rental supply. According to a Pew research analysis, rents in Austin's large apartment buildings—those with 50 or more units—fell 7% from 2023 to 2024. Rents in smaller buildings serving lower-income tenants fell about 11% over the same period. Austin asking rents have declined more than 16% from their 2022 peak.

Bezos's point is that Austin did nothing unusual in terms of economic fundamentals. It added supply. The implication is that cities maintaining restrictive single-family zoning—which limits how many units can be built on a given parcel—are producing scarcity artificially. Rents rise in tight markets because the market allows it, not necessarily because of pricing behavior independent of supply.

The counterpoint, which Bezos didn't address directly, is that Austin's geography and construction sector allowed fast scaling that older, denser cities cannot replicate as quickly. Building at Austin's pace in Chicago, New York, or San Francisco involves different land costs, labor costs, existing infrastructure, and political environments, according to housing researchers. The mechanism Bezos described is real; the timeline and scale vary considerably.

[Austin's housing construction and its effect on regional rents](https://karmactive.com/austin-housing-supply-rent-decline) sits alongside a broader pattern of [zoning reform efforts across US cities](https://karmactive.com/yimby-zoning-reform-us-cities) that advocates argue could produce similar outcomes elsewhere.

Bezos framed his defense of capitalism around the economic pain of younger Americans—an unusual rhetorical move that acknowledged the frustration driving the Gallup numbers rather than dismissing it. The argument is that pro-market solutions fail politically when the market prices people out of housing, and that solving the supply problem is the way to win back confidence in the system.
