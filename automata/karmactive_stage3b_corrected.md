**Hurricane Polo Reaches Category 5 with 165 MPH Winds: Path, Mexico Warnings, and US Flood Risks**

Hurricane Polo reached Category 5 strength early September 23, with maximum sustained winds of 165 mph, according to the National Hurricane Center, as it tracked slowly northwest off Mexico's Pacific coast. The National Hurricane Center had a Tropical Storm Warning in effect from Tecpan de Galeana to Punta San Telmo. The storm's center is not forecast to make direct landfall — but that does not mean residents from coastal Mexico to the US Southwest are in the clear.

As of NHC Advisory 14 at 9 p.m. CST on September 23, Polo's center was positioned roughly 210 miles south of Zihuatanejo, Mexico, moving northwest near 6 mph. Minimum central pressure was dropping as sustained winds reached approximately 160 mph (260 km/h), with gusts higher. Hurricane-force winds extended 40 miles from the center; tropical-storm-force winds reached out 105 miles. NHC forecasters described Polo's 24-hour intensification as "truly remarkable."

Even without a direct eye strike on land, Polo's slow crawl is generating real danger on two fronts. Coastal Mexico faces storm surge and 4 to 8 inches of rainfall, with isolated higher totals, driven into steep mountain terrain, where runoff concentrates rapidly regardless of where the center sits. Over the next 72 hours, residual tropical moisture may track north, pulled into a mid-latitude trough and steered toward Arizona, New Mexico, and parts of West Texas — desert basins that drain fast and produce flash flooding even from moderate totals.

**Why Polo Intensified So Fast — and What That Means Inland**

El Niño conditions pushed sea-surface temperatures in the eastern Pacific significantly above long-term averages. That warmer water is fuel: more heat energy available for the storm to convert into wind and rainfall. Low vertical wind shear — little variation in wind speed between low and high altitude — allowed Polo to organize without the disruption that normally limits intensification. Meteorologists confirmed both factors were present.

The element most breaking-news reports missed is what Polo's slow forward speed means for rainfall. A storm traveling under 5 mph lingers over terrain far longer than a fast-moving system. In the steep coastal ranges of Guerrero and neighboring states, slow movement combined with orographic lift — air forced up mountain slopes, cooling and releasing rain — can prolong rainfall and enhance precipitation totals. Communities in those inland valleys face flood risk that does not require direct eye passage.

For the US, the threat is separate from the storm core. As Polo weakens in cooler water north of its current position, upper-level wind patterns are expected to capture the remnant moisture plume and redirect it northeast, potentially raising flood risk in Arizona, New Mexico, and West Texas. [For the full 2026 Pacific hurricane season context, see our Pacific hurricane season tracker.] [For background on how El Niño raises sea surface temperatures and storm intensity, see our El Niño explainer.]

**Will Hurricane Polo hit the United States?**
Polo's center is not expected to reach the continental US. The storm will weaken as it moves into cooler waters off Baja California. Its remnant tropical moisture, however, may be captured by a mid-latitude trough and steered northeast into the US Southwest within four to six days, raising flash flood risk in Arizona, New Mexico, and West Texas. Desert terrain drains poorly; even moderate totals produce fast-moving conditions.

An international research team began core drilling at the Durupınar formation in eastern Türkiye on September 23, 2026. During drilling, a percussion bit broke after encountering a very hard layer about 4 to 5 metres below the surface in one of the holes. The expedition says the layer may be petrified wood. That has not been confirmed.

The drilling operation is a joint project between Sivas Cumhuriyet University and the Noah's Ark Scans research group. The Turkish government issued official permits for the excavation. Prof. Dr. Cenker Atila of Sivas Cumhuriyet University's archaeology department leads the site work alongside Andrew Jones, vice president of the Noah's Ark Research Project. A dry percussion system — no cooling fluid — was used, designed for hard materials. The bit broke on contact with the dense layer rather than passing through it. Cores from other depths have been extracted to depths of up to 18 metres and are being sent for third-party laboratory testing, with results expected in 2027. A drone is planned to investigate mapped voids and corridors inside the formation once access permits it.

Until laboratory assays return results, readers should treat the current findings as active evidence-gathering, not a confirmed discovery. The field team's working hypothesis — that the dense layer is petrified ancient timber — is one possibility. Other explanations remain possible until laboratory analysis identifies the composition of the material.

Ground-penetrating radar surveys conducted before the drilling found what the team interprets as right-angle structures and internal voids within the formation's 157-meter boat-shaped outline. Crucially, the team says no bedrock or solid limestone has been encountered so far *inside* the formation boundary, while standard bedrock samples taken just *outside* it behave differently. A 1996 paper by Lorence Collins and David Fasold concluded that the formation was a natural geological structure. That conclusion remains the subject of debate; the 2026 drilling is the latest effort to gather direct subsurface evidence.

**Did scientists find petrified wood at the Durupınar site?** Researchers encountered a layer hard enough to shatter a percussion drill bit at roughly 4 to 5 meters below the surface. ## STORY 5 (Priority 5)

**Kent Meningitis Outbreak: How the Bacteria Acquired Genes and Why Cases Were So Severe**

A meningitis B outbreak in Kent, England earlier this year infected 21 people and killed two. Researchers at UKHSA and the University of Oxford have now completed bacterial genome sequencing on the outbreak strain — and found something unusual: the bacteria had acquired genetic material from other bacteria that normally live harmlessly in the human throat.

The March 2026 outbreak was linked to a Canterbury nightclub. All 21 confirmed cases were hospitalized. Two people died. As of the UKHSA count on April 1, 2026, 18 of those cases carried the identified outbreak strain subtype. Researchers identified genetic changes that may help explain the severity of the outbreak.

Understanding what those changes meant for patients requires a brief explanation of how bacteria exchange DNA. Unlike humans, bacteria do not need sexual reproduction to share genetic traits. Through a process called horizontal gene transfer, bacteria can pick up fragments of DNA from other organisms in their environment. This can happen via direct contact between bacteria, through viral particles that carry genetic material between cells, or through bacteria absorbing free-floating DNA released when nearby cells die. The result is that a bacterium can acquire a new capability in a single generation — far more rapidly than through gradual mutation alone.

**What the Oxford and UKHSA researchers found**

The Kent outbreak strain had done exactly this. Genome analysis showed it had acquired DNA segments from bacteria that typically colonize the human throat without causing disease. The changes affected two areas that matter for how the bacteria behave during infection: how the bacteria interact with human cells when trying to establish infection, and how they acquire iron — a resource that bacteria need to multiply but that the human body actively restricts as a defense mechanism.

UKHSA researchers noted that the genetic changes may also have affected how the immune system recognizes the bacteria, potentially altering the immune response. Researchers are continuing to examine whether this contributed to the severity of cases in Kent.

This type of genetic acquisition is not hypothetical or rare in microbiology — it is one important mechanism by which antibiotic resistance spreads between bacterial populations, and it is a known driver of changes in pathogen behavior over time. What makes the Kent finding noteworthy is that it provides a specific, documented example of horizontal gene transfer appearing to correlate with an unusually severe outbreak, rather than a routine one. The outbreak happened in a nightclub environment where people were in close proximity, providing conditions for disease transmission.

**Vaccines and protection**

The meningitis B vaccine — MenB — is offered routinely to infants in the UK through the NHS childhood immunization schedule. Under the routine childhood schedule, teenagers were not automatically offered MenB vaccination — though a one-off catch-up programme was introduced in 2026 following this outbreak. This is relevant for a nightclub-linked cluster primarily affecting young adults. The MenACWY vaccine, which covers four other strains of meningococcal disease, is offered to teenagers before they enter secondary school. Neither vaccine covers every possible strain variant.

[For full information on UK meningitis vaccination schedules and which groups are covered, see our vaccine guide.] Parents and teenagers who are unsure of their vaccination status can check with their GP surgery.

**Why was the Kent meningitis outbreak so severe?** The outbreak strain had acquired genetic material from other bacteria through horizontal gene transfer — a process where bacteria pick up and incorporate DNA from their environment. The acquired genes appear to have altered how the bacteria interacted with human cells, how they obtained iron (essential for growth inside the body), and potentially how the immune system recognized them. UKHSA and Oxford researchers identified these changes through bacterial genome sequencing of outbreak samples.

UKHSA said the findings underline the importance of genomic surveillance and rapid outbreak response. The research is expected to contribute to understanding how horizontal gene transfer may shape future outbreak risk. ## STORY 7 (Priority 7)

**El Niño Is Strengthening: What the 2026–27 Winter Forecast Actually Says**

NOAA is forecasting a very strong El Niño event for the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter of 2026–27, with greater than a 90% chance of a very strong El Niño during fall and winter 2026–27, and a 75% chance of a historic-strength event during October through December. What that means for your winter depends heavily on where you live — and the popular framing connecting polar vortex behavior to severe cold in specific US states is more complicated than the headlines suggest.

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center released its seasonal outlook for October through December 2026. It favors above-normal temperatures across large parts of the United States, including nearly all areas east of the Mississippi River. That is the opposite of a widespread cold-winter forecast for the eastern US. El Niño's general pattern tends to bring warmer-than-average conditions to the northern tier of states during winter and increased precipitation to the South. That pattern holds more of the time than not — but seasonal forecasts are probabilistic, not deterministic. Any given location can see cold outbreaks even in a year where the overall seasonal average runs warm.

**What the polar vortex is and what it is not**

The polar vortex is a large area of low pressure and cold air surrounding the North and South Poles. It exists year-round. In winter, it is usually strong and keeps Arctic air penned toward the poles. Major stratospheric disturbances, including sudden stratospheric warming events, can disrupt the polar vortex, allowing cold Arctic air to spill south into the US, Europe, and Asia. That is what produces the severe cold events that US media covers as "polar vortex" events.

A *strengthening* polar vortex is the opposite of that scenario. A stronger polar vortex is generally associated with stronger circulation around the Arctic and can reduce the likelihood of major southward Arctic-air displacements, tending to produce milder conditions across the mid-latitudes, including the US. Forecasters watching for a severe winter pay more attention to signals that the polar vortex might weaken during winter — not strengthen.

El Niño winters in the eastern US tend to shift the jet stream south, which changes storm tracks and precipitation patterns without guaranteeing extreme cold. The connection between El Niño and any specific state's winter temperature is real but indirect, expressed as a statistical tendency over many past El Niño events, not a firm prediction for this particular winter.

For Ohio and New Jersey specifically — two states that have appeared in early winter forecast coverage — NOAA's October-December outlook does not show elevated probability of below-normal temperatures. Those states fall in the above-normal temperature zone under the current probabilistic forecast. That does not rule out cold outbreaks, which can happen even in warm-leaning seasons, but it contradicts a blanket prediction of a colder-than-normal winter for the eastern US. [For NOAA's full seasonal outlook maps, see our winter 2026–27 weather outlook tracker.] [For a plain-language explanation of how El Niño affects US weather patterns, see our El Niño explainer.]

**Will the polar vortex cause a colder winter?** A strengthening polar vortex is generally associated with keeping cold air near the poles and milder conditions across the US, not colder ones. Polar vortex *disruptions* — when it weakens — are what send Arctic air south. NOAA's current seasonal outlook for fall and winter 2026–27 favors above-normal temperatures across most of the eastern US under strong El Niño conditions. Severe cold is still possible during any individual cold snap but is not the dominant seasonal signal.

NOAA updates its seasonal outlook monthly. The next update covering November through January will sharpen the forecast as autumn data comes in.

**Venomous Yellow-Bellied Sea Snake Found at Crystal Cove Beach in Orange County**

A yellow-bellied sea snake turned up at Crystal Cove State Beach in Orange County this week — the first confirmed sighting of the species on a California beach since 2018. Experts told the Los Angeles Times the animal poses no danger to people who don't handle it.

The yellow-bellied sea snake (*Hydrophis platurus*) is a highly venomous tropical marine species found widely across the Pacific and Indian Oceans. It is the only sea snake with a confirmed range that occasionally reaches the Eastern Pacific coast of the Americas. In California, sightings are rare and there is no evidence of an established breeding population here. Sightings are clustered almost entirely around years when Pacific water temperatures run unusually warm.

If you see one on a beach, do not approach or pick it up. Yellow-bellied sea snakes carry potent venom used to immobilize fish prey. They are not aggressive toward humans, but bites can occur if a person handles or steps on one. Alert park rangers or wildlife officials if you encounter one that appears stranded or distressed.

The current sighting at Crystal Cove follows a period of elevated Pacific sea surface temperatures associated with developing El Niño conditions. Warmer surface water off the Southern California coast has also brought other warm-water marine species — including certain fish and jellyfish — into ranges where they are rarely seen. Researchers caution that one sighting of one animal cannot establish a directional range shift. A genuine northward expansion of the species' breeding or foraging range would require multiple years of observations and population data, none of which currently exists for California.

The last confirmed California sighting before this week's was in 2018.
