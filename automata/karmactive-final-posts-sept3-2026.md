This is not a lactose intolerance concern. It is a milk allergy warning. For people with a genuine milk allergy, eating this product could trigger a serious or even life-threatening reaction. Think of it like a peanut allergy — even a small amount can cause the immune system to react aggressively.

A TJX analyst first caught the problem when whey, a protein derived from milk, was found in the product while milk was completely absent from the ingredient label.

The candy was sold across 13 states: Arizona, California, Connecticut, Georgia, Indiana, Massachusetts, Nevada, New Jersey, North Carolina, Ohio, Texas, Virginia, and Wyoming.

Five specific product styles are affected:

- 54040-CHEY — 10 oz plastic bag
- 54077-CHEY — 7 oz acrylic box
- 58008-CHEY — 10.5 oz round plastic jar
- 58089-CHEY — 16 oz designer plastic pouch
- 54083-CHEY — 11 oz plastic bag with tag

No illnesses had been reported at the time of the recall notice.

Walking or biking your kid to school in San Francisco sounds simple. The reality is that only 37 out of 249 schools — roughly 15% — are currently connected to the city's established biking and rolling network for people of all ages and abilities.

That's the opening number from the San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency's draft Safe School Connections plan, which aims to give every K-12 school in the city a safe route to the broader cycling infrastructure.

The math shows just how far there is to go. Another 57 schools, about 23%, are connected to a planned future network that doesn't fully exist yet. That leaves 73 schools still in a category requiring a different approach entirely. So right now, most children attending San Francisco's public schools don't have a safe, continuous path that links where they walk or bike with the rest of the city's routes.

The reason the numbers are stark is partly by design. San Francisco's school system doesn't work like a typical city where kids walk to a school around the corner. The district uses a citywide choice model, meaning students often travel across neighborhoods, making safe routes harder to map and harder to build.

What SFMTA is calling a "School Connector" is not just a painted bike lane. The plan envisions a toolkit that includes raised crosswalks, speed humps, traffic circles, separated bike lanes, painted safety zones, traffic diverters, and rapid-flashing beacons at crossings. Each school gets matched with the combination that makes sense for its streets.

SFMTA says connecting every school could take up to 10 years. The first schools in line are those already within one block of the existing or planned network — so progress will build outward from where the infrastructure is already closest.

The agency hasn't been standing still in the meantime. It says it has already completed more than 40 school crossing improvements and removed sight-blocking objects from more than 2,300 school intersections, a process called daylighting. New 15-mph and 20-mph school zone speed limits have also been put in place.

The current draft is open for public comment until September 30, 2026. After that, SFMTA expects to bring a finalized version before its board in late 2026 or early 2027.

If you're a parent, school employee, or neighborhood resident in San Francisco and you have specific intersections or routes you think need attention, the window to say so officially closes at the end of this month.

**SEO Slug:** dusable-lake-shore-drive-bridge-stuck-heat-chicago-2026
**Category:** News / USA
**Tags:** bridge, extreme-weather, climate, emergency, disaster, usa, government

On September 2, one of Chicago's most heavily used roadways came to a standstill because the bridge wouldn't close.

The DuSable Lake Shore Drive bridge over the Chicago River was lifted for a routine pass-through around 10 a.m. When operators tried to bring it back down, it wouldn't fully close. The bridge stayed open — and traffic stayed blocked — until shortly after 2:30 p.m., a disruption of more than four hours.

The cause, according to city officials, was heat. When metal gets extremely hot, it expands. That's not a problem under normal conditions because bridges are built with small gaps and tolerances that account for seasonal changes in size. But on a day where heat index values reached 100°F, the steel in the bridge expanded beyond what the closing mechanism could compensate for. The two halves of the movable bridge couldn't align properly to lock together.

The fix was essentially a cooling job. Crews from the Chicago Fire Department pumped water over the steel structure to bring the temperature down. As the metal contracted, the bridge could finally seat itself and close.

What made this story more than a heat-related traffic inconvenience is what a 2024 inspection had found. City and state inspection records classified the DuSable Lake Shore Drive bridge as both structurally deficient and functionally obsolete.

That terminology is worth understanding carefully. Structurally deficient does not mean a bridge is about to fall down. Under federal inspection standards, the designation means that certain structural elements have deteriorated to a condition where they need additional monitoring, maintenance, or eventual rehabilitation. It's closer to a doctor flagging a condition to watch — not a crisis, but not something to ignore.

Chicago has a large and aging bridge network crossing the Chicago River, and maintenance backlogs on urban infrastructure are not unusual across American cities. This specific incident shows what can happen when aging mechanical components meet weather conditions they weren't originally designed to handle with the same margins.

No injuries were reported.

**Title:** Conservation Groups and Tribes Are Suing to Restore Utah's National Monuments. The Legal Battle Just Got a New Chapter.
**SEO Slug:** bears-ears-grand-staircase-escalante-lawsuit-2026-antiquities-act
**Category:** Policy / Environment / Nature
**Tags:** environmental-law, environmental-lawsuit, conservation, policy, politics, donald-trump, environmental-protection, indigenous

Two of America's most significant stretches of public land had their protections dramatically reduced in July 2026. Now that decision is heading back to court.

On July 13, President Trump signed proclamations reducing the size of Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monument and Bears Ears National Monument in Utah. The numbers are striking. Grand Staircase-Escalante was cut from approximately 1.87 million acres to about 181,500 acres — a reduction of more than 90%. Bears Ears was cut from approximately 1.36 million acres to about 121,100 acres, a similarly steep reduction.

The White House's stated rationale is that the remaining areas still protect specific historic, scientific, and cultural objects, while the land removed from monument status can now be managed for multiple uses — including mining and grazing.

On September 2, conservation organizations filed supplemental legal complaints asking federal courts to reinstate earlier challenges to the reductions and to review the 2026 proclamations alongside the prior 2017 monument reductions.

The core legal question is whether a president can actually shrink or eliminate a national monument once it's been established. The Antiquities Act of 1906 gives the executive branch clear authority to create national monuments. What the law doesn't say, and what has never been definitively settled by the Supreme Court, is whether that same authority extends to dismantling them. The organizations filing suit argue that reducing a monument by 90% is functionally dismantling it — and that only Congress holds that power under the Constitution.

The Bears Ears case carries specific weight because of how the monument was established. It was created in 2016 following unified advocacy by five Tribal nations: the Hopi Tribe, Navajo Nation, Ute Indian Tribe, Ute Mountain Ute Tribe, and Pueblo of Zuni. The monument included a co-management framework designed to give those nations a formal role in decisions about the land. The reductions remove that framework from the majority of the originally designated area.

The administration's position is that its 2026 proclamations set more appropriate boundaries. It argues that the law grants the president both the power to designate and the power to adjust monument boundaries. Conservation groups and tribal nations say that reading goes far beyond what Congress intended when it passed the Antiquities Act more than a century ago.

The legal proceedings are now active in federal court. No ruling has been issued. The outcome will likely determine not just the future of these two monuments, but the extent to which future administrations — of any party — can alter monument protections established by their predecessors.

**Title:** An 85-Year-Old WWII Shipwreck Off Albania Is Now Home to Critically Endangered Seals — and an Invasive Venomous Fish
**SEO Slug:** wwii-hospital-ship-po-shipwreck-albania-monk-seal-devil-firefish-2026
**Category:** Science / Nature / Marine Life
**Tags:** marine-life, conservation, endangered-species, ocean, ecosystem, archaeology, science, discovery, history

In August 2023, researchers photographing marine life near a sunken WWII warship off the Albanian coast captured something that hadn't been recorded in that bay for 27 years: a Mediterranean monk seal.

The vessel is the Hospital Ship Po — an Italian warship sunk by British torpedo bombers in 1941. It sits at between 33 and 37 meters depth in Vlora Bay, inside the Karaburun-Sazan Marine Protected Area along the Albanian coast. The ship is approximately 135 meters long and has spent the last 85 years becoming something it was never designed to be — a reef.

Researchers conducted a detailed monitoring program between 2022 and 2024. They used scientific dives, remotely operated vehicles, high-resolution sonar, 3D photogrammetry, and biological and sediment surveys — essentially every modern tool available for underwater environmental assessment.

What they found was substantial. In the broader seabed survey around the wreck, researchers recorded 151 different benthic taxa — that's the term for organisms living on or near the seafloor, including sponges, corals, worms, mollusks, and various invertebrates. On the wreck structures themselves, 54 distinct taxa were recorded. More than 80% of the ship's surface was covered in what researchers describe as biogenic material — meaning living organisms had colonized most of it.

The monk seal photograph is the most striking finding. The Mediterranean monk seal, known scientifically as Monachus monachus, is one of the most endangered marine mammals in the world. The researchers describe the August 2023 photograph near the Po wreck as the first confirmed sighting in Vlora Bay since 1996. Whether the seal was using the wreck as a feeding ground, a resting area, or was passing through remains unknown — the authors say longer-term behavioral research is needed to understand how the seal is using the area.

The wreck is not entirely a conservation success story, and the researchers are careful not to present it as one. Alongside the native species, the study documented the devil firefish, known scientifically as Pterois miles. This is an invasive species originally from the Red Sea and Indo-Pacific that has been spreading into the Mediterranean through the Suez Canal. It's venomous, it preys on native juvenile fish, and its presence on the Po wreck represents one of the northernmost confirmed sightings of the species in the Adriatic Sea.

The ecological picture the study presents is genuinely complex. A shipwreck provides hard structure in an otherwise soft-bottomed environment. Fish and invertebrates colonize it. A critically endangered seal turns up near it. But so does an invasive predator that wasn't supposed to be there at all.

The researchers also found evidence of squid-family spawning on or near the wreck — another sign that the structure is being actively used by marine life.

What the study establishes is that the Po wreck functions as an ecological hotspot in an area where hard substrate for marine colonization is limited. What it does not establish — and the researchers are explicit about this — is that the wreck has restored or repaired the surrounding ecosystem. The long-term effects, including how the monk seal and the devil firefish each continue to use the area, are still being studied.

It's a story about nature doing what it has always done: finding a use for whatever structure is available. In this case, that structure happens to be an 85-year-old hospital ship.
