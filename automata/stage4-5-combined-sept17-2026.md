Manitobans will not make the usual one-hour move back to standard time on November 1, 2026. Winter schedules will hold on daylight time rather than shifting back an hour. Off Australia's Gold Coast, a female humpback whale stayed close to her stillborn calf for hours after it died. Scientists have now documented the behaviour in a peer-reviewed study—and their conclusions are more careful than the word "grief" suggests.

The event was recorded on July 17, 2025, at Palm Beach reef near the Gold Coast. Researchers observed the female humpback from a private vessel and captured footage of the encounter. The study, [published September 16, 2026 in the journal *Discover Animals*](https://link.springer.com/journal/41703), is the first detailed documentation of this kind of behaviour in a baleen whale.

The calf was stillborn. The mother did not attempt to carry or surface the calf. She remained in close proximity to the body for an extended period—hours, and possibly longer, based on the researchers' observations.

The footage is scientifically valuable because it gives marine biologists a comparable record. Post-mortem behaviour has been documented in dolphins, orcas, and other toothed whales—sometimes called odontocetes. But baleen whales, the group that includes humpbacks, are rarely observed in this context. The Gold Coast case adds a baleen-whale data point to a small and growing body of evidence about how cetaceans respond to death. For researchers studying animal cognition and social behaviour, the rarity of the observation makes it worth examining closely, even from a single event. The [Australian government's review of humpback whale conservation status](https://www.karmactive.com/humpback-whales-not-endangered-in-australia-any-longer-australian-government/) provides important context on how population recovery has allowed research like this to happen in Australian coastal waters.

## What the science actually found

The paper's authors describe the behaviour as "post-mortem attentive behaviour." That phrase is deliberate. Grief is a subjective internal state, and scientists cannot measure it directly in a wild animal without access to physiological or neurological data. The researchers say the footage documents sustained proximity and apparent attention to the dead calf—but they explicitly stop short of claiming the whale was grieving in the way humans understand the term.

One detail the paper highlights is particularly telling. The mother did not attempt to bring the calf to the surface. In documented cases involving dolphins, mothers have sometimes tried to push dead calves above the waterline, seemingly attempting to get them to breathe. The humpback mother's behaviour was different. The researchers suggest this may indicate that she recognised the calf was dead shortly after birth, rather than persisting in a response driven by the expectation of a live animal. The observation raises the possibility that the whale responded differently because the calf was non-responsive, but the study cannot establish what the whale understood. Earlier work on [how ocean noise pollution affects whale behaviour and communication](https://www.karmactive.com/whales-could-lose-migration-paths-due-to-ocean-noise-pollution-100-times-louder-than-70-years-ago/) shows why observational windows like this one are becoming rarer—and more scientifically precious.

The distinction between documented behaviour and interpreted emotion matters for how this study gets reported. The paper records what the whale did. It does not record what she felt. Saying a humpback "grieves" borrows emotional vocabulary that the evidence does not fully support. What the evidence does support is that a humpback whale showed sustained post-mortem attention to her dead calf—behaviour the researchers describe as the first detailed documented occurrence in a baleen species.

**Do whales grieve?**

Evidence suggests some cetaceans show behaviours consistent with grief-like or post-mortem attention, but scientists cannot directly establish subjective grief in wild whales without physiological or neurological measurements. The Gold Coast case is notable because it documents prolonged attention by a humpback whale to a stillborn calf—behaviour documented as the first detailed occurrence in a baleen whale—and gives researchers a new point of comparison with post-mortem behaviour already documented in dolphins and orcas.

The Gold Coast footage now sits in the scientific record as a documented case. Whether more observations follow, and whether they show similar patterns, will shape how researchers interpret this one. The study is available through *Discover Animals* for those who want to read the researchers' language directly rather than through media summaries.

Researchers have worked out T. rex's body temperature by analysing the chemistry locked inside fossil teeth—and the number they landed on is close to a human's.

A team at UCLA used three fossilized T. rex teeth to estimate the dinosaur's body temperature at approximately 36.3°C, or about 97°F, with an uncertainty of approximately ±2.5°C. The research was [published in the journal *Science Advances*](https://www.science.org/journal/sciadv) and represents one of the clearest measurements yet of how T. rex regulated heat internally. The result is consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism rather than the cold-blooded biology associated with modern reptiles.

The finding puts T. rex in a thermal range that overlaps with living mammals and birds, not with crocodiles or lizards. A crocodile's body temperature tracks the environment around it. T. rex, if this measurement holds, maintained an internally regulated body temperature consistent with warm-blooded metabolism—which has implications for how it moved, hunted, and functioned as an animal.

The practical weight of this research is what it tells us about T. rex as a living animal. A warm-blooded T. rex would have had higher energy needs than a cold-blooded one, suggesting it required more food and likely had a faster metabolism—though these are biological inferences from the temperature finding, not direct measurements. Related discoveries about [how ancient reptile migrations shaped dinosaur evolution](https://www.karmactive.com/tiny-reptiles-10000-mile-trek-through-hellish-heat-zones-changed-dinosaur-evolution-path/) offer wider context on how temperature physiology played out across theropod lineages.

## How a fossil tooth becomes a thermometer

The technique behind this measurement is called clumped isotope thermometry. It works on a specific chemical property of tooth enamel: the way certain isotopes of carbon and oxygen bond together inside the mineral as it forms. The ratio of those bonded pairs changes with temperature—when enamel forms at higher temperatures, the ratio shifts in a predictable direction.

Tooth enamel is relatively resistant to alteration, allowing researchers to recover chemical information from some fossil specimens. It survives fossilisation better than bone, which means the chemical signals inside it can survive tens of millions of years. The researchers were able to read those signals from three T. rex teeth and calculate the temperature at which the enamel originally formed inside the living animal.

The method has been validated against modern animals with known body temperatures. It has also been used on other fossil species. Applying it to T. rex teeth gave a result of approximately 97°F, which the team at UCLA says is consistent with other lines of evidence pointing toward warm-blooded biology in large theropod dinosaurs. The study used three teeth, which is a small sample. The researchers acknowledge this, and the technique's consistency with other evidence adds context to the measurement. Research on [mass dinosaur deposits in Alberta](https://www.karmactive.com/river-of-death-in-alberta-holds-bones-of-10000-dinosaurs-trapped-by-flash-flood-72-million-years-ago/) continues to yield specimens that could expand the sample pool for studies like this one.

This finding applies to T. rex specifically. It does not establish the body temperature of all dinosaurs, and it does not prove every dinosaur species operated the same way. The diversity of the dinosaur family tree means generalising from T. rex to Triceratops, or to small feathered dinosaurs, requires separate evidence.

**How did scientists determine T. rex's body temperature?**

Researchers analysed chemical patterns preserved in fossilized tooth enamel. The technique—clumped isotope thermometry—uses the ratio of certain bonded carbon-oxygen pairs in enamel, which varies with temperature. That ratio was measured in three T. rex teeth, allowing the team to calculate the temperature at which the enamel originally formed inside the living animal. UCLA's result: approximately 97°F (±2.5°C), consistent with a warm-blooded metabolism.

The *Science Advances* paper is available for researchers and interested readers who want to examine the methodology and sample details. Further work with additional specimens would strengthen the finding. For now, the three teeth have put T. rex's temperature on the record at roughly human body heat.
