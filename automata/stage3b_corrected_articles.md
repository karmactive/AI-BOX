### Free NHS Flu and Covid Jabs: Who Qualifies This Autumn and How to Book

The main adult NHS autumn flu programme and the autumn Covid-19 booster campaign opened on 1 October 2026 in England, and millions of people are now eligible for free flu and Covid-19 booster jabs. The two programmes run side by side — but they do not have the same eligibility rules, and the age thresholds are different in a way that confuses a lot of people every year.

Here's who qualifies for what, and what the fastest way to book actually is.

**Who can get the free flu vaccine**

For the 2026 to 2027 programme, a free NHS flu jab is available to:

- Everyone aged 65 and over
- Adults aged 18 to 64 who have certain health conditions — including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, chronic heart disease, or conditions that affect the immune system
- Pregnant women, at any stage of pregnancy
- Residents of care homes for older adults
- Carers and frontline health and social care workers
- Children aged 2 and 3 (nasal spray offered through GP surgeries) and primary-school-age children (nasal spray through schools)
- Secondary-school-age children (nasal spray through schools, typically up to Year 11)
- Close household contacts of immunocompromised people
- People experiencing homelessness aged 16 and over

If you are aged 65 to 74 with no chronic health conditions, you qualify for a free flu jab — but you are not automatically eligible for a free NHS Covid booster this season. That is the most common point of confusion in the autumn campaign.

If you fall into an NHS-eligible category for either vaccine, booking through the NHS App, the NHS website, or a participating high street pharmacy takes a few minutes. Some pharmacies offer walk-in availability for flu jabs during the early weeks of the campaign, though availability varies — check with your local pharmacy before attending without an appointment. [INTERNAL LINK: how to use the NHS App]

**Who can get the free Covid-19 booster**

The 2026 autumn Covid booster programme has a stricter eligibility threshold than the flu campaign. The NHS is offering free Covid jabs to:

- Adults aged 75 and over
- Residents in care homes for older adults
- People aged 6 months and over who are severely immunosuppressed

If you are aged 65 to 74 and not immunosuppressed, you are not currently eligible for a free NHS Covid booster this autumn. You can access a Covid vaccine through private pharmacy services, but it will not be covered by the NHS. [INTERNAL LINK: cost of living health support UK]

**Can you have both jabs at the same appointment?**

Yes. Clinical guidance confirms it is safe to receive the flu vaccine and Covid-19 booster in the same appointment, typically in different arms. Combining both jabs into one visit is the recommended approach for anyone who qualifies for both — it halves the number of clinic trips and does not affect how well either vaccine works.

**Why is the Covid booster age limit 75, not 65?**

The Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation (JCVI) sets eligibility thresholds based on risk modelling updated each year. For the 2026 autumn programme, the JCVI recommended a broader flu offer for people aged 65 and over and a more targeted Covid booster offer for those aged 75 and over and severely immunocompromised people.

People aged 65 to 74 are not in the current priority cohort for the free Covid booster, which is why the age threshold sits 10 years above the flu threshold. This may change mid-campaign if the JCVI updates its guidance in response to new variant data. The UKHSA is expected to publish a mid-campaign uptake report in late November.

**What if you're not sure whether you qualify?**

The NHS eligibility checker on the NHS website is quick to use. You enter your age and any long-term health conditions, and it tells you which vaccinations you can book and how. If you qualify based on a health condition in the 18-to-64 age group, your GP surgery should send an invitation, but you can also book proactively without waiting for one.

The campaign runs through the winter. Supplies are currently available, but uptake typically increases sharply through October — booking early means more appointment choice and pharmacy availability. [INTERNAL LINK: autumn health preparations UK]

The UKHSA mid-campaign update is expected in late November. Check back for any eligibility changes or new variant guidance.

## ARTICLE 4 — Priority 4
### Meteor Showers October 2026: When and How to See the Draconids and Orionids in the UK

Two meteor showers are active this month. The Draconids peak this week, on the nights of 8 and 9 October, and the Orionids follow later in the month, peaking on 21 and 22 October. Neither shower requires binoculars, a telescope, or a trip to a remote dark-sky site — but the Draconids have a timing quirk that most stargazing guides get wrong.

**Draconids: watch in the evening, not the early hours**

The Draconids are one of the very few meteor showers best watched in the early evening rather than after midnight. Their radiant point — the area of sky the meteors appear to streak from — sits in the constellation Draco, which reaches its highest point in the northern sky shortly after dark in early October.

For UK viewers, the optimal window on 8 and 9 October is roughly 7:30 PM to 10:00 PM. Looking north-northwest and allowing 20 minutes for your eyes to adapt to the dark will give you the best chance of seeing individual streaks. After about 10 PM, the radiant begins to descend toward the horizon, and visible Draconid activity is likely to reduce as a result.

The parent comet is 21P/Giacobini-Zinner. The Draconids travel at around 20 kilometres per second on atmospheric entry — slow, by meteor standards — which means they produce longer, more gradual streaks rather than fast white flashes. In a typical year, the Draconids produce a relatively low rate of meteors — the IMO's expected rate is around 5 per hour under dark skies at peak. Activity can occasionally spike considerably higher when Earth passes through a denser filament of comet debris, though 2026 predictions describe the activity level as uncertain.

The 2026 Draconid peak falls near a new Moon, which means the sky will be genuinely dark during the optimal evening window — good news for visibility. [INTERNAL LINK: UK dark sky areas guide]

**Orionids: pre-dawn viewing from 21 October**

The Orionids are active from 2 October through to 7 November, but the peak falls on 21 and 22 October. Unlike the Draconids, these are pre-dawn meteors. The radiant point lies in Orion, which rises above the horizon in the early hours and is highest in the southern sky between roughly 2:00 AM and 5:00 AM.

The Orionids are fast-moving — around 66 kilometres per second — and often leave brief glowing ionisation trails in their wake. The International Meteor Organization puts the expected peak rate at around 20 meteors per hour under ideal dark-sky conditions, though typical suburban viewing will be lower.

One complication for the 2026 Orionid peak: the full Moon falls on 26 October, so the Orionid peak occurs under a waxing gibbous Moon rather than a full Moon — meaning on peak nights, the Moon will be nearly full and washing out faint meteors. The pre-dawn hours remain the best bet because the Moon will be lower by then, but expect a reduced visible count compared to a dark-Moon year. The Orionids' parent body is 1P/Halley — the same Halley's Comet that last passed through the inner solar system in 1986. [INTERNAL LINK: Perseid meteor shower 2026 results]

**What actually helps you see more meteors**

Telescopes and binoculars restrict your field of view and work against you for meteor watching. The more sky you can see at once, the better your chances. Lie flat on your back on a clear night, look straight up or slightly toward the radiant direction, and give your eyes at least 15 to 20 minutes to adjust before you start counting.

Urban light pollution can reduce visible meteor counts by up to 80 percent compared to a rural dark-sky location. If you're in a city, even moving to a local park away from streetlights makes a noticeable difference.

Both showers can be seen across the UK, Ireland, the northern US, and Canada. For Southern Hemisphere observers, the Draconids are very difficult to observe because Draco never rises high enough above the horizon; the Orionids are visible from the Southern Hemisphere, though they appear lower in the sky.

The Orionid peak on 21 to 22 October is the next opportunity if the Draconid display disappoints. Weather forecasts for your specific location are the most useful tool in the 24 hours before each peak night.

### BBC Licence Fee: What MPs Actually Recommended and What It Means for Your Household Bill

MPs have recommended cutting the BBC licence fee and expanding it beyond television ownership — but the £180 annual charge has not changed yet, and no new payment is due from streaming-only households right now. The distinction between what a parliamentary committee recommended and what is actually happening matters here, because the two are being routinely conflated in coverage.

Here is what was actually proposed, and what it would mean in practice.

**What the MPs recommended**

The House of Commons Culture, Media and Sport Committee published its BBC Royal Charter review report on 1 October 2026. The committee, chaired by Dame Caroline Dinenage, made several formal recommendations to government:

- Reduce the current £180 annual licence fee as part of a broader funding reform, subject to the BBC's financial sustainability
- Decouple BBC funding from television ownership and use, so that the fee is no longer triggered solely by owning a TV or watching live broadcast television
- Keep under consideration a universal household levy, with broadband-based collection as one possible mechanism among others
- Scrap the current 10-year Royal Charter renewal system in favour of a permanent statutory governance framework with periodic independent funding reviews
- The committee expressed concerns about the BBC acquiring foreign programming such as licensed animated franchises, which some members argued duplicates what commercial streaming services already provide

These are committee recommendations, not legislation. The government has not yet announced how it will respond, and the current BBC Royal Charter does not expire until the end of 2027. No change to the licence fee or eligibility rules takes effect until the government responds with a formal policy position and, if it chooses to reform funding, passes new legislation.

**What it could mean if adopted**

The headline proposal — cut the fee — contains a less-reported expansion of scope. The committee's suggested replacement mechanism would potentially apply to households that currently have no legal obligation to pay.

Under current UK law, you do not need a TV licence if you exclusively use non-BBC on-demand platforms — Netflix, YouTube, Disney+ — and never watch live broadcast television or BBC iPlayer. Millions of households, particularly younger renters who have dropped traditional TV, use this legal exemption.

If the government adopts a universal household levy or broadband surcharge, that exemption would close. A household paying for a broadband connection would contribute to BBC funding regardless of whether they watch the BBC at all. [INTERNAL LINK: do I need a TV licence 2026]

The committee received evidence that an advertising-funded model for the BBC could damage ITV, Channel 4, and the UK's regional news infrastructure; the committee did not recommend an advertising-based funding route.

**What the BBC's response signals**

The most contested territory is likely to be the commercial content rights question — the BBC has historically argued that acquiring popular content, including international programming, is part of its public service mission to build audiences for original UK content.

The next formal step is a government white paper from the Department for Culture, Media and Sport. The committee's report recommends the government set out its approach in that document, but a publication timeline has not been confirmed. [INTERNAL LINK: UK broadcasting policy 2027 charter review]

**Is the BBC licence fee being cut now?**

No. The current £180 annual fee remains in place. No new payment obligation exists for streaming-only households. The committee's report is the beginning of a consultation and legislative process, not the end of one.

The DCMS white paper is the next formal step. That is when the government's actual position will be known.

## ARTICLE 10 — Priority 10
### First-Past-The-Post vs Proportional Representation: What the UK Electoral Reform Debate Is Actually About

The UK currently uses a voting system called First-Past-The-Post for general elections. Parliamentary debate over electoral reform has intensified following the 2024 general election result — though the Representation of the People Bill currently before Parliament is not a bill to introduce proportional representation. The Guardian has published interactive modelling showing how different PR systems would have redistributed seats in recent elections. The debate generates strong claims on both sides. This article explains what the two systems actually do, what the modelling shows, and what the documented arguments for each position are — without endorsing either.

**How the current system works**

Under First-Past-The-Post, the UK is divided into 650 parliamentary constituencies. In each constituency, voters choose a single candidate. The candidate with the most votes wins that seat — they do not need a majority of votes, just more than any individual rival.

A party can win a general election and form a government with a parliamentary majority while receiving well under half the national vote. In the 2019 general election, the winning party received about 44 percent of votes cast. In 2024, the winning party received about 34 percent of votes cast but won a large parliamentary majority. The Electoral Reform Society records that in both elections, tens of millions of votes cast for losing candidates in safe constituencies had no effect on the outcome.

This disproportionality is the core objection from reform advocates. They argue that the gap between vote share and seat share means parliament does not reflect how the country actually voted. [INTERNAL LINK: how UK general elections work]

**How Proportional Representation works**

Proportional Representation is not a single system — it is a category of voting methods that share a common aim: making a party's seat count roughly match its vote share nationally or regionally.

The main variants discussed in the UK context are:

- **Party List PR**: Voters choose a party, not a candidate. Seats are allocated to parties in proportion to their vote share, using national or regional lists. Used in the Netherlands and in various forms across Europe.
- **Additional Member System (AMS/MMP)**: Voters cast two votes — one for a local constituency representative (as under FPTP) and one for a party list. The list seats are used to correct the disproportionality created by the constituency vote. Used for Scottish Parliament, Welsh Senedd, and in modified form for the Bundestag elections in Germany.
- **Single Transferable Vote (STV)**: Voters rank candidates in order of preference in multi-member constituencies. Seats are filled by eliminating candidates and redistributing preferences until all seats are filled. Used in Northern Ireland, the Republic of Ireland, and Australian Senate elections.

Each system produces different outcomes. The Guardian's interactive modelling — produced by More in Common using MRP methodology and based on the 2024 general election results — shows that under various PR scenarios, parties such as the Liberal Democrats, the Green Party, and Reform UK would have held substantially more seats. In 2024, the Green Party received about 7% of votes and won 4 seats; Reform UK received about 14% of votes and won 5 seats. The Guardian's figures are modelled seat distributions based on these inputs, not a projection of what any future election would produce.

**What supporters of FPTP argue**

Proponents of the current system, including both major party leaderships, make several documented arguments:

- FPTP typically delivers clear single-party majorities, allowing governments to implement their full manifesto without coalition negotiation
- Constituency representation — every voter having a named local MP — creates direct accountability between elected representatives and their geographic area
- Coalition governments formed under PR systems can be unstable or require post-election deals that voters did not vote for
- The Hansard record of parliamentary debates on the subject includes formal arguments that stability and decisive government are public goods that outweigh proportionality concerns

**What critics of FPTP argue**

The Electoral Reform Society and academic studies it cites make several documented counterarguments:

- Under FPTP, a significant proportion of votes cast in recent UK elections have been estimated to be "tactical votes" — voters choosing their second or third preference to block a candidate they oppose rather than voting for who they actually support
- Constituencies are classified as "safe seats" — where the incumbent party's majority is so large that the result is not in doubt — creating millions of voters whose choices have no practical effect on the outcome
- Parties campaigning primarily on environmental or other niche policies regularly receive substantial national vote shares but a fraction of parliamentary seats, suppressing their policy influence regardless of their support level
- Recent UK elections have produced governments elected on historically low vote shares, which critics argue has worsened public disengagement from voting

**What the Guardian's modelling shows and doesn't show**

The Guardian's interactive tool, produced by More in Common using MRP methodology, models how the 2024 general election results might have looked under different PR systems. The results vary considerably depending on which PR variant is applied.

This is modelling, not a prediction, and not a result. The assumptions built into the model affect every output — and different methodologies would produce different figures. Presenting modelled seat distributions as if they were the certain outcome of a PR election would misread what the tool actually does.

What it does show clearly is that the seat distributions produced by FPTP and by various PR systems can diverge substantially, and that smaller parties with distributed national support are the most affected by the choice of system. [INTERNAL LINK: Scottish Parliament voting system explained]

**What happens next in the debate**

Both major parties have historically resisted replacing FPTP with a proportional system, and no legislation to change the general election voting system has passed. The Representation of the People Bill currently before Parliament — which covers voting age, electoral registration, and voter identification — does not propose replacing First-Past-the-Post. The debate remains active because of the 2024 election result, in which a large parliamentary majority was won on a 34 percent vote share, reigniting proportionality arguments.

The Electoral Reform Society and campaign groups on both sides of the argument are active. Parliamentary debates on the subject are recorded on Hansard and publicly accessible. [INTERNAL LINK: UK constitutional reform tracker]

No vote in parliament on changing the general election system has been scheduled at the time of publication.
