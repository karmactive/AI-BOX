## Story 1 — Virginia I-81 Project Receives Two Federal Loans Totaling Up to $89.7M for Safety and Freight Upgrades

Interstate 81 carries 43% of Virginia's statewide interstate truck vehicle miles traveled across 325 rural miles of mountain grades and busy interchanges. The federal government is now sending two low-interest loans to speed up long-planned upgrades that have been under construction for several years — and the financing terms cut the project's borrowing cost in half.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announced two TIFIA loans through the Build America Bureau on October 7, 2026: one for up to $47.6 million and one for up to $42.1 million, totaling up to $89.7 million. The Virginia Commonwealth Transportation Board is the borrower. Both loans carry interest rates at half the standard TIFIA rate, made possible through the TIFIA Rural Project Initiative. The funding supports Virginia's existing $4 billion I-81 Corridor Improvement Program, which is projected to generate $5.2 billion in economic activity. The money goes toward truck climbing lanes in Washington and Augusta counties, merge lane and exit-ramp extensions in Wythe County and around Wytheville, and replacement of aging bridges at Peppers Ferry Road and the I-81 northbound span over I-77. Forty of 65 projects in the program are already underway, with completion of all components scheduled for late 2035.

Virginia freight shippers, commercial truckers, and Front Range commuters using I-81 will see targeted upgrades address some of the corridor's most congested stretches. Truck climbing lanes are intended to let slower vehicles ascend mountain grades without blocking passenger traffic. Extended ramps and merge lanes around Wytheville aim to reduce bottlenecks at the I-81 and I-77 interchange. Bridge replacements will address aging infrastructure at two locations that have carried the corridor's growing truck volumes for decades. Continued construction means some disruptions through 2035.

Build America Bureau Executive Director Morteza Farajian said the loans would help stretch taxpayer dollars further by reducing the interest costs on the financing. The $89.7 million is loan financing — not a grant — meaning the Commonwealth Transportation Board repays it over time. The half-rate interest structure makes the loans meaningfully cheaper than standard federal credit: lower debt service means more of the program's construction funding reaches the road rather than servicing the borrowing cost. The I-81 corridor moves over $300 billion in goods annually and is a primary East Coast connection for Midwest-to-Southeast freight. The ramp and merge-lane work around Wytheville is focused on the I-81 and I-77 junction, where traffic from two interstate routes converges. [Track VDOT's progress on I-81 construction projects near you.](/vdot-i81-corridor-improvement-projects-map)

**Is the Virginia I-81 federal funding a loan or a grant?**

The $89.7 million package consists of two TIFIA loans — not grants — announced by Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy on October 7, 2026. The Virginia Commonwealth Transportation Board repays the loans at half the standard TIFIA interest rate, which reduces financing costs and frees more program dollars for construction rather than debt service.

All 65 projects in the program are scheduled for completion by late 2035. VDOT's project tracker at improve81.vdot.virginia.gov lists current construction status by location. Check back for updates on bridge contract awards and additional construction phases as they are publicly announced.

## Story 2 — Cascadia High-Speed Rail Plan Due in 2028 Will Identify Route Options, Not Pick One

Washington state is planning for a high-speed rail corridor connecting Portland, Seattle, and Vancouver, B.C., but the next major milestone — a completed Service Development Plan — won't arrive until 2028. And even that document won't select a preferred route or say where stations will go.

Washington State Department of Transportation is leading the Service Development Plan, expected to be completed by the end of 2028. Planning work began in January 2025 and runs through the end of 2028. The Federal Railroad Administration awarded WSDOT $49.7 million in December 2024 for this planning phase, and the state Legislature added $5.5 million in matching funds. The current Cascadia High-Speed Rail study corridor runs between the Portland metropolitan area and Vancouver, B.C., with the project centered on connecting Portland, Seattle, and Vancouver. WSDOT envisions high-speed service at roughly 220 mph, though the agency describes the range as 160–250 mph and the operating specification has not been finalized. The Amtrak Cascades service, which currently runs the corridor, operates at a maximum of 79 mph. No construction timeline has been set. WSDOT describes phases beyond 2028 as "future phases to be determined."

Travelers, freight planners, and regional communities between Portland, Seattle, and Vancouver, B.C., are watching a multi-year planning process that could eventually transform the corridor's transportation options. The 2028 plan will identify potential route and service options — not a final design. Environmental review, alignment selection, funding, and construction would follow in separate phases. No passenger service date has been announced.

### What the 2028 Plan Will and Won't Decide

The Service Development Plan is the current scope. WSDOT has been explicit: the completed plan will identify potential route areas and conceptual service options but will not select a preferred route or station locations. Those decisions come in a later phase.

The comparison to existing service illustrates what the proposal is targeting. Amtrak Cascades currently runs at 79 mph along the same corridor. WSDOT's vision of roughly 220 mph service would mean trains covering the approximately 180-mile Portland-to-Seattle distance in under one hour — but that is a planning scenario, not a confirmed schedule, and the trip time depends on alignment, station stops, and operating specifications that have not been set. The project is a partnership among Washington state, Oregon, and British Columbia. Canadian federal and provincial authorities would need to participate in the cross-border segment. The planning work is scheduled to narrow potential route areas through 2026 and into 2027 before the full plan is published at the end of 2028. The plan will identify potential route areas and conceptual service options but will not select a preferred route or station locations. Those decisions would follow in separate planning, environmental review, and design phases. No operating date has been announced for passenger service.

## Story 3 — Alstom Launches U.S. Innovation Station in Pittsburgh, Its Fifth Worldwide

Alstom opened its first U.S. Innovation Station in Pittsburgh on October 7, 2026, establishing a collaborative hub it describes as focused on accessibility, sustainability, resilience, and passenger experience. The Pittsburgh facility is the fifth in the company's global Innovation Station network, joining existing sites in Paris, Stockholm, Singapore, and Montreal.

The station sits alongside Alstom's global development center for signaling and train control in Pittsburgh. Karina Ricks serves as its inaugural leader. Ricks most recently worked as a partner at Cityfi, an urban strategy consulting firm. Her earlier roles include Associate Administrator for Innovation, Research and Demonstration at the Federal Transit Administration, founding director of Pittsburgh's Department of Mobility and Infrastructure — where she launched MovePGH, described as the nation's first Mobility as a Service system — and director of transportation planning for Washington, D.C. Alstom Americas president Michael Keroullé framed the station's purpose as developing ideas locally and testing whether they apply more broadly across the company's operations.

Transit agencies, cities, urban planners, and mobility startups working in North America will have a new venue for collaborative work with one of the world's largest rail equipment manufacturers. The Pittsburgh hub is designed to bring together transit authorities, universities, cities, technology organizations, and startups around specific mobility challenges. Alstom has not announced specific projects, investment figures, or partner organizations for the station.

The Innovation Station model is not a corporate research laboratory. Alstom describes its approach as "local for global" — developing solutions with local partners that can then be tested for broader application across its international business. The other four stations in the network (Paris, Stockholm, Singapore, Montreal) work on regionally specific problems before those insights feed back into Alstom's wider product development.

Ricks's background directly fits the Pittsburgh context. She created Pittsburgh's Department of Mobility and Infrastructure from the ground up, overseeing the city's transition to smart signal networks, Vision Zero implementation, and autonomous vehicle policy before moving to Cityfi and, now, Alstom. The station's stated scope is collaborative mobility problem-solving across accessibility, sustainability, resilience, and passenger experience.
